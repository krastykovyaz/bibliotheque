
## I · Ivan and the Golden Horses

**NARRATOR**: The Little Humpbacked Horse. A Russian fairy tale in verse by Pyotr Yershov, first told in eighteen thirty-four. Make yourself comfortable, and listen. It is a story about Ivan the fool, who is not a fool at all, a tiny magic horse with two humps and ears a yard long, a bird made of fire, a Tsar-Maiden from the sea, a whale with a village on his back, and three great cauldrons.

**NARRATOR**: Here the tale begins to be told.

**NARRATOR**: Far beyond the hills and woods, Far beyond the wide blue seas, Underneath the sky, on earth, Lived an old man in a village. He had three sons, the old man did. The eldest son was clever, they said, The middle son was so-so, The youngest was a fool, quite plain. The brothers sowed their wheat each spring And took it to the town to sell; The capital was not too far From their little village. There they sold their golden grain, Counted the coins, and, with a bag Full of money, they went home.

**NARRATOR**: Soon, or maybe not so soon, Trouble came to them one day: Somebody walked about the field And trampled down their ripe wheat. The peasants had never in their lives Seen such a sorrow as this. They thought and thought, and wondered how To catch the thief who did the harm. At last they said: “Let us keep watch, Let us guard the wheat by night, And lie in wait for the wicked thief.” So when the evening grew dark, The eldest brother got ready: He took a pitchfork and an axe And went off to the field to watch. But the night was wet and stormy, And fear came over him, And in his fright our brave watchman Dug himself into a haystack. The night went by, the day came on; Down from the hay the watchman climbed, Poured water over his own head, And knocked at the cottage door:

**DANILO**: “Hey, you sleepy-heads, wake up! Open the door for your brother! I am soaked from the rain, From my head right down to my toes!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers opened up the door And let the watchman in, And asked him what he had seen, Whether he had seen anything at all. The watchman said a little prayer, Bowed to the right and to the left, Coughed, and told them in a grand voice:

**DANILO**: “All night long I did not sleep, And, to my great bad luck, The storm was terrible. The rain came down in buckets And wet my little shirt right through. It was dreadfully dull out there! But all is well, all is well.”

**NARRATOR**: The father praised him warmly:

**FATHER**: “Danilo, you are a fine lad! You have done me, so to say, A true and faithful service; You did not, as we say, Let the family down.”

**NARRATOR**: Again the evening grew dark, And the middle brother got ready. He took a pitchfork and an axe And went off to the field to watch. The night was cold and frosty; A shiver fell on the poor lad, His teeth began to dance and chatter; He took to his heels and ran, And all night long he kept his watch Under the neighbour's garden fence. He was very frightened, the poor fellow! But when morning came, he ran to the porch:

**GAVRILO**: “Hey, you sleepy-heads! Why are you sleeping? Open the door for your brother! The night was terribly cold, I have frozen to the bone!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers opened up the door And let the watchman in, And asked him what he had seen, Whether he had seen anything at all. The watchman said a little prayer, Bowed to the right and to the left, And through his teeth he answered:

**GAVRILO**: “All night long I did not sleep, But, to my unlucky fate, The cold was dreadful in the night; It chilled me to the heart. I galloped all the night about, It was all very hard work. But all is well, all is well.”

**NARRATOR**: And their father said to him:

**FATHER**: “Gavrilo, you are a fine lad!”

**NARRATOR**: When the third evening came on, It was the youngest's turn to go. But he did not care at all; In the corner, on the stove, He sang with all his silly might:

**IVAN**: “Oh, you lovely, lovely eyes!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers scolded him and scolded, They tried to push him to the field, But however long they shouted They only lost their voices: He did not budge. At last the father Came up to him and said:

**FATHER**: “Listen, Ivan, my dear boy, Go and keep the watch tonight; I will buy you toffee sticks, And give you peas and beans.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan climbed down from the stove, Put on his big warm cap, Tucked a crust of bread inside his shirt, And went off to watch the field.

**NARRATOR**: The night came; the moon rose up; Ivan walked all round the field, Looking carefully about, And sat himself under a bush; He counted the stars in the sky And munched his crust of bread. Suddenly, at midnight, a horse neighed... Our watchman got to his feet, Peeped out from under his mitten, And saw a mare in the field. That mare was all as white As the winter snow, and her mane, Of gold, fell down to the ground In little curling rings.

**IVAN**: “Oho! So this is the little thief Who tramples all our wheat! Just wait: I do not joke around, I will sit on your neck at once. Just look at that wild grasshopper!”

**NARRATOR**: And, seizing the moment, He ran up to the mare, Caught her by her wavy tail, And leaped on to her back, But, by mistake, he sat with his face to the tail. The young mare, with eyes flashing, Twisted her head like a snake And dashed away like an arrow. She circled over the fields, Hung flat across the ditches, Raced in leaps across the hills, Rose on her hind legs in the woods; She tried by strength or by trickery To get the better of Ivan; But Ivan was no fool himself, And held on tightly to her tail.

**NARRATOR**: At last the mare grew tired.

**MARE**: “Well, Ivan,” she said to him, “Since you were able to stay on, You shall be my master. Give me a place to rest, And look after me as you can. But mind: for three mornings Let me out to run In the wide and open field. And when three days are over, I will give you two horses Such as have never been seen, Not even in a dream; And I will give you a little horse, Only five inches tall, With two humps upon his back, And with ears a yard long. The two horses, if you like, sell; But the little horse, do not give him up, Not for a belt, not for a hat, Not for a black old witch. On earth and under the earth He will be your faithful friend: In winter he will warm you, In summer he will cool you, When you are hungry he will bring you bread, When you are thirsty he will bring you mead. And I will go back to the field, To try my strength in freedom.”

**NARRATOR**: “All right,” thought Ivan, And he drove the mare into The shepherd's little hut, Closed the door with a mat, And as soon as it was light, He went off home to the village, Singing his favourite song out loud, “A fine young fellow went to Presnya.”

**NARRATOR**: Then he climbed up on the porch And seized the ring of the door, And knocked as hard as he could, So that the roof almost fell in, And shouted to the whole market As if a fire had broken out. The brothers jumped up from the benches And cried out, stammering:

**DANILO**: “Who is knocking so hard?”

**IVAN**: “It is I, Ivan the fool!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers opened up the door, Let the fool into the cottage, And began to scold him For giving them such a fright. But Ivan, not taking off His bast shoes or his cap, Climbed up on to the stove And began to tell his story About his adventure in the night, To everybody's great surprise:

**IVAN**: “All night long I did not sleep, I counted all the stars in the sky; The moon was shining too, I think, I did not notice, to be honest. Suddenly the devil himself came, With a beard and with a moustache; His face was like a cat's, And his eyes were big as saucers! Then that devil began to jump And knock the wheat down with his tail. I do not joke around, you know, So I jumped on to his neck. He dragged me, and he dragged me, He almost broke my head. But I was not a fool either, I held him as in a vice. He struggled, and he struggled, the rascal, And begged me in the end: ‘Do not destroy me, please! For a whole year, for this, I promise to live quietly, And not to trouble good people.’ I did not weigh his words, And I believed the little devil.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the story-teller stopped, Gave a yawn, and fell asleep. The brothers, as cross as they were, Could not help it: they burst out laughing, Holding their sides, At the fool's story. Even the old father could not hold it in, He laughed till the tears came, Although it is a sin, they say, For old men to laugh like that.

**NARRATOR**: A long time, or a little time, Went by after that night; I have not heard a thing about it From anyone at all. Well, what does it matter to us, A year, or maybe two, went by, For we need not run after them... Let us go on with our tale. Well now, then! One day Danilo (It was a holiday, I think), Rather the worse for drink, Wandered to the shepherd's hut. And what does he see there? Two fine horses with golden manes, And a toy of a little horse, Only five inches tall, With two humps upon his back And ears a yard long.

**DANILO**: “Hm! Now I know Why the fool slept out here!”

**NARRATOR**: Danilo said to himself... The wonder made him sober at once; So Danilo ran into the house And said to Gavrilo:

**DANILO**: “Come and look what beautiful Golden-maned horses Our fool has got himself, You have never heard the like!”

**NARRATOR**: And Danilo and Gavrilo, As fast as their legs would go, Through the nettles, straight across, Ran there in their bare feet. They stumbled three times on the way, Rubbed their eyes, and rubbed their bruises, And came in to the two horses. The horses neighed and snorted, Their eyes shone like rubies; In little curling rings, Their golden tails streamed down, And their diamond hooves Were studded with big pearls. It was a joy to look at them! Fit for a Tsar to ride. The brothers stared at them so hard That they almost went blind.

**DANILO**: “Where did he get them?”

**NARRATOR**: The eldest said to the middle one.

**DANILO**: “It has long been said That only fools find treasure. You may knock your head against the wall, You will not earn two roubles. Well, Gavrilo, this very week Let us take them to the capital; There we will sell them to the nobles, And share the money equally. And with money, you know yourself, You can drink and make merry, You just have to slap your purse. And the good fool will not be clever Enough to guess where his horses are: Let him look for them here and there. Well, friend, it is a bargain!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers agreed at once, Embraced, and crossed themselves, And went home again, Talking all the way About the horses, and the feast, And the wonderful little creature.

**NARRATOR**: Time rolled on in its own way, Hour by hour, day by day, And the first week went by, And the brothers went to the capital, To sell their goods, And to find out at the harbour Whether ships had come to town With foreign merchants buying linen, And whether Tsar Saltan was marching To make the Christians heathens. They prayed before the holy icons, Got their father's blessing, Took the two horses secretly, And quietly went on their way.

**NARRATOR**: Evening was drawing on to night; Ivan got ready for his sleep; He walked along the village street, Eating his crust and singing. Then he came to the field, Put his hands upon his hips, And with a little skip, like a gentleman, Went sideways into the hut. Everything was as it was before, But the horses were not there; Only the little humpbacked toy Was spinning round his feet, Flapping his ears for gladness And dancing on his hooves. Then Ivan began to howl, Leaning on the hut:

**IVAN**: “Oh, my horses, brown and grey, Fine horses with golden manes! Did I not pet you, my friends? What devil has stolen you? May he be lost, the dog! May he die in a ditch! May he, in the other world, Fall through a bridge! Oh, my horses, brown and grey, Fine horses with golden manes!”

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse neighed to him.

**KONEK**: “Do not grieve, Ivan,” he said. “The trouble is big, I do not argue; But I can help you in your grief. Do not blame the devil for it: It was your brothers who took the horses. Well, what is the use of idle talk? Be easy, dear Ivan. Get up on me quickly, And only hold on tight; I am not very tall, But I can outrun any horse: When I start and run, I will catch the very devil.”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse lay down before him; Ivan got on the little horse, Took his ears for handles, And shouted with all his might. The Little Humpbacked Horse shook himself, Stood up on his little legs, Tossed his little mane and snorted, And flew off like an arrow; Only clouds of dust went whirling Under his feet like a storm. And in two moments, if not one, Our Ivan caught up with the thieves.

**NARRATOR**: The brothers, of course, were frightened, They scratched themselves and hesitated, And Ivan began to shout at them:

**IVAN**: “Shame on you, brothers, to steal! You are cleverer than Ivan, But Ivan is more honest than you: He never stole your horses.”

**NARRATOR**: The eldest, wriggling, said:

**DANILO**: “Dear brother of ours, Ivan! Never mind our little quarrel! But please take into account Our poor empty stomachs. However much wheat we sow, We have hardly our daily bread. And if the harvest fails, We might as well hang ourselves! In such a great trouble, Gavrilo and I talked All through the other night About how to help our sorrow. We thought it over this way and that, And at last we decided To sell your little horses For a thousand roubles. And, as a thank-you, if I may say, To bring you a new present: A red cap with a bell on it And little boots with heels. And besides, the old man is sick And cannot work any more, And we must all somehow live, You are a clever man yourself!”

**IVAN**: “Well, if that is so, then go,”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan, “and sell The two golden-maned horses. But take me with you, too.”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers looked rather sour, But they could not help it, and agreed.

**NARRATOR**: The sky began to darken, The air began to turn cold. So that they should not lose their way, They decided to stop for the night. Under the roof of the branches They tied all the horses up, Brought out a little basket of food, Had a little drink to warm themselves, And lay down as God might send, Each one as he could.

**NARRATOR**: Then Danilo suddenly noticed That a light shone in the distance. He looked at Gavrilo, Winked with his left eye, And coughed a little cough, And quietly pointed out the fire; Then he scratched the back of his head:

**DANILO**: “Oh, how dark it is!” he said. “If only the moon would peep At us for a minute, just for fun, It would be easier. But now, We are worse than blind grouse... But wait a moment... It seems to me That a bright smoke is curling there. Look, over there!... Yes, it is!... If only we could light a pipe! What a wonder that would be!... Listen, Run over there, brother Ivan. To tell the truth, I have Neither flint nor tinder.”

**NARRATOR**: But Danilo thinks to himself: “I hope something will crush you there!” And Gavrilo says:

**GAVRILO**: “Who knows what is burning! If robbers have camped there, We shall never see him again!”

**NARRATOR**: Nothing frightens a fool; He sits on the little horse, Kicks his sides with his feet, Pulls him with his hands, And shouts with all his strength... The horse flew up, and was gone.

**GAVRILO**: “May the holy cross be with us!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried Gavrilo then, Making the sign of the cross.

**GAVRILO**: “What kind of devil is under him!”

**NARRATOR**: The light burns brighter and brighter, The Humpbacked Horse runs faster. Now he is before the fire. The field is as bright as day; A wonderful light streams round, But it does not warm and does not smoke. Ivan was amazed.

**IVAN**: “What kind of sprite is this?” he said. “There is light enough for five caps, But no warmth and no smoke; What a wonderful little fire!”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse said to him:

**KONEK**: “There is something to wonder at! Here lies a feather of the Firebird. But for your own good, Do not take it for yourself. It will bring you a great deal, A great deal of trouble.”

**IVAN**: “Talk away! Not likely!”

**NARRATOR**: The fool grumbled to himself; And, picking up the Firebird's feather, He wrapped it in rags, Put the rags in his cap, And turned the little horse around. So he came back to his brothers, And, when they asked him, he answered:

**IVAN**: “When I got there, I saw a burnt old stump; I puffed and puffed at it, Till I almost burst myself; I blew on it for an hour, But, the devil take it, it went out!”

**NARRATOR**: The brothers did not sleep all night, They laughed at Ivan; And Ivan sat under the cart And snored until the morning.

**NARRATOR**: Then they harnessed the horses And drove into the capital, And took their places in the horse-market Opposite the great palace.

**NARRATOR**: In that capital there was a custom: Unless the city warden said so, No one could buy anything, No one could sell anything. Then the church bell rang for mass; The city warden rode out In slippers and a fur hat, With a hundred of the city guard. Beside him rode a herald, With a long moustache and beard; He blew on a golden trumpet, And cried in a loud voice:

**HERALD**: “Merchants! Open your shops, Buy and sell; And the watchmen shall sit Beside the shops and look That there be no disorder, No crushing and no riot, And that no rogue Cheat the people!”

**NARRATOR**: The merchants opened their shops And called the people:

**MERCHANT**: “Hey, honest gentlemen, Come over here to us! Here we have goods and gossip, Goods of every kind!”

**NARRATOR**: The buyers came, And took the goods from the merchants; The merchants counted their little coins And winked at the watchmen.

**NARRATOR**: Meanwhile the city guard Rode to the horse-market; They saw the crowd of people, No way in and no way out; They swarmed like bees, Laughing and shouting. The city warden was surprised That the people were so merry, And ordered his men To clear the road.

**GUARD**: “Hey, you barefoot rascals! Out of the way! Out of the way!”

**NARRATOR**: The moustached guards shouted, And cracked their whips. Then the people stirred And took off their hats and made way.

**NARRATOR**: Before their eyes was the horse-row; Two horses stood in the row, Young and black, Their golden manes curling in little rings, Their golden tails streaming... Our old warden, hot-tempered as he was, Scratched the back of his head for a long time.

**WARDEN**: “God's world is wonderful,” he said, “What wonders are not found in it!”

**NARRATOR**: All the guard bowed, Admiring his wise words. The warden meanwhile Gave strict orders to everyone Not to buy the horses, Not to gape or shout; Then he said he was riding to the palace To tell the Tsar of everything. And leaving part of the guard, He rode off to make his report.

**NARRATOR**: He came to the palace.

**WARDEN**: “Have mercy, Father Tsar,”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the city warden, And fell down flat before him,

**WARDEN**: “Do not order me to be punished, Let me speak!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar said: “All right, Speak, but speak plainly.”

**WARDEN**: “As well as I can, I will tell: I serve as city warden, And I do my duty Faithfully and truly...”

**TSAR**: “I know, I know!”

**WARDEN**: “Well, today, taking my guard, I rode to the horse-market. I arrive, and there is a crowd! No way in and no way out. What was I to do? I ordered The people to be driven off. So it was done, our dear Tsar! And I rode on, and what did I see? Before me was the horse-row; Two horses stood in the row, Young and black, Their golden manes curling in little rings, Their golden tails streaming, And their diamond hooves Studded with big pearls.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar could not sit still.

**TSAR**: “I must see these horses,”

**NARRATOR**: He said. “And it would be good To have such a wonder myself. Hey, bring my carriage!”

**NARRATOR**: And there the carriage was at the gate. The Tsar washed, and dressed, And rolled off to the market; Behind him rode a troop of archers.

**NARRATOR**: He drove into the horse-row. They all fell on their knees And cried “Hurrah!” to the Tsar. The Tsar bowed to them, and in a moment Jumped lightly from his carriage... He could not take his eyes off the horses, He walked round them to the right and to the left, Called them with kind words, Gently patted their backs, Stroked their strong necks, Smoothed their golden manes, And, when he had looked enough, He turned and asked The people round him: “Hey, lads! Whose are these young horses? Who is their owner?” Then Ivan, With his hands upon his hips, like a gentleman, Stepped out from behind his brothers, And, puffing himself up, answered:

**IVAN**: “This pair, Tsar, is mine, And the owner is I as well.”

**TSAR**: “Well, I will buy the pair! Will you sell?”

**IVAN**: “No, I will exchange.”

**TSAR**: “What do you want in exchange?”

**IVAN**: “Two-five caps of silver.”

**TSAR**: “That is, ten caps.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar ordered it weighed at once, And, out of his kindness, Gave five roubles extra. The Tsar was a generous man!

**NARRATOR**: Ten grey-haired grooms led the horses To the stables, All with golden stripes, All with bright sashes, And with leather whips. But on the road, as if for fun, The horses knocked them all down, Broke all the bridles, And ran back to Ivan.

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar went back And said to Ivan: “Well, brother, My men cannot handle the pair: There is nothing to be done, you must Come and serve in my palace. You will go about in gold, You will dress in red clothes, You will roll like cheese in butter, And I will give you charge Of all my royal stables. The Tsar's word is your guarantee. Well, do you agree?”

**IVAN**: “What a thing! I shall live in the palace, I shall go about in gold, I shall dress in red clothes, I shall roll like cheese in butter, And the Tsar gives me charge Of the whole stable-yard; That is, from a kitchen garden I shall become the Tsar's own general. It is a wonderful thing! So be it, Tsar, I will serve you. Only, mind, no fighting me, And let me have my sleep, Or else I shall be gone!”

**NARRATOR**: Then he called the horses And went along the capital's street, Waving his mitten. And to the fool's song The horses danced a merry dance; And his little humpback horse Went down in a squat dance, To the amazement of all the people.

**NARRATOR**: The two brothers meanwhile Got the Tsar's money, Sewed it up in their belts, Clinked their cups together And went off home. At home they shared it fairly, Both married at the same time, And lived on, and lived on, Remembering Ivan now and then.

**NARRATOR**: But now we shall leave them, And amuse the good Christian people With our tale once more, Of what our Ivan did When he was in the Tsar's service, At the Tsar's royal stable; How he fell in with a neighbour, How he slept over his feather, How cleverly he caught the Firebird, How he carried off the Tsar-Maiden, How he went to fetch the ring, How he was an envoy in the sky, How in the Sun's own village He begged the Whale's forgiveness; How, besides all the other things, He saved thirty ships; How he was not boiled in the cauldrons, And how he became a handsome prince. In a word: our story is about How he became a Tsar.


## II · The Firebird and the Tsar-Maiden

**NARRATOR**: The tale is told quickly, But the deed is not done quickly.

**NARRATOR**: Now the story begins With Ivan's tricks and pranks, With his grey horse and his brown, And his wise little dun. The goats have gone out to sea; The hills are overgrown with woods; The horse tore loose from his golden bridle And rose straight up to the sun; The forest stands beneath his feet, A thunder-cloud is at his side; The cloud goes by and flashes, Scattering thunder through the sky. This is only the beginning: wait, The tale itself will come. On the ocean sea, On the island of Buyan, A new coffin stands in the wood, And in the coffin a maiden lies; A nightingale sings over it; A black beast prowls the oak-grove. That is only the beginning; now The tale will go in order.

**NARRATOR**: Well, you see, good people, Good Christian folk, Our brave young fellow Had found his way into the palace. He serves in the Tsar's stables, And never grieves a bit For his brothers or his father In the Tsar's royal palace. And why should he? Ivan has Red clothes, red caps, and boots, Nearly ten boxes full of them; He eats sweetly, and he sleeps So much that it is a joy to see.

**NARRATOR**: About five weeks went by, And the Chamberlain began to notice... You must know that this Chamberlain, Before Ivan came, had been the master Of all the stables; He was a nobleman's son. So it is no wonder he was angry With Ivan, and swore That, come what may, he would drive The newcomer out of the palace. But, hiding his cunning, For every case, the rogue Pretended to be deaf, Short-sighted, and dumb; And he thought: “Just wait, I will push you down, you unwashed boy!” So, after five weeks, The Chamberlain began to notice That Ivan did not groom the horses, Did not clean them, did not train them; Yet the two horses were As if they had just been combed: Washed all clean and bright, Their manes braided in plaits, Their forelocks tied in tufts, Their coats as glossy as silk; In the stalls was fresh wheat, As if it had grown right there, And in the big tubs the honey drink Was there, as if just poured in.

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “What kind of riddle is this?”

**NARRATOR**: Sighed the Chamberlain, thinking.

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Is it not the house-spirit Who comes to us, the little rascal? I will lie in wait for him, And, if need be, I can cast a bullet Without blinking, if only I can Get rid of the fool. I will tell the Tsar's council That the Tsar's groom Is a heathen, a sorcerer, A black-magic man and a villain; That he eats bread and salt with the devil, Does not go to God's church, Keeps a foreign cross, And eats meat in Lent.”

**NARRATOR**: That very evening the Chamberlain, The old chief of the stables, Hid secretly in the stalls And covered himself with oats.

**NARRATOR**: Midnight came. His heart began to ache; He lay there more dead than alive, Saying his prayers. He waited for the house-spirit... Hark! indeed, The doors creaked softly. The horses stamped, and in came The old groom, Ivan. He locked the door with the bolt, Took off his cap carefully, Put it on the window sill, And from the cap he took, Wrapped in three layers of rags, The Tsar's treasure, the Firebird's feather. Such a light shone out That the Chamberlain almost cried aloud, And trembled so with fear That the oats fell off him. But the house-spirit had no idea! He put the feather in a bin, Began to groom the horses, Washed them, and tidied them, Braided their long manes, And sang all kinds of little songs. Meanwhile, curled up in a ball, With his teeth chattering, The Chamberlain, half-dead, watched What the house-spirit was doing. What kind of devil is this? Did he Dress up on purpose, the midnight rascal? No horns, and no beard, A fine, strong lad, fit for anything! Smooth hair, ribbons at the side, Braid on his shirt, Boots as red as scarlet leather, Why, it is Ivan himself. What a wonder! Again our gaper Looked at the house-spirit.

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Oh! So that is it!”

**NARRATOR**: The cunning one grumbled at last.

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Good, tomorrow the Tsar shall know What your silly mind is hiding. Just wait until daylight, You will remember me!”

**NARRATOR**: And Ivan, not knowing at all What trouble was threatening him, Went on braiding the manes and singing; And when he had done, into both tubs He poured honey drink And filled them to the brim With white-grain millet. Then, yawning, he wrapped the Firebird's feather Again in the rags, Put the cap under his ear, and lay down By the horses' hind legs.

**NARRATOR**: As soon as dawn began to glow, The Chamberlain began to stir, And, hearing that Ivan Was snoring like a hero of old, He climbed down quietly And crept up to Ivan, Put his fingers in the cap, Snatched the feather, and was gone.

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar had hardly woken up When our Chamberlain appeared before him, Struck the floor hard with his forehead, And began to sing to the Tsar:

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “With a guilty head I come before you, Tsar; Do not order me to be punished, Let me speak.”

**TSAR**: “Speak, and add nothing,”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar said to him, yawning,

**TSAR**: “And if you tell me lies, You will not escape the whip.”

**NARRATOR**: Our Chamberlain, gathering his strength, Said to the Tsar:

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Have mercy! As Christ is true, My report is just, Tsar. Our Ivan, everybody knows, Is hiding from you, Father, Not gold, and not silver, But a Firebird's feather...”

**TSAR**: “A Firebird's feather?... The wretch! And he dared, so rich... Just wait, you villain! You will not escape the lash!...”

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “And that is not all he knows,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the Chamberlain softly, bending low.

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Well and good! Let him keep the feather; But the Firebird herself, To your own bright chamber, Father, If you give the order, He boasts that he can bring.”

**NARRATOR**: And with these words the informer, Bent like a willow hoop, Went up to the bed, Gave the treasure to the Tsar, and fell to the floor again.

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar looked and wondered, Stroked his beard, and laughed, And bit off the tip of the feather. Then, putting it in a casket, He cried out, in his impatience, Striking the air with his fist:

**TSAR**: “Hey! Call the fool to me!”

**NARRATOR**: And the messenger nobles Ran to fetch Ivan, But, bumping together in a corner, They fell sprawling on the floor. The Tsar was delighted And laughed till his sides ached. And the nobles, seeing That it amused the Tsar, Winked at one another And tumbled again. The Tsar was so pleased with this That he gave them a cap as a prize. Then the messenger nobles Ran again to call Ivan, And this time They did it without any tricks.

**NARRATOR**: They ran up to the stable, Opened the doors wide, And began to push the fool With their feet on all sides. For half an hour they struggled with him, But could not wake him; At last one of the soldiers Woke him with a broom.

**IVAN**: “What kind of rabble is this?”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan, getting up.

**IVAN**: “If I give you a crack with my whip, You will not wake Ivan up For nothing, any more!”

**NARRATOR**: The nobles said to him:

**NOBLE**: “The Tsar has ordered us To call you to him.”

**IVAN**: “The Tsar?... Well, all right! I will dress And come to him at once.”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the messengers. Then he put on his coat, Tied his belt around him, Washed his face, and combed his hair, Hung his whip at his side, And sailed off like a little duck.

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan came before the Tsar, Bowed, and, plucking up his courage, Coughed twice and asked:

**IVAN**: “Why did you wake me?”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar, squinting with his left eye, Rose and cried to him in anger:

**TSAR**: “Silence! You must answer me: By whose order did you hide From our eyes Our royal treasure, the Firebird's feather? Am I a Tsar, or just a nobleman? Answer at once, you Tartar!”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan, waving his hand, Said to the Tsar:

**IVAN**: “Wait a bit! I did not give you my cap, did I? How did you find that out? Are you a prophet or what? Well, put me in prison, Have me beaten with sticks, There is no feather, and no rubbish!...”

**TSAR**: “Answer! I will have you flogged!...”

**IVAN**: “I tell you plainly: There is no feather! And where could I get such a wonder?”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar jumped up from his bed And opened the casket with the feather.

**TSAR**: “What? You dared to argue? No, you cannot wriggle out of this! What is this? Eh?”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan Trembled like a leaf in a blizzard, And dropped his cap in fright.

**TSAR**: “Well, friend, I see it is hard for you? Wait a moment, brother!...”

**IVAN**: “Oh, have mercy, I am guilty! Forgive Ivan, I will not tell lies again.”

**NARRATOR**: And, wrapping himself in his coat-tails, He lay down on the floor.

**TSAR**: “Well, for the first time I forgive your fault,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the Tsar to Ivan.

**TSAR**: “I am, God save us, quick to anger! And in my anger I sometimes Take off a man's hair and his head. So you see what I am like! But, to say it without more words, I have heard that you boast You can bring the Firebird To our royal chamber, If you take it into your head. Well, do not deny it, And try to get her.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan jumped up like a top.

**IVAN**: “I never said that,”

**NARRATOR**: He cried, wiping his face.

**IVAN**: “I do not deny the feather, But about the bird, as you please, You are accusing me falsely.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar, shaking his beard:

**TSAR**: “What? Am I to bargain with you?”

**NARRATOR**: He cried out.

**TSAR**: “But look out! If in three weeks You do not bring me the Firebird To our royal chamber, Then, I swear by my beard, You will pay me for it: To the whipping, to the grate, to the stake! Out, you slave!”

**NARRATOR**: Ivan wept, And went to the hayloft, Where his little horse was lying.

**NARRATOR**: The Humpbacked Horse, sensing him, Began to dance a merry dance; But when he saw the tears, He nearly wept himself.

**KONEK**: “What is the matter, Ivan dear? Why are you sad? Why hang your head?”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse, Turning about at his feet.

**KONEK**: “Do not hide it from me, Tell me everything that is in your soul; I am ready to help you. Are you ill, my dear? Or have you fallen into an enemy's hands?”

**NARRATOR**: Ivan fell on the little horse's neck, Hugged him and kissed him.

**IVAN**: “Oh, it is trouble, little horse!” he said. “The Tsar orders me to get the Firebird For his royal chamber. What shall I do, Humpbacked Horse?”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse said to him:

**KONEK**: “The trouble is big, I do not argue; But I can help you in your grief. Your trouble comes from this: That you did not listen to me. Do you remember, going to the capital, You found the Firebird's feather? I said to you then: Do not take it, Ivan, it is trouble! It will bring you a great deal, A great deal of unrest. Now you know Whether I told you the truth. But, as a friend, I will say: This is a small service, not the real one; The real service is still to come. Go to the Tsar now, And tell him plainly: ‘Tsar, I need two troughs Of white-grain millet And of foreign wine. And have it done quickly: Tomorrow, as soon as dawn comes, We shall set out on the journey.’”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan goes to the Tsar, And tells him plainly:

**IVAN**: “Tsar, I need two troughs Of white-grain millet And of foreign wine. And have it done quickly: Tomorrow, as soon as dawn comes, We shall set out on the journey.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar gave the order at once That the messenger nobles Should find everything for Ivan; He called him a fine fellow And said: “A happy journey!”

**NARRATOR**: Next day, early in the morning, The little horse woke Ivan:

**KONEK**: “Hey, master! Enough of sleeping! It is time to do the work!”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan got up And got ready for the road. He took the troughs, and the millet, And the foreign wine; He dressed himself warmly, Sat upon his little horse, Took out a little piece of bread, And rode off to the east To get that Firebird.

**NARRATOR**: They rode for a whole week, And at last, on the eighth day, They came to a thick forest. Then the little horse said to Ivan:

**KONEK**: “You will see a clearing here; In that clearing there is a mountain All of pure silver; Here, before the lightning of the dawn, The Firebirds fly in To drink water from the stream; Here we will catch them.”

**NARRATOR**: And, having ended his speech to Ivan, He ran out into the clearing. What a field! The grass there Was like the emerald stone, A breeze blew over it, Scattering sparks; And among the grass were flowers Of indescribable beauty. And in that clearing, Like a wave on the ocean, There rose a mountain All of pure silver. The sun with its summer rays Painted it with dawns, Ran in gold along its folds, And burned like a candle on its top.

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse climbed Up the slope of that mountain, Ran a mile or two, Stood still, and said:

**KONEK**: “Soon, Ivan, the night will come, And you will have to keep watch. Well, pour the wine into the trough And mix the millet with the wine. And so that you are hidden, Creep under that trough, Watch quietly, And be sure you do not yawn. Before the lightning of the dawn, The Firebirds will fly here And begin to peck the millet And to cry in their own way. Whichever one is nearest to you, Catch her, mind you! And when you have caught the fire-bird, Shout to the whole market; I will come to you at once.”

**IVAN**: “Well, and if I burn myself?”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the little horse, Spreading out his coat.

**IVAN**: “I shall have to take my mittens: I think the little rogue burns badly.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse vanished from sight, And Ivan, groaning, crept Under the oak trough And lay there like one dead.

**NARRATOR**: At the midnight hour A light spread over the mountain, As if noon were coming: The Firebirds flew in; They began to run and cry, And to peck the millet and wine. Our Ivan, hidden from them, Watched the birds from under the trough, And talked to himself, Waving his hand like this:

**IVAN**: “Pooh, what a devilish power! What a crowd of them has come! There must be fifty of them here. If only we could catch them all, What a profit that would be! No denying, they are terribly beautiful! Red legs, every one of them, And their tails are such a joke! I bet hens have no tails like these. And what a lot of light, Like father's stove!”

**NARRATOR**: And, having made this speech To himself, under the trough, Our Ivan, like a snake or a grass-snake, Crept up to the millet and the wine, And caught one bird by the tail.

**IVAN**: “Oh, little Humpbacked Horse! Run here quickly, dear friend! I have caught the bird!”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan the fool cried. The Humpbacked Horse came at once.

**KONEK**: “Ah, master, you have done well!”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse to him.

**KONEK**: “Now quickly, into the sack with her! And tie it up tightly; And hang the sack on your neck. We must go back.”

**IVAN**: “No, let me scare the birds first!”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan.

**IVAN**: “Look, they have shouted themselves hoarse!”

**NARRATOR**: And, seizing his sack, He swung it this way and that. Flashing with bright flame, The whole flock fluttered up, Wheeled round in a circle of fire, And rushed away above the clouds. And our Ivan, after them, With his mittens Waved and shouted, As if he had been scalded. The birds were lost in the clouds; Our travellers met again, Packed up the Tsar's treasure, And went back.

**NARRATOR**: So they came to the capital.

**TSAR**: “Well, have you got the Firebird?”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar said to Ivan, Looking at the Chamberlain. And the Chamberlain, perhaps from boredom, Had bitten all his hands.

**IVAN**: “Of course I have,”

**NARRATOR**: Our Ivan told the Tsar.

**TSAR**: “Where is she?”

**IVAN**: “Wait a little. First order the window In the bedchamber to be shut, To make it dark.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the nobles ran And shut the window. Ivan put the sack on the table:

**IVAN**: “Come on, Granny, out you go!”

**NARRATOR**: Such a light then spread That all the people covered their eyes. The Tsar cried out to the whole market:

**TSAR**: “Oh, dear fathers, a fire! Hey, call the firemen! Put it out! Put it out!”

**HUNTER**: “It is not a fire, you know, It is the light of the Firebird,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the huntsman, himself bursting With laughter.

**HUNTER**: “I have brought you a treat, my lord!”

**NARRATOR**: And the Tsar said to Ivan:

**TSAR**: “Now I love my friend Ivan! You have made my soul happy, And for such joy, Be my Tsar's own stirrup-man!”

**NARRATOR**: Seeing this, the cunning Chamberlain, The former chief of the stables, Said to himself, under his nose:

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “No, wait, you milksop! You will not always have the luck To distinguish yourself like that, the rascal. I will bring you to grief again, My little friend!”

**NARRATOR**: Three weeks after that, One evening, there sat In the Tsar's kitchen the cooks And the servants of the court, Sipping honey drink from a jug And reading about Eruslan.

**SERVANT**: “Oh!” said one servant, “What a wonderful little book I got today from my neighbour! It has not so very many pages, And only five tales, But the tales — I tell you, You will wonder how it was thought up!”

**NARRATOR**: Then they all cried at once:

**COOKS**: “Do us a favour! Tell us, brother, tell us!”

**SERVANT**: “Well, which do you want? There are five tales; look: The first is about a beaver, And the second about a Tsar; The third... God help my memory... yes! About a lady of the East; In the fourth: Prince Bobyl; In the fifth... the fifth... oh, I forget! In the fifth tale it is said... It keeps turning in my head...”

**COOKS**: “Well, forget it!”

**SERVANT**: “Wait!...”

**COOKS**: “About some beauty?”

**SERVANT**: “Exactly! In the fifth It is about the lovely Tsar-Maiden. Well, which one, friends, Shall I tell today?”

**COOKS**: “The Tsar-Maiden!”

**NARRATOR**: They all cried.

**COOKS**: “We have heard about tsars already, Give us the beauties quickly! They are more fun to hear.”

**NARRATOR**: And the servant, sitting down in state, Began to tell his tale in a slow, long voice:

**SERVANT**: “In far-off German lands, My lads, there is an ocean. On that ocean sail Only heathen folk; From our own Christian land No nobleman and no villager Has ever sailed That heathen ocean. But travellers say That a maiden lives there; And no common maiden, For she is the Moon's own daughter, And the Sun is her brother. That maiden, they say, Goes about in a red sheepskin coat, In a golden boat, my lads, And steers it herself With a silver oar; She sings all kinds of songs And plays upon the little harp...”

**NARRATOR**: Then the Chamberlain jumped down from the bunk And ran on both his feet To the palace, to the Tsar, And came straight before him, Struck the floor with his forehead, And sang to the Tsar:

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “With a guilty head I come before you, Tsar, Do not order me to be punished, Let me speak!”

**TSAR**: “Speak, but only the truth, And do not tell a single lie!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the Tsar from his bed. The cunning Chamberlain answered:

**CHAMBERLAIN**: “Today we were in the kitchen, Drinking to your health, And one of the court servants Amused us by telling a tale aloud; In that tale there is a lovely Tsar-Maiden. And your royal groom Ivan Swore by your beard That he knows this bird, For so he called the Tsar-Maiden, And that he boasts He can get her for you.”

**NARRATOR**: The Chamberlain struck the floor again.

**TSAR**: “Hey! Call my groom to me!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar cried to the messengers. The Chamberlain hid behind the stove; And the messenger nobles Ran to fetch Ivan, Found him in a deep sleep, And brought him in his shirt.

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar began: “Listen, There is a report against you, Ivan. They say that just now You boasted that you would find us Another bird, That is, the Tsar-Maiden...”

**IVAN**: “What, what, God be with you!”

**NARRATOR**: Began the Tsar's groom.

**IVAN**: “I must have said it half asleep, And played such a trick. Be as cunning as you like, You cannot fool me.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar, shaking his beard:

**TSAR**: “What? Am I to bargain with you?”

**NARRATOR**: He cried.

**TSAR**: “But look, if in three weeks You do not bring me the Tsar-Maiden To our royal chamber, Then, I swear by my beard, You will pay me for it: To the whipping, to the grate, to the stake! Out, you slave!”

**NARRATOR**: Ivan wept And went to the hayloft, Where his little horse was lying.

**KONEK**: “What is the matter, Ivan dear? Why are you sad? Why hang your head? Are you ill, my dear? Or have you fallen into an enemy's hands?”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse. Ivan fell on the horse's neck, Hugged him and kissed him.

**IVAN**: “Oh, it is trouble, little horse!” he said. “The Tsar orders me to get him the Tsar-Maiden For his chamber. What shall I do, Humpbacked Horse?”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse said to him:

**KONEK**: “The trouble is big, I do not argue; But I can help you in your grief. Your trouble comes from this: That you did not listen to me. But, as a friend, I will say: This is a small service, not the real one; The real service is still to come! Go to the Tsar now And say: ‘To catch her, Tsar, I need two embroidered cloths, A tent sewn with gold, And a dinner set, All of foreign jams And sweets to refresh her.’”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan goes to the Tsar And says to him:

**IVAN**: “To catch the princess, Tsar, I need two embroidered cloths, A tent sewn with gold, And a dinner set, All of foreign jams And sweets to refresh her.”

**TSAR**: “You should have done so long ago,”

**NARRATOR**: Answered the Tsar from his bed, And ordered the nobles To find everything for Ivan; He called him a fine fellow And said: “A happy journey!”

**NARRATOR**: Next day, early in the morning, The little horse woke Ivan:

**KONEK**: “Hey! Master! Enough of sleeping! It is time to do the work!”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan got up, Got ready for the road, Took the cloths and the tent And the dinner set, All of foreign jams And sweets to refresh her; He put all in a travelling bag And tied it with a rope, Dressed himself warmly, Sat upon his little horse, Took out a little piece of bread, And rode off to the east After that Tsar-Maiden.

**NARRATOR**: They rode for a whole week, And at last, on the eighth day, They came to a thick forest. Then the little horse said to Ivan:

**KONEK**: “Here is the road to the ocean, And on it, all the year round, That beauty lives; Only twice does she come down From the ocean and bring The long day to us on the earth. You will see it for yourself tomorrow.”

**NARRATOR**: And, having ended his speech to Ivan, He ran out to the ocean, Where a white wave Was walking all alone. Then Ivan got down from the horse, And the horse said to him:

**KONEK**: “Now, put up the tent, Set the dinner on the cloth, The foreign jams And sweets to refresh her. Lie down yourself behind the tent And use your wits. You see, a little boat is flashing... The princess is sailing near. Let her go into the tent, Let her eat and drink; When she begins to play the harp, Know that the time has come. Run into the tent at once, Seize the princess, Hold her tight, And call me quickly. At your first word I will run straight to you; And we shall ride off... But mind, Watch her closely, For if you sleep through it, You will not escape the trouble.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse vanished from sight, Ivan hid behind the tent, And began to bore a hole To peep at the princess.

**NARRATOR**: Clear noon was coming, The Tsar-Maiden was sailing near, She came into the tent with her harp And sat down at the dinner set.

**IVAN**: “Hm! So that is the Tsar-Maiden! How is it said in tales,”

**NARRATOR**: Reasoned the Tsar's groom,

**IVAN**: “That the Tsar-Maiden is so beautiful, That it is a wonder? This one is not beautiful at all: Pale, and thin, Not much round the waist; And her little feet, her little feet! Pooh! Like a chicken's! Let somebody else like her, I would not take her even for nothing.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the princess began to play, And sang so sweetly That Ivan, not knowing how, Leaned his head on his fist And, to that soft and gentle voice, Fell fast asleep.

**NARRATOR**: The west was quietly burning out. Suddenly the little horse neighed over him, And, kicking him with his hoof, Cried in an angry voice:

**KONEK**: “Sleep, my dear, until the star comes out! Sleep off your troubles, It is not I who will be put on the stake!”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan began to weep, And, sobbing, begged The little horse to forgive him:

**IVAN**: “Forgive Ivan, I will never sleep again.”

**KONEK**: “Well, God will forgive you!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the Humpbacked Horse.

**KONEK**: “Perhaps we can put it right, But mind you do not fall asleep; Tomorrow, early in the morning, The maiden will sail again To the gold-sewn tent To drink sweet mead. But if you sleep again, You will lose your head.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse vanished again; And Ivan began to gather Sharp stones and nails From wrecked ships, So that he might prick himself If he felt sleepy again.

**NARRATOR**: The next day, in the morning, To the gold-sewn tent The Tsar-Maiden sailed, Threw her little boat on the shore, Went into the tent with her harp And sat down at the dinner set... Then the princess began to play, And sang so sweetly That Ivan again Wanted to sleep.

**IVAN**: “No, wait, you naughty girl!”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan, rising.

**IVAN**: “This time you will not get away, And you will not fool me.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan ran into the tent, And caught her long braid...

**IVAN**: “Oh, run, little horse, run! My Humpbacked Horse, help me!”

**NARRATOR**: In a moment the little horse was there.

**KONEK**: “Ah, master, you have done well! Now sit up quickly, And hold her tight!”

**NARRATOR**: So they reached the capital. The Tsar ran out to the princess, Took her by her white hands, Led her into the palace, Sat her at an oak table Under a silken curtain, Looked tenderly into her eyes, And spoke sweet words:

**TSAR**: “Matchless maiden, Agree to be my queen! I had hardly seen you When I burned with a great love. Your falcon eyes Will not let me sleep at night Or in the white day: Oh, they will torment me! Say one kind word! Everything is ready for the wedding; Tomorrow morning, my light, We shall be married, And begin to live in song.”

**NARRATOR**: But the young princess, Saying nothing, Turned away from the Tsar. The Tsar was not at all angry, But fell in love still more; He knelt before her, Gently pressed her little hands, And began his talk again:

**TSAR**: “Say one kind word! How have I grieved you? Is it that I fell in love? Oh, my sad fate!”

**NARRATOR**: Then the princess said:

**MAIDEN**: “If you want to take me, Then bring me in three days My ring from the ocean.”

**TSAR**: “Hey! Call Ivan to me!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar cried in a hurry, And almost ran himself.

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan came before the Tsar, And the Tsar turned to him And said: “Ivan! Go to the ocean; In that ocean is kept The Tsar-Maiden's ring. If you bring it to me, I will reward you with everything.”

**IVAN**: “I can hardly drag my feet From the first journey, And you send me again to the ocean!”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the Tsar.

**TSAR**: “How can I help hurrying, rascal: You see, I want to marry!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar cried in anger And stamped his feet.

**TSAR**: “Do not argue with me, But set off quickly!”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan was about to go.

**MAIDEN**: “Hey, listen! On the way,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the princess to him,

**MAIDEN**: “Call in and bow At my emerald palace And tell my own mother: Her daughter wants to know Why she hides, for three nights And three days, Her bright face from me? And why my bright brother Has wrapped himself in stormy gloom And in the misty heights Does not send a ray to me? Do not forget!”

**IVAN**: “I will remember, if only I do not forget; But I must find out Who your brother is, and who your mother, So that we do not get mixed up with relations.”

**MAIDEN**: “The Moon is my mother, the Sun my brother.”

**TSAR**: “And mind, be back in three days!”

**NARRATOR**: Added the Tsar, the bridegroom. Then Ivan left the Tsar And went to the hayloft, Where his little horse was lying.

**KONEK**: “What is the matter, Ivan dear? Why hang your head?”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse.

**IVAN**: “Help me, Humpbacked Horse! You see, the Tsar has taken it into his head to marry, To a thin little queen, And so he sends me to the ocean,”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the horse.

**IVAN**: “He gave me only three days; Now try to get that wretched ring! And that thin queen told me To call in somewhere, at a palace, To bow to the Sun and the Moon, And ask something besides...”

**NARRATOR**: Then the horse said: “As a friend, I will say, This is a small service, not the real one; The real service is still to come! Now go and sleep; And tomorrow, early in the morning, We shall ride to the ocean.”

**NARRATOR**: Next day our Ivan, Putting three onions in his pocket, Dressed himself warmly, Sat upon his little horse, And set off on a distant journey... Let us rest awhile, my friends!


## III · The Whale, the Moon and the Cauldrons

**NARRATOR**: Until now Makar dug his kitchen garden, And now Makar has become a governor.

**NARRATOR**: Tra-ra-ra-li, tra-ra-ra! The horses ran out of the yard; The peasants caught them And tied them up good and tight. A raven sits upon an oak, And plays upon a trumpet; As he plays upon his trumpet, He cheers the Christian folk:

**RAVEN**: “Hey, listen, honest people! Once there lived a husband and his wife; The husband was fond of jokes, The wife of merry sayings, And they gave a feast so grand That the whole wide world heard of it!”

**NARRATOR**: That is only the beginning of the tale, The tale itself will come after. At our gate a fly Sings a little song:

**FLY**: “What will you give me for the news? A mother-in-law is beating her son's wife: She put her on the perch, Tied her up with a cord, Pulled her hands to her feet, And took off her right shoe: ‘Do not go out at dawn! Do not show yourself to the lads!’”

**NARRATOR**: That was only the beginning of the tale, Now the tale itself begins.

**NARRATOR**: Well, so our Ivan rides For the ring to the ocean. The Humpbacked Horse flies like the wind, And on the first evening, just to start, He went a hundred thousand miles And did not rest anywhere.

**NARRATOR**: As they came near the ocean, The little horse said to Ivan:

**KONEK**: “Now, Ivan dear, look: In just three minutes We shall come to a clearing, Right to the ocean sea; Across it lies A wonder-fish, a whale; For ten years he has been suffering, And does not know yet How to win forgiveness; He will begin to ask you To beg forgiveness for him In the Sun's own village; Promise to do it. But mind you do not forget!”

**NARRATOR**: So he rode into the clearing, Right to the ocean sea; Across it lay The wonder-fish, the whale. All his sides were dug up, Stakes were driven into his ribs, A pine forest rustled on his tail, A village stood upon his back; Peasants ploughed upon his lip, Boys were dancing between his eyes, And in the oak wood, in his whiskers, Girls were looking for mushrooms.

**NARRATOR**: The little horse runs along the whale, Knocking with his hooves on his bones. The wonder-fish, the whale, Spoke to the travellers, Opening his wide mouth, Sighing heavily and sadly:

**WHALE**: “A good road to you, gentlemen! Where are you from, and where do you go?”

**KONEK**: “We are envoys of the Tsar-Maiden, We both come from the capital,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse to the whale,

**KONEK**: “Straight to the Sun, in the east, To his golden halls.”

**WHALE**: “Then, dear fathers, could you not Ask the Sun for me: Shall I be in disgrace for long, And for what sins Do I suffer pains and torment?”

**IVAN**: “All right, all right, whale!”

**NARRATOR**: Our Ivan cried to him.

**WHALE**: “Be a merciful father to me! See how I, poor fellow, suffer! Ten years I have lain here... I will do you a service too!...”

**NARRATOR**: The whale begged Ivan, And sighed bitterly.

**IVAN**: “All right, all right, whale!”

**NARRATOR**: Our Ivan called to him.

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse sprang up beneath him, Leaped to the shore and dashed away, And you could only see the sand Whirling round his feet.

**NARRATOR**: They ride near or far, They ride low or high, And whom they saw on the way, I do not know a thing. A tale is told quickly, But a deed is done slowly. Only, my friends, I found out That the little horse ran to the place Where (so I heard from others) The sky comes down to meet the earth, Where the peasant women spin their flax And lay their distaffs on the sky.

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan said goodbye to the earth And found himself in the sky, And rode on like a prince, His cap on one side, quite at ease.

**IVAN**: “What a wonder! What a wonder! Our kingdom may be pretty,”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the little horse Among the sky-blue meadows,

**IVAN**: “But compared to the sky, It is not even good for a boot-sole. What is the earth? It is Black and dirty; But here the earth is blue, And how bright it is!... Look, little Humpback, Do you see, over there, in the east, Something shines like lightning... It must be the heavenly palace... It is so very high!”

**NARRATOR**: So Ivan asked the little horse.

**KONEK**: “That is the palace of the Tsar-Maiden, Our future queen,”

**NARRATOR**: The Humpbacked Horse said to him.

**KONEK**: “At night the Sun sleeps here, And at noon-time The Moon comes in to rest.”

**NARRATOR**: They came up; at the gates There was a crystal arch on pillars; All those pillars were cleverly twined With little golden snakes, On their tops were three stars, Round the palace were gardens, On silver branches there, In gilded cages, Birds of paradise lived, And sang royal songs. And the palace had other palaces round it Like a town with villages; And on the palace, made of stars, Was a Russian Christian cross.

**NARRATOR**: The little horse rode into the yard; Our Ivan got down from him, Went into the palace to the Moon, And said:

**IVAN**: “Good day, Moon Moonovich! I am Ivan, son of Peter! I come from far-off lands And bring you greetings.”

**MOON**: “Sit down, Ivan, son of Peter,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the Moon Moonovich,

**MOON**: “And tell me the reason Why you came from the earth To our bright country; What people you are of, How you came to these parts, Tell me everything, hide nothing.”

**IVAN**: “I came from the Earthly land, A Christian country,”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan, sitting down.

**IVAN**: “I crossed the ocean With an errand from a queen: To bow in your bright palace And say, wait a moment: ‘Tell my own mother: Her daughter wants to know Why she hides, for three nights And three days, Her face from me And why my bright brother Has wrapped himself in stormy gloom, And in the misty heights Does not send a ray to me?’ Is that right? The queen is a fine speaker; I cannot remember it all That she told me.”

**MOON**: “And what queen is that?”

**IVAN**: “Why, the Tsar-Maiden, you know.”

**MOON**: “The Tsar-Maiden?... Then was it you Who carried her away?”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the Moon Moonovich. And Ivan, son of Peter, said:

**IVAN**: “Yes, I did, of course! I am the Tsar's groom, you see; Well, so the Tsar sent me To bring her to the palace In three weeks; Or else he threatened To put me on a stake.”

**NARRATOR**: The Moon wept for joy, And began to hug Ivan, To kiss and cuddle him.

**MOON**: “Ah, Ivan, son of Peter! You have brought such news That I do not know how to reckon it! How we grieved That we had lost our princess!... That is why, you see, For three nights, for three days, I walked in a dark cloud, Sad and sad again, For three days I did not sleep, And did not touch a crumb of bread. That is why my bright son Wrapped himself in stormy gloom, Put out his hot ray, And did not shine on God's world: He grieved for his sister, That fair Tsar-Maiden. Tell me, is she well? Not sad, not ill?”

**IVAN**: “She is a beauty in every way, But she is rather thin, it seems: Thin as a match, Not much round the waist; When she is ready to be married, No doubt she will get fatter: The Tsar, you know, is going to marry her.”

**NARRATOR**: The Moon cried: “Oh, the villain! At seventy, to want to marry A very young girl! But I stand firm in this: He will sit a bridegroom for ever! See what the old radish has thought of: He wants to reap where he has not sown! Enough, he has grown too greedy!”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan said again:

**IVAN**: “I have one more request for you, About forgiving the whale... There is a sea, and a wonder-whale Lies across it: All his sides are dug up, Stakes are driven into his ribs... The poor fellow begged me To ask you: Will his torment end soon? How can he win forgiveness? And why does he lie there?”

**NARRATOR**: The bright Moon said:

**MOON**: “He suffers this torment Because, without God's leave, He swallowed, in the middle of the sea, Thirty ships. If he lets them go free, God will lift the trouble from him, Heal all his wounds in a moment, And give him a long life.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan got up And said goodbye to the bright Moon, Hugged him tightly round the neck, And kissed him three times on the cheeks.

**MOON**: “Well, Ivan, son of Peter,”

**NARRATOR**: Said the Moon Moonovich,

**MOON**: “I thank you For my son and for myself. Take my blessing To comfort our daughter And tell my own child: ‘Your mother is always with you; Stop weeping and grieving: Soon your sadness will be ended, And not an old man with a beard, But a handsome young man Will lead you to the altar.’ Well, goodbye! God be with you!”

**NARRATOR**: Bowing as well as he could, Ivan got on his little horse, Whistled like a famous hero, And set off on the way back.

**NARRATOR**: The next day our Ivan Came again to the ocean. The little horse runs along the whale, Knocking with his hooves on his bones. The wonder-fish, the whale, Sighed, and said:

**WHALE**: “Well, fathers, what of my request? Shall I ever be forgiven?”

**KONEK**: “Wait a little, whale!”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse cried to him.

**NARRATOR**: Then he ran into the village, Called the peasants together, Shook his black mane, And spoke these words:

**KONEK**: “Hey, listen, good people, Orthodox Christians! If any of you does not want To serve the Water Spirit, Get away from here at once. Here a wonder will happen right now: The sea will boil up fiercely, The whale will turn over...”

**NARRATOR**: Then the peasants and the villagers Cried: “There will be trouble!” And hurried home. They gathered all their carts; Without delay they loaded All the goods they had, And left the whale. Morning met noon, And in the village there was left Not a single living soul, As if Mamai's army had come!

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse ran on to his tail, Lay down close to his fins, And shouted with all his might:

**KONEK**: “Wonder-fish, whale! Your torment comes from this: That, without God's leave, You swallowed, in the middle of the sea, Thirty ships. If you let them go free, God will lift the trouble from you, Heal all your wounds in a moment, And give you a long life.”

**NARRATOR**: And, having ended this speech, He bit his steel bridle, Strained himself, and in a moment Jumped to the far shore.

**NARRATOR**: The whale stirred, Like a hill turning over, Began to stir the sea, And threw out of his jaws Ships after ships, With their sails and oarsmen.

**NARRATOR**: Then such a noise arose That the Sea King woke: They fired the copper cannons, They blew the forged trumpets; The white sails rose up, The flags flew on the masts; The priest and all his helpers Sang thanksgiving on the deck; And the merry row of oarsmen Struck up a song together:

**SAILORS**: “On the little sea, the sea, On the wide, wide open space, To the very edge of the earth, The ships come sailing out...”

**NARRATOR**: The waves of the sea rolled up, The ships were lost from sight. The wonder-fish, the whale, Cried in a loud voice, Opening his wide mouth, Splashing the waves with his fin:

**WHALE**: “How can I serve you, friends? How can I reward you for your service? Do you want colourful shells? Do you want little golden fish? Do you want big pearls? I am ready to get you all!”

**IVAN**: “No, whale, we do not need Any of that as a reward,”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to him.

**IVAN**: “Better find the ring for us, The ring, you know, of the Tsar-Maiden, Our future queen.”

**WHALE**: “All right, all right! For a friend I would give even the earring from my ear! I will find before the lightning of dawn The ring of the fair Tsar-Maiden,”

**NARRATOR**: The whale answered Ivan And dropped to the bottom like a stone.

**NARRATOR**: He slaps the water with his fin, And in a loud voice calls All the sturgeon folk, And says to them:

**WHALE**: “Find, before dawn, The ring of the fair Tsar-Maiden, Hidden in a little box on the sea-bed. Whoever brings it to me, I will reward with a rank: He shall be a nobleman of the council. But if you do not carry out My wise order... I will show you!”

**NARRATOR**: The sturgeons bowed, And swam off in order.

**NARRATOR**: After a few hours, Two white sturgeons Swam slowly to the whale, And humbly said:

**STURGEON**: “Great King! Do not be angry! We have walked and dug over The whole sea, it seems, But we have found no sign. Only the ruff, one of us, Could carry out your order: He wanders in all the seas, So he surely knows the ring; But, as if to spite us, He has gone off somewhere.”

**WHALE**: “Find him in a minute And send him to my cabin!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the whale angrily, Wagging his whiskers.

**NARRATOR**: The sturgeons bowed, Ran off to the district court, And ordered at once That a decree be written by the whale, That messengers be sent quickly And the ruff be caught. The bream, hearing this order, Wrote the decree in the whale's name; The catfish (he was called a counsellor) Signed the decree; The black crayfish folded the decree And put the seals on it. Then they called two dolphins, And, giving them the decree, said That, in the name of the king, They should run through all the seas And find that rover of a ruff, Brawler and bully, Wherever he might be, And bring him to the king. Then the dolphins bowed And went to look for the ruff.

**NARRATOR**: They search for an hour in the seas, They search for an hour in the rivers, They have walked round all the lakes, They have swum through all the straits, They could not find the ruff And turned back, Almost weeping from sadness...

**NARRATOR**: Suddenly the dolphins heard, Somewhere in a little pond, A terrible shouting in the water. The dolphins turned into the pond And dived to the bottom: Look! In the pond, under the reeds, The ruff is fighting a carp.

**DOLPHINS**: “Quiet! May the devils take you! What a row you have raised, Like two great fighters!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the messengers.

**RUFF**: “And what is it to you?”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the ruff boldly to the dolphins.

**RUFF**: “I do not like jokes, I will prick you all at once!”

**DOLPHINS**: “Oh, you everlasting rover, Shouter and bully! You are always at your wanderings, Always fighting and shouting. You cannot sit at home!... Well, why bargain with you, Here is the king's decree, That you swim to him at once.”

**NARRATOR**: Then the dolphins took the rascal By his bristles And started back. The ruff began to struggle and shout:

**RUFF**: “Be kind, brothers! Let me have just a little fight. That wretched carp Abused me yesterday Before the whole honest company With all sorts of rude names...”

**NARRATOR**: The ruff shouted for a long time, And at last he was quiet; And the dolphins went on dragging The rascal by his bristles, Saying nothing, And appeared before the king.

**WHALE**: “Why did you not come sooner? Where have you been wandering, rascal?”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the whale in anger. The ruff fell on his knees, And, confessing his fault, He prayed to be forgiven.

**WHALE**: “Well, God will forgive you! But for this forgiveness You must carry out my order.”

**RUFF**: “Glad to serve, wonder-whale!”

**NARRATOR**: Squeaked the ruff on his knees.

**WHALE**: “You wander in all the seas, So you surely know the ring Of the Tsar-Maiden?”

**RUFF**: “Of course I know it! We can find it at once.”

**WHALE**: “Then go as quickly as you can And find it fast!”

**NARRATOR**: Then the ruff bowed to the king And went out, bending low, Quarrelled with the king's servants, Chased after the roach, And on the way bloodied the noses Of six little sprats. Having done such a deed, He boldly dived into a whirlpool And in the underwater deep Dug up a little chest from the bottom, A hundred pounds in weight at the very least.

**RUFF**: “Oh, this is not so simple!”

**NARRATOR**: And he began to call the herrings From all the seas to him. The herrings gathered in a crowd And set about dragging the little chest, And all you could hear was “Oo-oo-oo!” and “Oh-oh-oh!” But however loudly they shouted, They only strained their bellies, And the wretched chest Did not move an inch.

**RUFF**: “Fine herrings, indeed! A whip for you instead of vodka!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the ruff, in a great rage, And dived for the sturgeons. The sturgeons swam up And without a shout lifted The little red chest with the ring, Stuck fast in the sand.

**RUFF**: “Well, lads, now mind you Swim to the king, And I will go down to the bottom And rest a little: Sleep is overcoming me, And my eyes are closing...”

**NARRATOR**: The sturgeons swim to the king, And the roving ruff to the pond (From which the dolphins Dragged him by his bristles), To finish his fight with the carp, But I do not know about that. Now we will say goodbye to him And go back to Ivan.

**NARRATOR**: The ocean sea was quiet. Ivan sat on the sand, Waiting for the whale from the blue sea, And purring from sorrow; Lying on the sand, The faithful Humpback dozed. Evening was drawing on; The sun had sunk; Burning with a quiet flame, The sunset spread out. But there was no sign of the whale.

**IVAN**: “May you be crushed, you thief! What a sea-devil!”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to himself.

**IVAN**: “He promised to bring up The Tsar-Maiden's ring before dawn, And has not found it yet, The wretched joker! And the sun has set, and...”

**NARRATOR**: Then the sea began to boil: The wonder-whale appeared And said to Ivan:

**WHALE**: “For your kindness I have kept my promise.”

**NARRATOR**: With these words he put the little chest Down upon the sand with a thud, And the shore shook.

**WHALE**: “Now we are even. If you are in need again, Call me again; I shall never forget Your kindness... Goodbye!”

**NARRATOR**: Then the wonder-whale was silent, And, splashing, dived to the bottom.

**NARRATOR**: The Humpbacked Horse woke up, Stood on his little legs, and shook himself, Looked at Ivan, And jumped four times:

**KONEK**: “Hurrah for Whale Whalovich! Splendid! He has paid his debt exactly! Well, thank you, whale!”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the Humpbacked Horse.

**KONEK**: “Well, master, dress yourself, And set off on the road; Three days have already gone: Tomorrow is the appointed day. The old man must be dying by now.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan answered:

**IVAN**: “I would be glad to lift it, But I cannot borrow strength! The little chest is very heavy, The wicked whale must have put Five hundred devils in it. I have lifted it three times: It is such a terrible weight!”

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse, without answering, Lifted the little chest with his foot As if it were a little pebble, And swung it on to his neck.

**KONEK**: “Well, Ivan, get on quickly! Remember, tomorrow the time is up, And the way back is far.”

**NARRATOR**: The fourth day began to dawn. Our Ivan was already in the capital. The Tsar ran down from the porch.

**TSAR**: “What about my ring?”

**NARRATOR**: He cried. Then Ivan got down And answered very grandly:

**IVAN**: “Here is your little chest! But call out a regiment: The chest is small to look at, But it would crush even a devil.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar called the archers at once And ordered them without delay To carry the chest to his chamber, And went himself for the Tsar-Maiden.

**TSAR**: “Your ring is found, my soul,”

**NARRATOR**: He said in a sweet voice,

**TSAR**: “And now, I say again, There is no obstacle at all For us to be married Tomorrow morning, my light. But would you not like, my dear, To see your little ring? It lies in my palace.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar-Maiden said:

**MAIDEN**: “I know, I know! But, to tell the truth, We cannot be married yet.”

**TSAR**: “Why not, my light? I love you with all my soul, Forgive my boldness, I am dying to marry. And if you... I shall die Tomorrow morning of grief. Have pity, dear queen!”

**NARRATOR**: The maiden said to him:

**MAIDEN**: “But look, you are grey-haired, I am only fifteen: How can we be married? All the tsars will begin to laugh: ‘The grandfather,’ they will say, ‘has taken the grandchild!’”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar cried in anger:

**TSAR**: “Let them only laugh, I will turn them upside down: I will take all their kingdoms, I will root out all their families!”

**MAIDEN**: “Even if they do not laugh, Still we cannot be married: Flowers do not grow in winter: I am a beauty, and you?... What can you boast of?”

**NARRATOR**: Said the maiden to him.

**TSAR**: “Though I am old, I am a dashing fellow!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar answered the queen.

**TSAR**: “When I tidy myself up a little, I shall look to anyone Like a dashing young man. But what does it matter? If only we can be married.”

**NARRATOR**: The maiden said to him:

**MAIDEN**: “It matters this much: I shall never marry A bad old grey one, Without any teeth!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar scratched the back of his head And said, frowning:

**TSAR**: “What am I to do, queen? I am dreadfully anxious to marry; And you, as if to spite me, Say: ‘I will not, I will not!’”

**MAIDEN**: “I will not marry a grey-haired man. Become a young man as you were, And I will go to the altar at once.”

**TSAR**: “Remember, dear queen, One cannot be born again; God alone works wonders.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar-Maiden said:

**MAIDEN**: “If you do not spare yourself, You will grow young again. Listen: tomorrow at dawn, In the wide courtyard, You must have your servants Set three big cauldrons And make fires under them. Fill the first To the brim with cold water, The second with boiled water, And the last with milk, Boiled till it bubbles. Now, if you want to marry And become a handsome man, Without your clothes, lightly, Bathe in the milk; Then stay a while in the boiled water, And then again in the cold, And I tell you, Father, You will be a splendid fellow!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar did not say a word, But called the royal groom at once.

**IVAN**: “What, again to the ocean?”

**NARRATOR**: Said Ivan to the Tsar.

**IVAN**: “No, no, your Grace! I am all shaken up already. I will not go for anything!”

**TSAR**: “No, Ivan, not that. Tomorrow I want to have Cauldrons set in the courtyard, And fires lit under them. I think to fill the first To the brim with cold water, The second with boiled water, And the last with milk, Boiled till it bubbles. And you must try, for a test, To bathe in these three big cauldrons, In the milk and in the two waters.”

**IVAN**: “See where he is going with this!”

**NARRATOR**: Ivan began to say.

**IVAN**: “They scald only piglets, And turkeys, and chickens; Look, I am not a piglet, Not a turkey, not a chicken. Now, in cold water, I might Bathe all right, But when you begin to warm it, You will not tempt me. Enough, Tsar, of your cunning and cleverness, And of seeing Ivan off!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar, shaking his beard:

**TSAR**: “What? Am I to bargain with you!”

**NARRATOR**: He cried.

**TSAR**: “But look! If at the break of dawn You do not do what I order, I will give you over to torment, I will have you tortured, And torn into little pieces. Out of here, you plague!”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan, weeping, Dragged himself to the hayloft, Where his little horse lay.

**KONEK**: “What is the matter, Ivan dear? Why do you hang your head? I think our old bridegroom Has thought up a new trick?”

**NARRATOR**: Said the little horse. Ivan fell on the little horse's neck, Hugged him and kissed him.

**IVAN**: “Oh, it is trouble, little horse!” he said. “The Tsar is getting rid of me for good; Think, he orders me To bathe in cauldrons, In milk and in two waters: One cold water, In another boiled water, And the milk, you know, boiling.”

**NARRATOR**: The little horse said:

**KONEK**: “Now that is a real service! Here all my friendship is needed. I must say, by the way: We had better not have taken the feather; From that villain feather So much trouble has come on your neck... Well, do not cry, God be with you! We will manage this trouble somehow. I would sooner die myself Than leave you, Ivan. Listen: tomorrow at dawn, When, in the courtyard, You have undressed as you should, Say to the Tsar: ‘Could you not, Your Grace, order My Humpbacked Horse to be sent to me, To say goodbye to him for the last time.’ The Tsar will agree to it. Then, when I wave my tail, Dip my nose into the cauldrons, Spray you twice, And whistle a loud whistle, Be sure you do not yawn: Dive first into the milk, Then into the cauldron of boiled water, And from there into the cold. And now say your prayers And go quietly to bed.”

**NARRATOR**: The next day, early in the morning, The little horse woke Ivan:

**KONEK**: “Hey, master, enough of sleeping! It is time to do your service.”

**NARRATOR**: Then Ivan scratched himself, Stretched, and got up, Said a prayer to the fence, And went to the Tsar's courtyard.

**NARRATOR**: There the cauldrons were already boiling; Beside them, in a row, sat The coachmen, and the cooks, And the servants of the court, Zealously adding wood, Talking about Ivan Quietly among themselves, And laughing now and then.

**NARRATOR**: Then the doors opened; The Tsar and the queen appeared And got ready from the porch To look at the brave fellow.

**TSAR**: “Well, Ivan, undress, And bathe in the cauldrons, brother!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar cried to Ivan. Then Ivan took off his clothes, Not answering a word. And the young queen, Not to see his nakedness, Wrapped herself in her veil. Ivan went up to the cauldrons, Looked in, and scratched himself.

**TSAR**: “Why have you stopped, Ivan?”

**NARRATOR**: Cried the Tsar again.

**TSAR**: “Do what you must, brother!”

**IVAN**: “Could you not, your Grace, Order my Humpbacked Horse to be sent to me? I would say goodbye to him for the last time.”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar, thinking, agreed And ordered The horse to be sent to him. The servant brought the little horse And stepped aside himself.

**NARRATOR**: Then the little horse waved his tail, Dipped his nose in the cauldrons, Sprayed Ivan twice, And whistled a loud whistle. Ivan looked at the little horse And dived into the cauldron at once, Then into another, and then into the third, And became so handsome That no tale can tell And no pen can write! Then he put on his clothes, Bowed to the Tsar-Maiden, Looked about him, in good heart, With a grand air, like a prince.

**CROWD**: “What a wonder!”

**NARRATOR**: They all cried.

**CROWD**: “We never heard of it, That one could grow handsomer!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar ordered himself undressed, Crossed himself twice, Splash! into the cauldron, and was boiled there!

**NARRATOR**: Then the Tsar-Maiden stands up, Gives a sign for silence, Lifts her veil, And speaks to the servants:

**MAIDEN**: “The Tsar has ordered you to live long! I want to be your queen. Do you like me? Answer me! If you like me, then acknowledge As master of everything And as my husband...”

**NARRATOR**: Then the queen was silent, And pointed to Ivan.

**CROWD**: “We like you, we like you!”

**NARRATOR**: They all cry.

**CROWD**: “For you we would go even to the end of the world! For the sake of your good fortune We acknowledge Tsar Ivan!”

**NARRATOR**: The Tsar takes the queen, Leads her to God's church, And with his young bride He walks round the altar.

**NARRATOR**: The cannon fire from the fortress; The forged trumpets blow; They open all the cellars, They set out barrels of foreign wine, And the people, having drunk, Shout with all their might:

**CROWD**: “Hail to our Tsar and Tsaritsa! To the most beautiful Tsar-Maiden!”

**NARRATOR**: And in the palace the feast is a mountain: Wine flows like a river, At oak tables The nobles and princes drink. It is a joy to the heart! I was there, I drank mead, wine and beer; It ran down my moustache, But not a drop got into my mouth.

**NARRATOR**: And that is the end of the tale. Thank you for listening, and sleep well.
