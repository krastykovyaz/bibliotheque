
## I · The Boy with His Hand Out

**NARRATOR**: Children are a strange people; they appear in dreams and in visions. Before Christmas, and right up to Christmas itself, I kept meeting one little boy on the street, at a certain corner, no more than seven years old. In the terrible frost he was dressed almost for summer, yet his neck was wrapped in some old rag, so somebody had still equipped him and sent him out. He went about “with his hand out”; that is a technical term, it means begging. The boys themselves invented the term. There are a great many like him; they swarm across your path and whine something they have learned by heart. But this one did not whine. He spoke somehow innocently and unaccustomedly, and looked trustfully into my eyes, so he was evidently only beginning the trade. To my questions he answered that he had a sister, out of work and sick. Perhaps it was true; but I learned afterwards that there are countless numbers of these boys: they are sent out “with the hand” even in the most dreadful frost, and if they collect nothing, a beating surely awaits them. Having gathered his kopecks, the boy comes back with red, numbed hands to some cellar where a gang of smock-wearing workmen is drinking, the very men who, “having gone on strike at the factory on Saturday before Sunday, return to work no earlier than Wednesday evening.” There in the cellars their hungry, beaten wives drink with them, and right there their hungry babies squeal. Vodka, and filth, and debauchery, and above all, vodka. With the kopecks he has gathered, the boy is sent at once to the tavern, and he brings back more drink. For fun they sometimes pour a glass into his mouth too, and roar with laughter when, with his breath cut short, he falls almost senseless to the floor, and they pour on,

**NARRATOR**: and into my mouth the foul vodka, without pity they poured...

**NARRATOR**: When he grows a little older, they pack him off as quickly as they can to some factory, but everything he earns he is bound to bring back to the workmen, who drink it away again. But even before the factory these children become complete criminals. They wander about the town and know places in various cellars where one can crawl in and spend the night unnoticed. One of them slept several nights running in a basket belonging to a certain yard-keeper, and the man never noticed him. Naturally they become little thieves. Stealing turns into a passion even in eight-year-old children, sometimes without any awareness that the act is a crime. In the end they endure everything, hunger, cold, beatings, for one thing only, freedom, and they run away from their workmen to wander on their own. This wild creature sometimes understands nothing at all: neither where he lives, nor of what nation he is, nor whether there is a God, nor whether there is a Tsar. Such things are told about them that it is hard to believe, and yet they are all facts.


## II · The Cellar

**NARRATOR**: But I am a novelist, and it seems I have made up one “story” myself. Why do I write “it seems”? I know very well that I made it up; yet I keep imagining that it happened somewhere, sometime, that it happened just on the eve of Christmas, in some enormous city and in a terrible frost.

**NARRATOR**: I imagine there was a boy in a cellar, but still very small, six years old or even less. This boy woke up in the morning in a damp, cold cellar. He was dressed in some sort of little smock and was shivering. His breath flew out as white steam, and sitting in a corner on a chest, he blew the steam out of his mouth on purpose, out of boredom, and amused himself by watching it fly out. But he very much wanted to eat. Several times since morning he had gone up to the bunk where his sick mother lay on a bedding as thin as a pancake, with some bundle under her head for a pillow. How had she come to be here? She must have arrived with her boy from some strange town and suddenly fallen ill. The woman who kept the lodgings had been taken to the police two days before; the lodgers had scattered, it was a holiday, and the one workman who was left had already lain dead drunk for a whole day, not having waited even for the holiday itself. In another corner of the room an eighty-year-old woman, who had once lived somewhere as a nurse and was now dying alone, groaned with rheumatism, sighing, grumbling and growling at the boy, so that he had come to be afraid of going near her corner. A drink of water he had found somewhere in the passage, but a crust he could find nowhere, and for the tenth time already he went to wake his mama. At last he began to feel uneasy in the dark: evening had begun long ago, and no light was lit. Touching his mama's face, he was surprised that she did not move at all and had become as cold as the wall. “It is very cold here,” he thought, stood a little while, unconsciously forgetting his hand on the dead woman's shoulder, then breathed on his fingers to warm them, and suddenly, groping for his little cap on the bunk, he went quietly, feeling his way, out of the cellar. He would have gone even earlier, but upstairs on the staircase he was afraid of a big dog that had howled all day by the neighbours' door. But the dog was gone, and he suddenly came out into the street.


## III · The City

**NARRATOR**: Lord, what a city! He had never seen anything like it. Where he had come from, the nights are such black darkness, one lamp for a whole street. Low wooden houses are shut up with shutters; in the street, as soon as dusk falls there is nobody, everyone shuts himself in his house, and only whole packs of dogs howl, hundreds and thousands of them, howling and barking all night. But there it was so warm, and they gave him something to eat, and here, Lord, if only he could eat! And what a clatter and thunder here, what light, and people, horses and carriages, and the frost, the frost! Frozen steam pours from the driven horses, from their hotly breathing muzzles; through the loose snow the horseshoes ring on the stones, and everyone jostles so, and, Lord, he wants so much to eat, just a morsel of something, and his little fingers suddenly hurt so. A policeman passed by and turned away so as not to notice the boy.

**NARRATOR**: And here is another street, oh, how wide! Here they will surely crush him; how they all shout, run and ride, and the light, the light! And what is this? Oh, what a big pane of glass, and behind the glass a room, and in the room a tree up to the ceiling; it is a Christmas tree, and on the tree how many lights, how many gold papers and apples, and all around little dolls, tiny horses; and children run about the room, dressed up, clean, laughing and playing, and eating and drinking something. Here a girl has begun to dance with a boy, what a pretty girl! And there is music, you can hear it through the glass. The boy looks, marvels, is already laughing, but his little fingers already hurt, both on his feet and on his hands, which have turned quite red, will no longer bend, and hurt to move. And suddenly the boy remembered that his little fingers hurt so, began to cry and ran on, and now he sees through another pane a room, again there are trees, but on the tables there are pies, all kinds, almond ones, red, yellow, and four rich ladies are sitting there, and whoever comes in they give a pie, and the door opens every minute, and many gentlemen come in to them from the street. The boy crept up, suddenly opened the door and went in. Oh, how they shouted at him and waved! One lady quickly came up and thrust a little kopeck into his hand, and herself opened the door to the street for him. How frightened he was! And the little kopeck rolled right out and rang on the steps: he could not bend his red fingers and hold on to it. The boy ran out and went quickly, quickly, where he did not know himself. He wants to cry again, but is afraid, and runs and runs and blows on his hands. And misery seizes him, because he suddenly feels so lonely and uneasy, and suddenly, Lord! What is this again? People stand in a crowd marvelling: in a window behind glass there are three dolls, small, dressed in little red and green frocks and quite, quite like living! An old man sits and seems to play a big violin, two others stand there and play little violins, and nod their little heads in time, and look at each other, and their lips move, they speak, really speak, only through the glass you cannot hear. And at first the boy thought they were alive, and when he guessed that they were dolls, he suddenly burst out laughing. Never had he seen such dolls and he did not know there were such! He wants to cry, but it is so funny, so funny, the little dolls. Suddenly he felt that someone had seized him from behind by his little smock: a big wicked boy stood beside him and suddenly cracked him on the head, tore off his cap, and from below tripped him with his foot. The boy rolled to the ground, there was a shout, he went faint, jumped up and ran and ran, and suddenly ran he did not know where, into a gateway, into a strange yard, and crouched down behind the firewood: “They will not find me here, and it is dark.”


## IV · Christ's Tree

**NARRATOR**: He crouched down and curled up, unable to catch his breath for fear, and suddenly, quite suddenly, it became so good for him: his hands and feet suddenly stopped hurting and it grew so warm, so warm, as on top of a stove; now he shuddered all over: ah, but he had been about to fall asleep! How good it is to fall asleep here: “I will sit here a little and then go and look at the dolls again,” thought the boy, and smiled, remembering them, “quite like living!...” And suddenly he heard his mama singing a little song over him. “Mama, I am asleep, ah, how good it is to sleep here!”

**VOICE**: “Come to my Christmas tree, little boy,” a quiet voice suddenly whispered over him.

**NARRATOR**: He thought at first that it was still his mama, but no, it was not she; who it was that called him he cannot see, but someone bent over him and embraced him in the darkness, and he stretched out his hand to him and... and suddenly, oh, what light! Oh, what a tree! And it is not even a Christmas tree, he has never seen such trees! Where is he now? Everything glitters, everything shines, and all around are dolls, but no, these are all boys and girls, only so bright, they all whirl around him, fly, they all kiss him, take him, carry him with them, and he himself flies, and he sees: his mama is looking at him and laughing at him joyfully.

**BOY**: “Mama! Mama! Ah, how good it is here, Mama!” the boy cries to her, and again he kisses the children, and wants to tell them quickly about those dolls behind the glass. “Who are you, boys? Who are you, girls?” he asks, laughing and loving them.

**NARRATOR**: “This is Christ's tree,” they answer him. “Christ always has a tree on this day for the little children who have no tree of their own there...” And he learned that these boys and girls were all just such children as he, but some had frozen already in their baskets, in which they had been left on the stairs at the doors of the officials of Petersburg; others had been smothered in the care of Finnish women, put out to nurse from the foundling hospital; others had died at the dried-up breasts of their mothers, during the Samara famine; others had suffocated in third-class railway carriages from the stench; and now they are all here, all of them now are like angels, all with Christ, and He Himself is in the midst of them, and stretches out His hands to them, and blesses them and their sinful mothers... And the mothers of these children all stand there too, to one side, and weep; each one recognises her own boy or girl, and they fly up to them and kiss them, wipe away their tears with their little hands and beg them not to cry, because it is so good for them here...


## V · The Writer's Word

**NARRATOR**: And down below, in the morning, the yard-keepers found the small body of the boy who had run in and frozen behind the firewood; and they found his mama too... She had died even before him; the two met again at the Lord God's, in heaven.

**NARRATOR**: And why did I make up such a story, so out of place in an ordinary sensible diary, and a writer's diary at that? And I had promised stories chiefly about real events! But that is just it, it keeps seeming and appearing to me that all this could really have happened, I mean what went on in the cellar and behind the firewood; but as for the tree at Christ's, I really do not know how to tell you whether it could have happened or not. That is why I am a novelist, to invent.
