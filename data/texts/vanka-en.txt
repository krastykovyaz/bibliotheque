## I · Christmas Eve

Vanka Zhukov, a nine-year-old boy who three months ago had been apprenticed to the shoemaker Alyakhin, did not go to bed on Christmas Eve. He waited until the master, the mistress and the journeymen had gone to early service, then took a bottle of ink and a pen with a rusty nib from the master's cupboard and, spreading a crumpled sheet of paper in front of him, began to write. Before he made the first letter he looked round fearfully several times at the door and the windows, glanced sideways at the dark icon, on both sides of which shelves of shoe lasts stretched away, and sighed in little gasps. The paper lay on the bench, and he himself knelt in front of the bench.

“Dear Grandfather, Konstantin Makarych,” he wrote. “I am writing you a letter. I wish you a merry Christmas and all the best from the Lord God. I have no father and no mother, only you are left to me.”

Vanka lifted his eyes to the dark window, where the reflection of his candle flickered, and vividly imagined his grandfather, Konstantin Makarych, who was night watchman for the Zhivarevs, the gentry family. He is a small, thin, but wonderfully quick and lively little old man of about sixty-five, with a face that is always laughing and drunken eyes. By day he sleeps in the servants' kitchen or jokes with the cooks; at night, wrapped in a roomy sheepskin coat, he walks round the estate and taps on his watchman's board. Behind him, heads hanging, trudge old Kashtanka and the little dog Eel, called that because of his black colour and his body, as long as a weasel's. This Eel is wonderfully respectful and affectionate, and looks just as sweetly at his own people as at strangers, but he enjoys no credit. Under his respect and humility hides the most Jesuitical malice. No one knows better how to creep up in time and snap at a leg, get into the ice-cellar, or steal a hen from a peasant. His hind legs have been beaten more than once, twice he has been hanged, every week he is flogged half to death, but he always comes back to life.

## II · The Village

Now, no doubt, his grandfather is standing at the gate, squinting at the bright red windows of the village church and, stamping his felt boots, joking with the house servants. His watchman's board is tied to his belt. He claps his hands, hunches against the cold and, chuckling like an old man, pinches now a maid, now a cook.

“Shall we have a pinch of snuff?” he says, holding out his snuffbox to the women.

The women take snuff and sneeze. The grandfather is beside himself with delight, bursts into merry laughter and shouts:

“Tear it off, it's frozen on!”

They let the dogs have a sniff of snuff too. Kashtanka sneezes, shakes her muzzle and, offended, walks away. Eel, out of respect, does not sneeze, and wags his tail. And the weather is magnificent. The air is still, clear and fresh. The night is dark, but you can see the whole village with its white roofs and the threads of smoke rising from the chimneys, the trees silvered with frost, the snowdrifts. The whole sky is strewn with merrily twinkling stars, and the Milky Way stands out so clearly that it might have been washed and rubbed with snow before the holiday...

## III · The Letter

Vanka sighed, dipped his pen and went on writing:

“And yesterday I got a thrashing. The master dragged me out into the yard by my hair and thrashed me with a strap because I was rocking their baby in the cradle and fell asleep by accident. And one day this week the mistress told me to clean a herring, and I began from the tail, and she took the herring and started poking me in the face with its head. The journeymen make fun of me, send me to the tavern for vodka and tell me to steal the master's cucumbers, and the master beats me with whatever comes to hand. And there is no food at all. In the morning they give me bread, at dinner porridge, and in the evening bread again, but as for tea or cabbage soup, the masters gobble that up themselves. And they make me sleep in the passage, and when their baby cries I don't sleep at all, I rock the cradle. Dear grandfather, do me the kindness of God, take me away from here, home to the village, there is no way for me to bear it... I bow down to your feet and will pray to God for you for ever, take me away from here or I shall die...”

Vanka twisted his mouth, rubbed his eyes with his black fist and sobbed.

“I will grind snuff for you,” he went on, “and pray to God, and if anything, then flog me like Sidor's goat. And if you think there is no work for me, then for Christ's sake I will ask the steward to let me clean his boots, or I will go instead of Fedka as the shepherd's boy. Dear grandfather, there is no way, it is simply death. I wanted to run away to the village on foot, but I have no boots and I am afraid of the frost. And when I grow up I will feed you for this, and not let anyone hurt you, and when you die I will pray for the rest of your soul, just as for my mother Pelageya.

And Moscow is a big city. The houses are all gentlemen's and there are many horses, but no sheep, and the dogs are not fierce. The boys don't go round here with the star at Christmas and they don't let anybody sing in the choir, and once I saw in a shop window hooks for sale, with line and all, for every kind of fish, very good ones, there is even one hook that would hold a catfish of forty pounds. And I saw shops where there are guns of every kind, like the master's, so that I daresay each one costs a hundred roubles... And in the butchers' shops there are black grouse and hazel grouse and hares, and where they are shot the shop assistants don't say.

Dear grandfather, and when the gentry have their Christmas tree with presents, take a gilded walnut for me and hide it in the green chest. Ask Miss Olga Ignatyevna, say it is for Vanka.”

## IV · The Christmas Tree

Vanka sighed convulsively and again stared at the window. He remembered that it was always his grandfather who went into the forest for the gentry's Christmas tree and took his grandson with him. It was a merry time! The grandfather grunted, and the frost grunted, and seeing them Vanka grunted too. Before cutting down the tree, the grandfather would smoke a pipe, take a long pinch of snuff, and laugh at the shivering little Vanya... The young fir trees, wrapped in hoarfrost, stand motionless and wait to see which of them is to die. From nowhere at all a hare comes flying like an arrow over the snowdrifts... The grandfather cannot help shouting:

“Catch him, catch him... catch him! Ah, the short-tailed devil!”

The grandfather dragged the felled tree to the big house, and there they set about decorating it... Miss Olga Ignatyevna, Vanka's favourite, bustled about most of all. When Vanka's mother Pelageya was still alive and served the gentry as a chambermaid, Olga Ignatyevna used to feed Vanka sweets and, having nothing better to do, taught him to read, to write, to count up to a hundred and even to dance the quadrille. But when Pelageya died, the orphan Vanka was packed off to the servants' kitchen to his grandfather, and from the kitchen to Moscow, to the shoemaker Alyakhin...

## V · The Post Box

“Come, dear grandfather,” Vanka went on, “I beg you in the name of Christ God, take me away from here. Have pity on me, a poor unhappy orphan, because everyone beats me and I am terribly hungry, and it is so dull that I can't tell you, I cry all the time. And the other day the master hit me on the head with a last so that I fell down and could hardly come to. My life is lost, worse than any dog's... And I send my bows to Alyona, to one-eyed Yegorka and to the coachman, and don't give my accordion to anybody. I remain your grandson Ivan Zhukov, dear grandfather, come.”

Vanka folded the written sheet in four and put it into an envelope bought the day before for a kopeck... After thinking a little, he dipped his pen and wrote the address:

“To grandfather in the village.”

Then he scratched himself, thought, and added: “Konstantin Makarych.” Pleased that no one had disturbed him while he wrote, he put on his cap and, without throwing his little fur coat over himself, ran out into the street just as he was, in his shirt...

The shop assistants at the butcher's, whom he had questioned the day before, had told him that letters are dropped into post boxes, and from the boxes they are carried all over the world on mail troikas with drunken drivers and ringing bells. Vanka ran to the first post box and pushed the precious letter into the slot...

## VI · The Dream

Lulled by sweet hopes, an hour later he was fast asleep... He dreamed of a stove. On the stove sat his grandfather, dangling his bare feet, and reading the letter to the cooks... Round the stove walked Eel, wagging his tail...
