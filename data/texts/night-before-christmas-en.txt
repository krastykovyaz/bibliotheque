## I · The Stolen Moon

The last day before Christmas had passed. A clear winter night had come. The stars looked out. The moon rose majestically into the sky to shed light on good people and on the whole world, so that everyone might have a merry time singing carols and praising Christ. The frost was sharper than in the morning, but it was so still that the creak of the frost under a boot could be heard half a mile away. Not one crowd of lads had yet appeared under the windows of the cottages; only the moon peeped into them, stealthily, as if calling the girls who were dressing up to run out quickly onto the creaking snow. Then smoke came tumbling in clouds out of the chimney of one cottage and spread like a storm cloud across the sky, and with the smoke a witch rose up, riding on a broom.

If at that moment the Sorochintsy assessor had been driving by in a troika of requisitioned horses, in a cap with a lambskin band cut in the lancer fashion, in a blue sheepskin coat lined with black astrakhan, with the devilishly plaited whip he is accustomed to use on his driver, he would certainly have noticed her, because no witch in the world can slip past the Sorochintsy assessor. He knows to the last piglet how many a woman's sow has farrowed, and how much linen lies in her chest, and exactly which of his clothes and belongings a good man will pawn at the tavern on Sunday. But the Sorochintsy assessor was not driving by, and why should he care about other people's affairs? He has his own district. Meanwhile the witch rose so high that she showed only as a little black speck up there. But wherever the speck appeared, the stars vanished from the sky, one after another. Soon the witch had gathered a whole sleeveful of them. Three or four were still shining. Suddenly, from the other side, another speck appeared, grew bigger, began to stretch out, and was a speck no longer. A short-sighted man, even if he put the wheels of a commissary's carriage on his nose instead of spectacles, would not have made out what it was. In front he was a perfect German: a narrow little muzzle, forever turning and sniffing at everything that came its way, ended, like our pigs', in a round little snout; his legs were so thin that if the Yareski headman had had such legs he would have broken them in the first Cossack dance. But behind he was a real provincial attorney in uniform, because a tail hung from him, as sharp and long as the tails of a uniform coat nowadays; only by the goat's beard under his muzzle, by the little horns sticking out of his head, and by being no whiter than a chimney sweep could one guess that he was neither a German nor a provincial attorney, but simply a devil, who had one last night left to wander the wide world and teach good people their sins. Tomorrow, at the first bells for morning service, he would run without looking back, tail between his legs, to his lair. Meanwhile the devil crept quietly up to the moon and had already stretched out his hand to seize it; but suddenly he snatched it back, as if burned, sucked his fingers, jigged about on one foot, ran round to the other side, and again jumped away and drew back his hand. Still, in spite of all his failures, the cunning devil did not give up his mischief. He ran up and suddenly grabbed the moon with both hands, and, grimacing and blowing, tossed it from one hand to the other, like a peasant who has picked up a live coal with his bare hands to light his pipe; at last he hurriedly hid it in his pocket and, as if nothing had happened, ran on. In Dikanka no one heard how the devil stole the moon. True, the district clerk, coming out of the tavern on all fours, saw the moon dancing in the sky for no reason at all, and swore to the whole village that it was so; but the villagers shook their heads and even laughed at him. And what was the reason that made the devil decide on such a lawless deed? This was the reason: he knew that the rich Cossack Chub had been invited by the deacon to the Christmas Eve supper of kutya, where the headman would be; and the deacon's kinsman, who had come from the bishop's choir, in a blue frock coat, singing the lowest bass; and the Cossack Svyerbyhuz and a few others; where, besides the kutya, there would be varenukha, vodka brewed with saffron, and plenty of other good things to eat. Meanwhile Chub's daughter, the beauty of the whole village, would stay at home, and to the daughter the blacksmith would surely come, a strong man and a fine fellow, whom the devil hated worse than the sermons of Father Kondrat. In his spare time from his work the blacksmith took up painting, and was known as the best painter in all the neighbourhood. Colonel L...ko himself, who was still alive then, sent for him specially to Poltava to paint the board fence round his house. All the bowls from which the Dikanka Cossacks ate their borscht had been painted by the blacksmith. The blacksmith was a God-fearing man and often painted icons of the saints, and even now you can find his Evangelist Luke in the church at T... But the triumph of his art was a picture painted on the church wall in the right-hand porch, in which he had shown Saint Peter on the Day of Judgement, with the keys in his hands, driving the evil spirit out of hell: the frightened devil threw himself in every direction, sensing his doom, while the sinners who had been shut up there before beat and drove him with whips, logs and anything that came to hand. While the painter was working on this picture, painting it on a large wooden board, the devil tried with all his might to hinder him: he jogged his arm unseen, lifted ashes from the forge's furnace and sprinkled them over the picture; but in spite of everything the work was finished, the board was carried into the church and set into the wall of the porch, and from that time the devil swore to take revenge on the blacksmith. He had only one night left to wander the world; but even on this night he was looking for some way to pay the blacksmith back for his spite. And for that he decided to steal the moon, hoping that old Chub was lazy and slow to get going, and that the deacon's house was not close to his cottage: the road ran behind the village, past the windmills, past the cemetery, and wound round a ravine. Even on a moonlit night the varenukha and the vodka brewed with saffron might have lured Chub; but in such darkness it would hardly have been possible for anyone to drag him off his stove and out of his cottage. And the blacksmith, who had long been on bad terms with him, would never dare, with Chub there, to go to the daughter, strong as he was. And so, as soon as the devil had hidden his moon in his pocket, it suddenly became so dark all over the world that not everyone could have found the way to the tavern, let alone to the deacon's. The witch, finding herself suddenly in the dark, cried out. Then the devil, sidling up like a little imp, took her by the arm and began whispering in her ear the very thing that is usually whispered to the whole female race. A strange arrangement we have in this world! Everything that lives in it tries to copy and mimic everything else. Once upon a time, in Mirgorod, only the judge and the mayor went about in winter in sheepskin coats covered with cloth, while all the petty officials wore plain bare ones; but now the assessor and the chamberlain have both had new fur coats made from Reshetilovka lambskin with cloth covers. The clerk and the district scribe, the year before last, bought blue Chinese cotton at sixty kopecks an arshin. The sexton has made himself nankeen trousers for summer and a waistcoat of striped cloth. In a word, everyone is climbing up in the world! When will these people stop being vain? One could wager that many will find it surprising to see the devil setting off down the same road. What is most annoying is that he surely imagines himself a handsome fellow, while his figure is a shame to look at. A face, as Foma Grigoryevich says, the ugliest of the ugly, and yet even he courts the ladies! But in the sky and under the sky it had become so dark that it was no longer possible to see what happened next between them.

“So you, friend, have not been to the deacon's new cottage yet?” said the Cossack Chub, coming out of the door of his cottage, to a lean, tall peasant in a short sheepskin coat, with an overgrown beard, which showed that for more than two weeks it had not been touched by the bit of scythe blade with which peasants usually shave for want of a razor. “There'll be a fine drinking party there now,” Chub went on, his face breaking into a grin. “If only we are not late.” With this Chub straightened his belt, which held his sheepskin coat tight, pulled his cap down more firmly, gripped in his hand his whip, the terror and scourge of pestering dogs, and then, glancing upward, stopped short. “What the devil! Look! Look, Panas!”

“What?” said his friend, and lifted his head, looking up too.

“What do you mean, what? There's no moon!”

“What the plague! There really is no moon.”

“That's just it, there isn't,” said Chub, with some annoyance at his friend's unfailing calm. “You, I dare say, don't care.”

“What am I to do about it!”

“It had to be some devil,” Chub went on, wiping his moustache with his sleeve, “may that dog never get a glass of vodka in the morning, to meddle in this! Really, as if to mock us. I sat in the cottage on purpose and looked out of the window: the night was a marvel! Bright; the snow shone in the moonlight. You could see everything as in the day. I had hardly stepped out of the door when, look, you can't see your hand before your face!” Chub went on grumbling and swearing for a long time, and meanwhile was wondering what to decide. He was dying to chat about all sorts of nonsense at the deacon's, where without a doubt the headman was already sitting, and the visiting bass, and Mykyta the tar-seller, who drove to Poltava every fortnight to the markets and let fly such jokes that all the villagers held their sides with laughter. Chub could already see in his mind the varenukha standing on the table. All this was tempting, it was true; but the darkness of the night reminded him of that laziness which is so dear to every Cossack. How good it would be now to lie with his legs tucked under him on the stove-bench, calmly smoking his pipe, and through a blissful drowsiness listen to the carols and songs of the merry lads and girls crowding in bunches under the windows. Without a doubt he would have decided on the latter if he had been alone; but now, with two of them, it was not so dull or frightening to go in the dark night, and he also did not want to appear lazy or cowardly in front of others. When he had finished his curses, he turned again to his friend.

“So there's no moon, friend?”

“No.”

“Strange, really. And give me a pinch of snuff! You've got famous snuff, friend! Where do you get it?”

“Famous, what the devil!” answered his friend, closing his birch snuffbox, pricked all over with patterns. “An old hen wouldn't sneeze at it!”

“I remember,” Chub went on in the same tone, “the late tavern-keeper Zuzulya once brought me tobacco from Nezhin. Ah, that was tobacco! Good tobacco! Well, friend, what shall we do? It's dark out.”

“Then perhaps we should stay at home,” said his friend, taking hold of the door handle.

If his friend had not said that, Chub would surely have decided to stay; but now something seemed to be pulling him to go against him. “No, friend, let's go! We can't not, we must go!” No sooner had he said this than he was annoyed with himself for saying it. It was most unpleasant to him to trudge out on such a night; but it consoled him that he had wanted it himself, on purpose, and had done as he pleased and not as he had been advised.

The friend, without showing the slightest trace of annoyance on his face, like a man to whom it is quite the same whether he sits at home or trudges out of it, looked about, scratched his shoulders with the stick of his whip, and the two friends set off.

## II · Oksana and Solokha

Now let us see what the beautiful daughter is doing, left alone. Oksana was not yet seventeen, and all over the world, one might say, on this side of Dikanka and on that, there was talk of nothing but her. The lads declared in chorus that there had never yet been a better girl in the village, and never would be. Oksana knew and heard everything that was said about her, and was capricious, as a beauty is. If she had gone about not in a plakhta and a zapaska but in some kind of fine town gown, she would have driven away all her girl friends. Lads chased after her in crowds, but, losing patience, they left her little by little and turned to others who were not so spoiled. Only the blacksmith was stubborn and did not give up his courting, although he too was treated no better than the rest. After her father had gone out, she went on dressing up and primping for a long time before a small mirror in a pewter frame, and could not admire herself enough. “What have people taken it into their heads to spread about, that I am pretty?” she said, as if absent-mindedly, only to have something to chatter about with herself. “People lie, I am not pretty at all.” But the fresh, lively face that flashed in the mirror, young as a child's, with shining black eyes and an inexpressibly pleasant smile that burned into the soul, suddenly proved the opposite. “Are my black brows and eyes,” the beauty went on, not letting go of the mirror, “so lovely that there is nothing like them in the world? What is good about this turned-up nose? And these cheeks? And these lips? Are my black braids lovely, then? Oh! They could frighten you in the evening: like long snakes, they are twisted and wound around my head. I see now that I am not pretty at all!” And, holding the mirror a little farther from her, she cried out: “No, I am pretty! Oh, how pretty! A wonder! What joy I shall bring to him whose wife I become! How my husband will admire me! He won't remember himself. He will kiss me to death!”

“A strange girl!” whispered the blacksmith, who had come in quietly. “And she isn't short of boasting! She has been standing an hour looking in the mirror, and cannot look enough, and even praises herself aloud!”

“Yes, lads, are you a match for me? Look at me,” the pretty coquette went on, “how smoothly I walk; my shift is sewn with red silk. And what ribbons on my head! You will never see richer braid! My father bought me all this so that the finest young man in the world would marry me!” And, smiling, she turned the other way, and saw the blacksmith...

She cried out and stopped sternly in front of him.

The blacksmith let his arms drop.

It is hard to say what the dark-skinned face of the wonderful girl expressed: there was sternness in it, and through the sternness some mockery of the confused blacksmith, and a barely noticeable flush of vexation spread delicately over her face; and all this was so mingled and so indescribably lovely that to kiss her a million times was the best thing anyone could have done then.

“Why have you come here?” Oksana began. “Do you want me to drive you out of the door with a shovel? You are all masters at getting round us. You sniff out in a moment when our fathers are not at home. Oh, I know you! Well, is my chest ready?”

“It will be ready, my little heart, it will be ready after the holiday. If you knew how much I have slaved over it: two nights I did not leave the forge; but not one priest's daughter will have such a chest. I put on it iron for the binding such as I did not put on the colonel's cart when I went to work in Poltava. And how it will be painted! Though you walk the whole district on your little white feet, you will not find one like it! Red and blue flowers will be scattered all over the field. It will burn like fire. Don't be angry with me! Let me at least talk, at least look at you!”

“Who forbids you? Talk and look!” She sat down on the bench and again looked in the mirror, and began to set her braids straight on her head. She looked at her neck, at her new shift embroidered with silk, and a delicate feeling of self-satisfaction showed on her lips, on her fresh cheeks, and shone in her eyes.

“Let me sit beside you, too!” said the blacksmith.

“Sit,” said Oksana, keeping the same feeling on her lips and in her contented eyes.

“Wonderful, dearest Oksana, let me kiss you!” said the emboldened blacksmith, and pressed her to him, meaning to snatch a kiss; but Oksana turned away her cheeks, which were already at an imperceptible distance from the blacksmith's lips, and pushed him off. “What more do you want? When he has honey, he needs a spoon too! Go away, your hands are harder than iron. And you smell of smoke yourself. I think you have smeared me all over with soot.” Then she held up the mirror and began to preen before it again.

“She doesn't love me!” the blacksmith thought to himself, hanging his head. “To her it is all a game; and I stand before her like a fool and can't take my eyes off her. And I would go on standing before her, and never take my eyes off her! A wonderful girl! What would I not give to know what is in her heart, whom she loves. But no, she does not care about anyone. She admires herself; she torments me, poor man; and for sorrow I can't see the light; and I love her as no man in the world has loved, or ever will love.”

“Is it true that your mother is a witch?” said Oksana, and laughed; and the blacksmith felt that everything inside him laughed. That laughter seemed at once to echo in his heart and in his gently trembling veins, and yet vexation sank into his soul that it was not in his power to kiss that face which had laughed so sweetly.

“What do I care about my mother? You are mother to me, and father, and everything that is dear in the world. If the tsar called me and said: Blacksmith Vakula, ask of me whatever is best in my kingdom, and I will give it to you. I shall order a golden forge to be made for you, and you will forge with silver hammers. I don't want, I would say to the tsar, neither precious stones, nor a golden forge, nor all your kingdom: give me rather my Oksana!”

“See what you are! But my father is no fool himself. You will see whether he marries your mother!” said Oksana, smiling slyly. “But the girls are not coming... What can it mean? It is high time to go carolling. I am getting bored.”

“God be with them, my beauty!”

“Not likely! The lads will surely come with them. Then the fun begins. I can imagine what funny stories they will tell!”

“So you have a good time with them?”

“Yes, better than with you. Ah! Someone knocked; it must be the girls with the lads.”

“What more is there to wait for?” the blacksmith said to himself. “She is mocking me. To her I am as dear as a rusted horseshoe. But if so, then at least no one else will get to laugh at me. Let me only see for certain who pleases her more than I do; I will cure him...”

A knock at the door and a voice ringing sharply in the frost, “Open!”, interrupted his thoughts.

“Wait, I will open it myself,” said the blacksmith, and went out into the passage, meaning in his vexation to break the ribs of the first man he met.

The frost grew stronger, and up there it became so cold that the devil hopped from one little hoof to the other and blew into his fist, trying to warm his freezing hands a little. No wonder, though, that he should freeze, who had been knocking about from morning to morning in hell, where, as is well known, it is not so cold as with us in winter, and where, putting on a cook's cap and standing before the hearth like a real chef, he roasted sinners with the pleasure with which a housewife usually fries sausage at Christmas. The witch herself felt it was cold, although she was warmly dressed; and so, raising her arms, thrusting out one leg, and putting herself in the position of a man flying on skates, without moving a single joint she slid down through the air as down a slippery icy slope, straight into the chimney. The devil followed her in the same manner. But since this creature is nimbler than any dandy in stockings, it is no wonder that at the very entrance to the chimney he ran into his sweetheart's neck, and both found themselves in the roomy stove among the pots. The traveller quietly pushed back the stove door to look and see whether her son Vakula had invited guests into the cottage, but seeing that there was no one, except the sacks lying in the middle of the room, she climbed out of the stove, threw off her warm sheepskin, tidied herself, and no one could have known that a minute before she had been riding a broom. The mother of the blacksmith Vakula was no more than forty years old. She was neither pretty nor plain. It is hard to be pretty at such an age. Yet she knew how to charm the most respectable Cossacks (who, it does no harm to remark, cared little for beauty), so that the headman used to visit her, and the deacon Osip Nikiforovich (of course, if the deacon's wife was not at home), and the Cossack Korniy Chub, and the Cossack Kasyan Svyerbyhuz. And, to her credit, she knew how to deal with them skilfully. It never entered the head of any of them that he had a rival. Whether a pious peasant, or a gentleman, as the Cossacks call themselves, dressed in a cloak with a hood, was going on Sunday to church, or, if the weather was bad, to the tavern, how could he not drop in on Solokha, eat the rich dumplings with sour cream, and chat in a warm cottage with the talkative, obliging hostess? And the gentleman would make a big detour on purpose before reaching the tavern, and call it dropping in on the way. And when Solokha went to church on a holiday, in a bright plakhta with a Chinese-cotton zapaska and over it a blue skirt with gold whiskers sewn on the back, and stood right by the right-hand choir, the deacon would surely begin to cough and involuntarily screw up his eyes in that direction; the headman would stroke his moustache, wind his forelock round his ear and say to the neighbour standing near him: “Ah, a fine woman! A devil of a woman!” Solokha bowed to each of them, and each thought she bowed to him alone. But anyone fond of meddling in other people's business would have noticed at once that Solokha was friendliest of all to the Cossack Chub. Chub was a widower; eight stacks of grain always stood before his cottage. Two pairs of sturdy oxen always thrust their heads out of the wattled shed into the street and lowed when they saw the cow, their friend, passing, or Uncle, the fat bull. A bearded goat climbed right up on the roof and bleated from there in a harsh voice, like a mayor, teasing the turkeys that strutted about the yard, and turning his back when he saw his enemies, the boys who made fun of his beard. In Chub's chests there was plenty of linen, coats, and old-fashioned kuntushes with gold braid: his late wife had been a fashionable woman. In the vegetable garden, besides poppies, cabbages and sunflowers, two fields of tobacco were sown every year. All this Solokha thought it no bad thing to add to her own household, considering beforehand what order it would take when it passed into her hands, and she doubled her kindness to old Chub. And so that her son Vakula might not somehow get at his daughter and manage to take everything himself, in which case he would certainly not let her meddle in anything, she resorted to the usual means of all forty-year-old gossips: to set Chub and the blacksmith at odds as often as possible. Perhaps it was these very tricks and her shrewdness that were the cause of old women beginning here and there to say, especially when they had drunk a drop too much at some merry gathering, that Solokha really was a witch; that the lad Kizyakolupenko had seen a tail on her behind no bigger than a woman's spindle; that only the Thursday before last she had run across the road as a black cat; that once a pig had run to the priest's wife, crowed like a cock, put Father Kondrat's hat on its head and run back. It happened that while the old women were discussing this, the cowherd Tymish Korostyavy came along. He did not fail to tell how in summer, just before Saint Peter's day, when he had lain down to sleep in the cattle shed with straw under his head, he had seen with his own eyes a witch with her hair loose, in her shift alone, begin to milk the cows, and he could not stir, so bewitched was he; when she had milked the cows, she came to him and smeared his lips with something so foul that he spat for a whole day afterwards. But all this is somewhat doubtful, because only the Sorochintsy assessor can see a witch. And so all the distinguished Cossacks waved their hands when they heard such talk. “They are lying, the bitches!” was their usual answer.

When she had climbed out of the stove and tidied herself, Solokha, like a good housewife, began to clear up and put everything in its place; but she did not touch the sacks: Vakula had brought them, so let him carry them out himself! The devil meanwhile, as he flew into the chimney, happened to turn round and saw Chub arm in arm with his friend, already far from the cottage. In a moment he flew out of the stove, ran across their path, and began to tear up the heaps of frozen snow on all sides. A blizzard rose. The air turned white. The snow whirled back and forth like a net and threatened to plaster the eyes, mouth and ears of the walkers. And the devil flew back into the chimney, firmly convinced that Chub would turn back with his friend, find the blacksmith, and give him such a welcome that for a long time he would be unable to take a brush in his hand and paint insulting caricatures.

## III · The Blizzard

In fact, no sooner had the blizzard risen and the wind begun to cut straight into his eyes than Chub declared his regret and, pulling his cap lower on his head, treated himself, the devil and his friend to curses. However, this annoyance was feigned. Chub was very glad of the blizzard. To the deacon's there was still eight times as much distance as they had covered. The travellers turned back. The wind blew on the back of their heads; but through the sweeping snow nothing could be seen.

“Stop, friend! I think we are not going the right way,” said Chub, after going a little distance. “I don't see a single cottage. Ugh, what a blizzard! Turn a little to the side, friend, perhaps you will find the road; and in the meantime I will look here. The unclean spirit must have driven us to trudge about in such a storm! Don't forget to shout when you find the road. Ugh, what a heap of snow Satan has thrown in my eyes!”

The road, however, was nowhere to be seen. The friend, moving off to the side, wandered back and forth in his long boots and at last came straight upon the tavern. This find so delighted him that he forgot everything and, shaking off the snow, went into the porch, not troubling himself in the least about his friend left in the street. To Chub meanwhile it seemed that he had found the road; stopping, he began to shout at the top of his voice, but seeing that his friend did not appear, he decided to go on alone. After going a little way he saw his cottage. Drifts of snow lay around it and on the roof. Clapping his hands, numb with cold, he began to knock at the door and shout, in a commanding voice, to his daughter to open it.

“What do you want here?” shouted the blacksmith, coming out, sternly.

Chub, recognising the blacksmith's voice, stepped back a little. “Ah, no, this is not my cottage,” he said to himself, “the blacksmith would not stray into my cottage. And then, if one looks carefully, it is not the blacksmith's either. Whose cottage could it be? There now! I didn't recognise it! This is lame Levchenko's, who has lately married a young wife. His is the only cottage like mine. That is why it seemed a little strange to me at first that I had come home so quickly. But Levchenko is sitting at the deacon's now, I know that; so why is the blacksmith here? Aha, ha, ha! He is visiting his young wife. So that's how it is! Good! Now I understand it all.”

“Who are you, and why are you hanging about at the door?” said the blacksmith, more sternly than before, coming closer.

“No, I won't tell him who I am,” thought Chub. “Heaven knows, the accursed brute may even thrash me!” And, changing his voice, he answered: “It is I, a good man, who have come to sing carols a little under your windows, for your amusement.”

“Go to the devil with your carols!” shouted Vakula angrily. “Why are you standing there? Do you hear, get out of here this minute!”

Chub himself already had this sensible intention; but it seemed annoying to him to be forced to obey the blacksmith's orders. It seemed that some evil spirit jogged his arm and forced him to say something contrary. “Why are you really shouting so?” he said in the same voice. “I want to sing carols, and that's all.”

“Aha! You won't stop at words!” With these words Chub felt a very painful blow on the shoulder.

“So you are beginning to fight, I see!” he said, backing away a little.

“Go, go!” shouted the blacksmith, rewarding Chub with another shove.

“What are you doing!” said Chub, in a voice that expressed pain, and annoyance, and timidity. “You are not fighting in jest, I see, and you hit hard, too!”

“Go, go!” shouted the blacksmith and slammed the door.

“Look how brave he has become!” said Chub, left alone in the street. “Just try to come near! Look at him! What a great fine fellow! You think I won't find justice against you. No, my dear, I will go, and go straight to the commissary. You will learn from me. I won't look at your being a blacksmith and a painter. But let me look at my back and shoulders: I think there are blue marks. He must have beaten me hard, the enemy's son! A pity it is cold and I don't want to take off my sheepskin! Just wait, you devilish blacksmith, may the devil beat you and your forge, you will dance for this! Look at the accursed rascal! But then, he is not at home now. Solokha, I think, is sitting alone. Hm... it is not far from here; I might go! It is the sort of time that no one will catch us. Perhaps it will even be possible to... look how hard he beat me, the accursed blacksmith!”

Here Chub, scratching his back, set off in another direction. The pleasure awaiting him ahead in his meeting with Solokha eased the pain a little and made him insensible even to the frost, which crackled in all the streets, unmuffled by the whistling of the blizzard. From time to time on his face, whose beard and moustache the storm had lathered with snow more quickly than any barber who tyrannically seizes his victim by the nose, a half-sweet expression appeared. But if the snow had not been crossing and recrossing everything before his eyes, one could have seen for a long time how Chub stopped, scratched his back, said, “He beat me hard, the accursed blacksmith!” and set off on his way again.

At the time when the nimble dandy with the tail and goat's beard was flying out of the chimney and then back into the chimney, the pouch that hung at his side on a strap, into which he had hidden the stolen moon, somehow happened to catch in the stove and came open, and the moon, taking advantage of the chance, flew out through the chimney of Solokha's cottage and rose smoothly into the sky. Everything was lit up. The blizzard was gone as if it had never been. The snow blazed into a wide silver field and was all sprinkled with crystal stars. The frost seemed to grow warmer. Crowds of lads and girls appeared with sacks. Songs rang out, and there was scarcely a cottage under whose windows carollers were not crowding. How wonderfully the moon shines! It is hard to tell how good it is to knock about, on such a night, among a crowd of laughing, singing girls and among lads ready for any joke and invention that a merrily laughing night can inspire. Under a thick sheepskin it is warm; the frost makes the cheeks burn even brighter; and for mischief the sly one himself pushes you from behind. Crowds of girls with sacks burst into Chub's cottage and surrounded Oksana. Shouts, laughter, stories deafened the blacksmith. They all vied in hurrying to tell the beauty something new, unloaded their sacks and boasted of the loaves, sausages and dumplings which they had already managed to collect in plenty for their carols. Oksana seemed to be in perfect pleasure and joy, chattered now with one, now with another, and laughed without stopping. With a sort of vexation and envy the blacksmith looked at such merriment and, this time, cursed the carols, although he himself was usually mad about them.

“Ah, Odarka!” said the merry beauty, turning to one of the girls. “You have new shoes! Oh, how pretty! And with gold! It is good for you, Odarka, you have a man who buys you everything; and I have no one to get such lovely shoes for me.”

“Don't grieve, my dearest Oksana!” the blacksmith broke in. “I will get you such shoes as few young ladies wear.”

“You?” said Oksana, glancing quickly and haughtily at him. “I shall see where you will get shoes that I could put on my foot. Unless you bring me the very ones the Empress wears.”

“Look what she has asked for!” cried the crowd of girls, laughing.

“Yes!” the beauty went on proudly. “Be witnesses, all of you: if the blacksmith Vakula brings me the very shoes the Empress wears, then here is my word that I will marry him that very hour.”

The girls led the capricious beauty away with them.

“Laugh, laugh!” said the blacksmith, going out after them. “I laugh at myself! I think and cannot think where my wits have gone. She does not love me, well, God be with her! As if there were only one Oksana in all the world. Thank God, there are plenty of good girls in the village without her. And what is Oksana? She will never make a good housewife; she is only a master at dressing up. No, enough, it is time to stop playing the fool.” But at the very moment when the blacksmith was preparing to be resolute, some evil spirit carried before him the laughing image of Oksana, saying mockingly: “Get me the Empress's shoes, blacksmith, and I will marry you!” Everything in him was stirred, and he thought of Oksana alone.

Crowds of carollers, the lads by themselves, the girls by themselves, hurried from one street to another. But the blacksmith walked and saw nothing and took no part in the merriment that he had once loved more than anyone.

## IV · Solokha's Visitors

The devil meanwhile had grown seriously tender at Solokha's: he kissed her hand with the grimaces of an assessor before a priest's daughter, clutched his heart, groaned, and said plainly that if she would not agree to satisfy his passion and reward him, as is the custom, he was ready for anything, would throw himself into the water, and send his soul straight to the pit. Solokha was not so cruel; besides, the devil, as is well known, was working on her side. She did like to see a crowd dragging after her and was seldom without company; this evening, however, she had thought to spend alone, because all the distinguished inhabitants of the village had been invited to the deacon's for kutya. But everything went otherwise: the devil had barely put forward his demand when suddenly the voice of the stout Headman was heard. Solokha ran to open the door, and the nimble devil climbed into a sack that was lying there. The Headman, shaking the snow from his cap and drinking a glass of vodka from Solokha's hand, told her that he had not gone to the deacon's because a blizzard had risen; and seeing a light in her cottage, he had turned in with the intention of spending the evening with her. He had hardly said this when there was a knock at the door and the deacon's voice. “Hide me somewhere,” whispered the Headman. “I don't want to meet the deacon now.” Solokha thought a long time where to hide such a solid guest; at last she chose the biggest sack of coal; she poured the coal into a tub, and the stout Headman climbed into the sack, with his moustache, his head and his cap.

The deacon came in, coughing and rubbing his hands, and told her that no one had come to his house, and that he was heartily glad of this chance to spend a little time at her place, and had not been afraid of the blizzard. Then he came closer to her, coughed, smiled, touched her bare, plump arm with his long fingers, and said, with a look that showed both slyness and self-satisfaction: “And what is this you have here, magnificent Solokha?” And having said this, he jumped back a little.

“What do you mean, what? An arm, Osip Nikiforovich!” answered Solokha.

“Hm! An arm! Heh, heh, heh!” said the deacon, heartily pleased with his beginning, and walked about the room.

“And what is this you have here, most precious Solokha?” he said, with the same look, approaching her again and lightly seizing her neck with his hand, and in the same manner jumping back.

“As if you can't see, Osip Nikiforovich!” answered Solokha. “A neck, and on the neck a string of beads.”

“Hm! On the neck a string of beads! Heh, heh, heh!” And the deacon again walked about the room, rubbing his hands.

“And what is this you have here, incomparable Solokha?” It is not known what the deacon would now have touched with his long fingers, when suddenly a knock came at the door and the voice of the Cossack Chub.

“Ah, my God, an outsider!” cried the deacon in fright. “What if they find a person of my calling here? It will reach Father Kondrat!” But the deacon's fears were of another kind: he was more afraid that his better half would find out, who with her terrible hand had already turned his thick pigtail into the very thinnest. “For God's sake, virtuous Solokha,” he said, trembling all over, “your kindness, as the Gospel of Luke says, chapter thir... thir... They are knocking, by God, knocking! Oh, hide me somewhere!”

Solokha poured the coal from another sack into the tub, and the deacon, not too bulky in body, climbed into it and sat right at the bottom, so that another half sack of coal could have been poured on top of him.

“Good evening, Solokha!” said Chub, coming into the cottage. “Perhaps you did not expect me, eh? True, you did not expect me? Perhaps I have interrupted you...” Chub went on, putting on his face a merry and significant look, which gave notice beforehand that his slow head had been working and was preparing to deliver some sharp and clever joke. “Perhaps you were amusing yourselves here with someone! Perhaps you have already hidden somebody, eh?” And, delighted with this remark of his, Chub laughed, inwardly triumphing that he alone enjoyed Solokha's favour. “Well, Solokha, give me some vodka now. I think my throat has frozen from the accursed frost. God sent such a night before Christmas! How it took hold, you hear, Solokha, how it took hold... ugh, how my hands have gone stiff: I can't unbutton my sheepskin! How the blizzard took hold...”

“Open!” a voice rang out in the street, accompanied by a thump on the door.

“Somebody is knocking?” said Chub, stopping short.

“Open!” they shouted, louder than before.

“It is the blacksmith!” said Chub, clutching his cap. “Listen, Solokha, put me wherever you like; for nothing in the world will I show myself to that accursed brute, may a blister as big as a haystack rise under each of his eyes, the devil's son!” Solokha, frightened herself, rushed about like one in a daze and, forgetting herself, signed to Chub to climb into the very sack in which the deacon was already sitting. The poor deacon did not dare even to show his pain by a cough or a groan when the heavy peasant sat almost on his head and placed his boots, frozen from the frost, on both sides of his temples.

The blacksmith came in without saying a word, without taking off his cap, and almost fell onto the bench. It was plain that he was in a very bad mood. Just as Solokha was shutting the door behind him, someone knocked again. It was the Cossack Svyerbyhuz. This one could no longer be hidden in a sack, because no such sack could be found. He was heavier in body than the Headman himself, and taller than Chub's friend. And so Solokha led him out into the vegetable garden, to hear from him everything he wanted to tell her.

The blacksmith looked absently into the corners of his cottage, listening from time to time to the songs of the carollers carrying far off; at last he rested his eyes on the sacks. “Why are these sacks lying here? They should have been taken away long ago. Because of this stupid love I have gone quite silly. Tomorrow is a holiday, and all kinds of rubbish are still lying in the cottage. Take them to the forge!” With that the blacksmith bent down to the huge sacks, tied them tighter and prepared to heave them onto his shoulders. But it was plain that his thoughts were wandering God knows where, or else he would have heard how Chub hissed when the rope tying the sack twisted the hair on his head, and the stout Headman began to hiccup quite audibly. “Will that wretched Oksana never get out of my mind?” said the blacksmith. “I don't want to think of her; yet I keep thinking, and as if on purpose, of her alone. Why is it that a thought forces itself into the head against one's will? What the devil, the sacks seem to have grown heavier than before! Something besides coal must have been put in here. Fool that I am! I forgot that now everything seems heavier to me. Once upon a time I could bend and straighten a copper coin and a horseshoe in one hand; and now I can't lift sacks of coal. Soon I shall be falling over in the wind. No,” he cried, after a pause, taking heart, “what sort of woman am I! I won't let anyone laugh at me. Even ten such sacks, I will lift them all.” And he briskly heaved onto his shoulders the sacks that two stout men could not have carried. “Take this one too,” he went on, lifting the little one, at the bottom of which the devil lay curled up. “I think I put my tools in here.” Saying this, he went out of the cottage, whistling a song:

“I'll have no bother with a wife.”

## V · The Devil's Bargain

Songs and shouts rang out ever louder in the streets. The crowds of jostling people were swelled still more by those who came from the neighbouring villages. The lads played pranks and ran wild to their heart's content. Often among the carols one heard some merry song which one of the young Cossacks had just made up on the spot. Then suddenly one of the crowd, instead of a carol, would let fly a shchedrovka and roar at the top of his voice:

“Shchedryk, vedryk! Give us a dumpling! A little lump of porridge, a ring of sausage!”

Laughter rewarded the joker. Little windows were raised, and the lean hand of an old woman (they alone, with the staid fathers, stayed in the cottages) was thrust out of the window with a sausage in it or a piece of pie. The lads and girls vied in holding out their sacks and catching their booty. In one place the lads, coming up from all sides, surrounded a crowd of girls: noise, shouting, one threw a snowball, another snatched a sack full of all sorts of things. In another place the girls caught a lad, tripped him, and he flew headlong to the ground together with his sack. It seemed they were ready to make merry all night long. And the night, as if on purpose, glowed so luxuriously! and the light of the moon seemed still whiter from the glitter of the snow. The blacksmith stopped with his sacks. In the crowd of girls he fancied he heard Oksana's voice and thin laughter. Every little vein in him trembled; throwing the sacks on the ground, so that the deacon at the bottom groaned from the bump and the Headman hiccuped at the top of his voice, he trudged with the small sack on his shoulders after the crowd of lads who were following the girls' crowd, among whom he had heard Oksana's voice.

Yes: it is she! She stands like a queen, and her black eyes flash! A fine lad is telling her something; surely something funny, because she is laughing. But she is always laughing. As if involuntarily, not understanding himself how, the blacksmith pushed through the crowd and stood beside her. “Ah, Vakula, you are here! Good evening!” said the beauty with the very same smile that nearly drove Vakula out of his mind. “Well, have you collected much? Oh, what a small sack! And the shoes the Empress wears, have you got them? Get me the shoes and I will marry you!” And, laughing, she ran off with the crowd.

The blacksmith stood rooted to the spot. “No, I can't; I have no more strength...” he said at last. “But, my God, why is she so devilishly beautiful? Her look, and her words, and everything, it just burns and burns... No, I cannot master myself any longer! It is time to put an end to everything: let my soul perish, I will go and drown myself in the ice-hole, and goodbye!” Then with a resolute step he went forward, overtook the crowd, drew level with Oksana, and said in a firm voice: “Farewell, Oksana! Find yourself whatever husband you like, fool whom you like; but you will not see me any more in this world.” The beauty seemed surprised, wanted to say something, but the blacksmith waved his hand and ran off.

“Where to, Vakula?” shouted the lads, seeing the blacksmith run. “Farewell, brothers!” shouted the blacksmith in reply. “God willing, we shall meet in the next world; but in this one we shall not make merry together any more. Farewell, do not think ill of me! Tell Father Kondrat to say a requiem for my sinful soul. I have not painted the candles for the icons of the Wonder-Worker and the Mother of God, I am sinful, being busy with worldly affairs. All the goods that are found in my chest, for the church! Farewell!” Having said this, the blacksmith began to run again with the sack on his back. “He has gone out of his mind!” said the lads. “A lost soul!” an old woman passing by muttered piously. “I will go and tell how the blacksmith hanged himself!”

Vakula meanwhile, having run several streets, stopped to catch his breath. “Where am I really running to?” he thought. “As if everything were already lost. I will try one more way: I will go to the Zaporozhian, Pot-bellied Patsyuk. They say he knows all the devils and will do whatever he wants. I will go, for my soul will have to be lost anyway!” At this the devil, who had lain a long time without any movement, began to jump for joy in the sack; but the blacksmith, thinking that he had somehow caught the sack with his hand and made the movement himself, struck the sack with his stout fist and, shaking it on his shoulders, set off to Pot-bellied Patsyuk.

This Pot-bellied Patsyuk had indeed once been a Zaporozhian; but whether they had driven him out or he had himself run away from the Zaporozhian Host, no one knew. It was long since, ten years, perhaps fifteen, that he had lived in Dikanka. At first he lived like a true Zaporozhian: he did no work, slept three quarters of the day, ate for six mowers, and drank almost a whole pail at one time; though there was room for it, because Patsyuk, for all his small height, was quite hefty in breadth. Besides, the trousers he wore were so wide that however big a step he took, his legs could not be seen at all, and it seemed as if a distillery vat were moving down the street. Perhaps this very thing gave rise to his nickname, Pot-bellied. Only a few days had passed after his arrival in the village when everyone already knew that he was a healer. If anyone was ill with anything, they at once called Patsyuk; and all Patsyuk had to do was whisper a few words, and the ailment was removed as if by hand. If it happened that a hungry gentleman choked on a fishbone, Patsyuk knew how to strike him on the back with his fist so skilfully that the bone went where it ought, causing no harm to the gentleman's throat. Lately he was rarely seen anywhere. The reason for this was perhaps laziness, perhaps also that squeezing through doors became harder for him every year. Then the villagers had to go to him themselves if they had need of him. The blacksmith, not without timidity, opened the door and saw Patsyuk sitting on the floor, Turkish fashion, in front of a small tub on which stood a bowl of dumplings. This bowl stood, as if on purpose, level with his mouth. Without moving a single finger he bent his head slightly to the bowl and slurped the broth, catching the dumplings now and then with his teeth. “No, this one,” thought Vakula to himself, “is even lazier than Chub: Chub at least eats with a spoon; and this one won't even lift a hand!” Patsyuk was surely deeply occupied with the dumplings, for he seemed not to have noticed the blacksmith's arrival at all, and the blacksmith, hardly setting foot on the threshold, gave him a very low bow.

“I have come to your honour, Patsyuk!” said Vakula, bowing again. The fat Patsyuk raised his head and began again to slurp his dumplings.

“They say, no offence meant,” said the blacksmith, gathering his courage, “I say this not to cause you any insult, that you are somewhat related to the devil.”

Having said these words, Vakula was frightened, thinking that he had still spoken too plainly and had softened the strong words too little, and expecting that Patsyuk, seizing the tub together with the bowl, would send it straight at his head, he drew back a little and covered himself with his sleeve so that the hot broth from the dumplings would not splash his face.

But Patsyuk glanced at him and began again to slurp his dumplings.

Encouraged, the blacksmith decided to go on: “I have come to you, Patsyuk, may God give you everything, all good things in plenty, bread in proportion!” The blacksmith sometimes knew how to slip in a fashionable word; he had become practised in that while still in Poltava, when he was painting the colonel's board fence. “I am to be lost, sinner that I am! Nothing helps in the world! Come what may, I must ask for help from the devil himself. Well, Patsyuk?” said the blacksmith, seeing his unchanging silence. “What am I to do?”

“When you need the devil, go to the devil!” answered Patsyuk, not raising his eyes to him and going on clearing away the dumplings.

“That is why I have come to you,” answered the blacksmith, making a bow, “I think no one in the world but you knows the way to him.”

Patsyuk said not a word, and finished the last dumplings. “Do me the kindness, good man, do not refuse!” the blacksmith pressed on. “Pork, or sausages, buckwheat flour, well, linen, millet, or anything else in case of need... as is usual among good people... we will not be stingy; only tell me how, let us say, to get on the road to him?”

“He need not go far who has the devil at his back,” said Patsyuk indifferently, without changing his position.

Vakula fixed his eyes on him as if the explanation of these words were written on his forehead. What is he saying? his look asked silently; and his half-open mouth was ready to swallow the first word like a dumpling. But Patsyuk was silent. Then Vakula noticed that neither dumplings nor tub were before him; instead there stood on the floor two wooden bowls: one was filled with vareniki, the other with sour cream. His thoughts and eyes turned involuntarily to these dishes. “Let us see,” he said to himself, “how Patsyuk will eat the vareniki. He surely will not want to bend down to slurp them as he did the dumplings, and it is not possible: a varenik must first be dipped in the sour cream.” He had hardly had time to think this when Patsyuk opened his mouth, looked at the vareniki, and opened his mouth wider. At that moment a varenik splashed out of the bowl, plopped into the sour cream, turned over on the other side, leaped up, and landed right in his mouth. Patsyuk ate it and opened his mouth again, and a varenik went the same way again. He took upon himself only the work of chewing and swallowing. “Look, what a marvel!” thought the blacksmith, opening his mouth in amazement, and at that very moment noticed that a varenik was climbing into his mouth too and had already smeared his lips with sour cream. Pushing the varenik away and wiping his lips, the blacksmith began to reflect on what wonders there are in the world and what wisdom the unclean spirit can lead a man to, noting at the same time that only Patsyuk could help him. “I will bow to him once more, let him explain it properly... But what the devil! Today is the fasting kutya; and he is eating vareniki, meat-day vareniki! What sort of fool am I, really: standing here and gathering sin! Back!” And the pious blacksmith ran headlong out of the cottage.

But the devil, who sat in the sack and had been rejoicing beforehand, could not bear that such a fine prey should escape his hands. As soon as the blacksmith put down the sack, he jumped out of it and sat astride his neck.

A chill ran over the blacksmith's skin; frightened and pale, he did not know what to do, and already wanted to cross himself... But the devil, bending his doggy little snout to his right ear, said: “It is I, your friend; I will do everything for a comrade and friend! I will give you as much money as you like,” he squeaked into his left ear. “Oksana will be ours today,” he whispered, turning his muzzle again to the right ear. The blacksmith stood thinking.

“Very well,” he said at last, “for such a price I am ready to be yours!”

The devil clapped his hands and began to gallop with joy on the blacksmith's neck. “Now the blacksmith is caught!” he thought to himself. “Now I will pay you back, my dear, for all your paintings and the tall tales told about devils! What will my comrades say now when they learn that the most pious man in the whole village is in my hands?” Here the devil laughed for joy, remembering how he would tease the whole tailed tribe in hell, how the lame devil, considered the first among them for inventions, would rage.

“Well, Vakula!” squeaked the devil, still not getting down from his neck, as if afraid that he would run away. “You know that nothing is done without a contract.”

“I am ready!” said the blacksmith. “I hear that with you they sign in blood; wait, I will get a nail out of my pocket!” Here he put his hand behind him, and grab! he had the devil by the tail.

“Look what a joker!” cried the devil, laughing. “Well, enough, enough of this mischief!”

“Wait, my dear!” cried the blacksmith. “And how does this strike you?” At these words he made the sign of the cross, and the devil became as quiet as a lamb. “Wait, then,” he said, dragging him down to the ground by the tail, “you will learn from me to prompt good people and honest Christians to sin.” Then the blacksmith, without letting go of the tail, leaped astride him and raised his hand for the sign of the cross.

“Have mercy, Vakula!” groaned the devil pitifully. “Everything you need, I will do; only let my soul go to repentance: do not lay the terrible cross on me!”

“Ah, so that is the voice you sing in, accursed German! Now I know what to do. Carry me on yourself this very instant! Do you hear, carry me like a bird!”

“Where?” said the sorrowful devil.

“To Petersburg, straight to the Empress!” And the blacksmith went numb with fear, feeling himself rise into the air.

## VI · The Sacks

Oksana stood a long time, pondering the blacksmith's strange words. Already something inside her told her that she had treated him too cruelly. “What if he really decides on something terrible? Heaven knows! Perhaps in his grief he will take it into his head to fall in love with another and out of spite will call her the first beauty in the village? But no, he loves me. I am so pretty! He will not exchange me for anything; he is playing, pretending. Not ten minutes will pass before he surely comes to look at me. I really am harsh. I must let him kiss me, as if unwillingly. How glad he will be!” And the flighty beauty was already joking with her friends. “Wait,” said one of them, “the blacksmith has forgotten his sacks; look: what terrible sacks! He did not carol as we do: I think they have thrown a whole quarter of a ram into them; and the sausages and loaves surely cannot be counted. Luxury! You could gorge on them for whole holidays.”

“Are these the blacksmith's sacks?” Oksana caught up. “Let us drag them quickly to my cottage and look properly at what he has packed in here.” All approved this proposal with laughter.

“But we can't lift them!” the whole crowd cried at once, trying to move the sacks.

“Wait,” said Oksana, “let us run quickly for a sledge and take them on the sledge!”

And the crowd ran for a sledge.

The prisoners were greatly tired of sitting in the sacks, although the deacon had poked a fair hole for himself with his finger. If there had been no people about, he might have found a means of climbing out; but to climb out of the sack before everyone, to show himself to be laughed at... this held him back, and he decided to wait, only coughing a little under Chub's impolite boots. Chub himself wished no less for freedom, feeling that under him lay something on which it was terribly uncomfortable to sit. But as soon as he heard his daughter's decision he calmed down and no longer wanted to climb out, reasoning that to reach his cottage he had to go at least a hundred paces, perhaps two hundred. And once out, he would have to tidy himself, button his sheepskin, tie his belt: so much work! and besides, his cap had been left at Solokha's. Better let the girls bring him on the sledge. But it turned out quite differently from what Chub expected: at the time the girls ran for the sledge, the lean friend was coming out of the tavern, upset and out of sorts. The tavern-keeper would not on any account trust him with credit; he had meant to wait in case some pious gentleman came and treated him; but, as if on purpose, all the gentlemen stayed at home and, as honest Christians, were eating kutya in the midst of their families. Reflecting on the corruption of manners and on the wooden heart of the Jewess who sold wine, the friend came upon the sacks and stopped in astonishment. “Look what sacks someone has thrown down in the road!” he said, looking about on all sides. “There must be pork here, too. Somebody was lucky to collect so many things by carolling. What terrible sacks! Suppose they are stuffed only with buckwheat cakes and flat cakes, even that is good. Even if there were only loaves here, that would be tasty: the Jewess gives a glass of vodka for each loaf. Drag them off quickly, so that no one sees.” Then he heaved onto his shoulders the sack with Chub and the deacon, but felt that it was too heavy. “No, it will be hard to carry alone,” he said, “but here, as if on purpose, comes the weaver Shapuvalenko. Good evening, Ostap!”

“Good evening,” said the weaver, stopping.

“Where are you going?”

“Oh, just walking where my feet take me.”

“Help me carry the sacks, good man! Someone was carolling and threw them down in the middle of the road. We will share them in friendship, half and half.”

“Sacks? And what is in the sacks, rolls or loaves?”

“I think there is everything.” Then they quickly pulled some sticks out of a wattle fence, laid the sack on them, and carried it on their shoulders.

“Where shall we carry it? To the tavern?” asked the weaver on the way.

“I thought so myself, to the tavern; but the accursed Jewess will not believe it, she will even think we stole it somewhere; besides, I have only just come from the tavern. We will carry it to my cottage. No one will disturb us: my wife is not at home.”

“But is she really not at home?” asked the careful weaver.

“Thank God, we are not quite out of our wits yet,” said the friend. “Would the devil bring me where she is? She will drag about with the women till daylight, I think.”

“Who is there?” cried the friend's wife, hearing the noise in the porch made by the arrival of the two companions with the sack, and opening the door.

The friend stood rooted to the spot.

“Well, there you are!” said the weaver, letting his arms fall.

The friend's wife was the sort of treasure of which there are not a few in the world. Like her husband she almost never sat at home and crawled about almost the whole day among her gossips and well-to-do old women, praising and eating with great appetite, and fought only in the mornings with her husband, because only at that time did she sometimes see him. Their cottage was twice as old as the district clerk's trousers: in some places the roof had no thatch. Of the fence only remnants were visible, because everyone who left the house never took a stick for the dogs, in the hope that he would pass the friend's vegetable garden and pull one out of his fence. The stove was not lit for days at a time. Everything the tender wife begged from good people she hid as far as possible from her husband, and often forcibly took away his booty if he had not managed to drink it away at the tavern. The friend, in spite of his constant coolness, did not like to give way to her; and so he almost always left the house with lanterns under both eyes, and his dear half, groaning, trailed off to tell the old women about her husband's outrages and the beatings she had suffered from him.

Now one can imagine how the weaver and the friend were taken aback by such an unexpected appearance. Putting down the sack, they stood in front of it and covered it with the skirts of their coats; but it was too late: the friend's wife, although she saw badly with her old eyes, noticed the sack all the same. “That is good!” she said with a look in which the joy of a hawk could be seen. “It is good that you have collected so much by carolling! That is how good people always do; but no, I think you have picked it up somewhere. Show me at once, do you hear, show me your sack this minute!”

“The bald devil will show you, not we,” said the friend, drawing himself up.

“What business is it of yours?” said the weaver. “We collected it, not you.”

“No, you will show me, worthless drunkard!” cried the wife, striking the tall friend with her fist on the chin and pushing through to the sack. But the weaver and the friend bravely defended the sack and made her fall back. They had hardly recovered when the wife ran out into the porch, already with a poker in her hands. Quickly she struck her husband on the hands with the poker, the weaver on the back, and was already standing beside the sack.

“Why did we let her in?” said the weaver, coming to his senses.

“Oh, why did we let her! And why did you let her!” said the friend coolly.

“Your poker must be iron!” said the weaver after a short silence, scratching his back. “My wife bought a poker last year at the fair; gave half a kopeck for it; and that one is nothing... it doesn't hurt...”

Meanwhile the triumphant wife, setting a little lamp on the floor, untied the sack and looked into it.

But surely her old eyes, which had seen the sack so well, were deceived this time. “Oh, there is a whole boar lying here!” she cried, clapping her hands with joy.

“A boar! Do you hear, a whole boar!” the weaver nudged his friend. “And it is all your fault!”

“What is to be done!” said the friend, shrugging his shoulders.

“What do you mean, what? Why are we standing here? Let us take the sack away! Come on, attack!”

“Get away! Away! This is our boar!” shouted the weaver, stepping forward.

“Go, go, you devil's woman! This is not your property!” said the friend, coming closer.

The wife took up the poker again, but at that moment Chub climbed out of the sack and stood in the middle of the porch, stretching like a man who had just woken from a long sleep.

The friend's wife cried out, striking her hands against her skirts, and everyone involuntarily gaped.

“What is she saying, the fool: a boar! This is not a boar!” said the friend, goggling.

“Look what a man has been thrown into the sack!” said the weaver, backing away in fright. “Say what you like, burst if you will, but there is the unclean power in this. He would not even fit through the window!”

“It is friend Chub!” cried the friend, looking closer.

“And who did you think it was?” said Chub, smiling. “Well, what a fine trick I played on you! And you, I dare say, wanted to eat me instead of pork? Wait, then, I will please you: there is something else in the sack, if not a boar then surely a piglet or some other creature. Something was moving under me all the time.”

The weaver and the friend rushed to the sack, the lady of the house seized it from the opposite side, and the fight would have broken out again if the deacon, seeing now that he had nowhere to hide, had not scrambled out of the sack.

The friend's wife, rooted to the spot, let go of the leg by which she had begun to pull the deacon out of the sack.

“Here is another one!” cried the weaver in fear. “The devil knows how things are in the world... my head is spinning... not sausages and loaves, but people are thrown into sacks!”

“It is the deacon!” said Chub, more astonished than anyone. “Well, there you are! Good for Solokha! To put him in a sack... So that is why I saw her cottage full of sacks... Now I know everything: she had two men sitting in every sack. And I thought she had only me... well, there is Solokha for you!”

The girls were a little surprised at not finding one sack. “Nothing to be done, this one will do for us,” babbled Oksana. All took hold of the sack and heaved it onto the sledge. The Headman resolved to keep silent, reasoning that if he shouted to be let out and the sack untied, the foolish girls would run away and think that the devil was sitting in the sack, and he would be left in the street, perhaps till tomorrow. The girls meanwhile, joining hands in a friendly way, flew like a whirlwind with the sledge over the creaking snow. A crowd of them, playing about, sat on the sledge; others climbed onto the Headman himself. The Headman resolved to bear everything. At last they arrived, threw wide the door in the porch and the cottage, and with laughter dragged in the sack. “Let us see what is lying here,” they all cried, rushing to untie it. Then the hiccups, which had not ceased to torment the Headman the whole time he sat in the sack, grew so strong that he began to hiccup and cough at the top of his voice. “Oh, there is someone sitting in there!” they all cried, and in fright rushed out of the door.

“What the devil! Where are you rushing off to, like mad things?” said Chub, coming in at the door.

“Oh, father!” said Oksana. “There is someone sitting in the sack!”

“In the sack? Where did you get this sack?”

“The blacksmith threw it down in the middle of the road,” they all said at once.

“Well, so, did I not say...?” thought Chub to himself. “What are you frightened of? Let us see: come now, my good man, I beg you not to be angry that we do not call you by name and patronymic, climb out of the sack!”

The Headman climbed out.

“Ah!” cried the girls.

“And the Headman has got in there too,” Chub said to himself in bewilderment, measuring him from head to foot. “Look at that!... Eh!...” More than this he could not say.

The Headman himself was no less embarrassed and did not know what to begin. “It must be cold outside?” he said, turning to Chub.

“There is a bit of frost,” answered Chub, “and allow me to ask you, what do you grease your boots with, lard or tar?” He did not mean to say this; he meant to ask: how did you, Headman, get into this sack; but he himself did not understand how he had said something quite different.

“Tar is better,” said the Headman. “Well, farewell, Chub!” And, pulling his cap down, he left the cottage.

“Why did I ask, like a fool, what he greases his boots with!” said Chub, glancing at the door by which the Headman had gone out. “Good for Solokha! To stuff a man like that into a sack!... Look at her, the devil's woman! And I am a fool... but where is that accursed sack?”

“I threw it in the corner, there is nothing more in it,” said Oksana.

“I know these tricks, nothing in it! Hand it here: there is another one sitting in there! Shake it well... What, no?... Look at her, the accursed woman! And to look at her, a saint, as if she had never put anything but Lenten food in her mouth.”

But let us leave Chub to pour out his vexation at leisure, and return to the blacksmith, for it must surely be nine o'clock outside by now.

## VII · To Petersburg

At first it seemed terrifying to Vakula when he rose from the earth to such a height that he could see nothing below, and flew like a fly right under the moon, so that if he had not bent down a little he would have caught it with his cap. But a little later he took heart and began to make fun of the devil. It amused him beyond measure how the devil sneezed and coughed when he took the cypress-wood cross from his neck and held it out to him. On purpose he would lift his hand to scratch his head, and the devil, thinking he was about to be crossed, flew still faster. Everything was bright up on high. The air, in a light silver mist, was transparent. Everything could be seen, and one could even notice how a sorcerer swept past them like a whirlwind, sitting in a pot; how the stars, gathered in a heap, played blind man's buff; how a whole swarm of spirits swirled to one side like a cloud; how a devil, dancing in the moonlight, took off his cap when he saw the blacksmith riding along on horseback; how a broom came flying back, on which a witch had evidently just ridden where she was needed... they met much more such rubbish. All of them, seeing the blacksmith, stopped for a minute to look at him and then rushed on again and went about their business; the blacksmith flew on, and suddenly Petersburg blazed up before him, all in fire. (There was an illumination at the time, for some occasion.) The devil, flying over the barrier, turned into a horse, and the blacksmith saw himself on a spirited trotter in the middle of the street. My God! Clatter, thunder, glitter; on both sides four-storey walls loomed; the horse's hooves and the sound of the wheels echoed like thunder and rang back from four sides; houses grew and seemed to rise out of the earth at every step; bridges trembled; carriages flew; cabmen and postilions shouted; the snow whistled under a thousand sledges flying from every side; pedestrians huddled and crowded under houses strung with lamps, and their enormous shadows flickered along the walls, reaching the chimneys and roofs with their heads. In amazement the blacksmith looked around on all sides. It seemed to him that all the houses had fixed on him their countless fiery eyes and were watching. Gentlemen in cloth-covered fur coats he saw so many that he did not know to whom to take off his cap. “My God, how many gentry there are here!” thought the blacksmith. “I think everyone who walks down the street in a fur coat is an assessor, an assessor! And those who ride in such wonderful glass-windowed carriages, if they are not mayors, then surely commissaries, and perhaps even more.” His words were interrupted by the devil's question: “Shall we go straight to the Empress?” “No, it is frightening,” thought the blacksmith. “Somewhere here, I don't know where, the Zaporozhians who passed through Dikanka in the autumn are lodging. They were travelling from the Sich with papers for the Empress; all the same I should consult them. Hey, Satan, climb into my pocket, and lead me to the Zaporozhians!” In one minute the devil grew thin and became so small that he climbed into his pocket without difficulty. And Vakula had no time to look round before he found himself in front of a big house, went, not knowing how, up the stairs, opened the door, and drew back a little from the glitter at the sight of a splendidly furnished room; but he took heart a little, recognising the very Zaporozhians who had passed through Dikanka, sitting on silk sofas, with their tarred boots tucked under them, smoking the strongest tobacco, usually called roots.

“Good health to you, gentlemen! God help you! So this is where we meet!” said the blacksmith, coming close and bowing to the ground.

“What man is that?” asked one who sat right in front of the blacksmith, of another who sat farther off.

“And you do not recognise me?” said the blacksmith. “It is I, Vakula the blacksmith! When you passed through Dikanka in the autumn, you stayed, God give you every health and long life, nearly two days. And I put a new tire on the front wheel of your wagon then!”

“Ah!” said the same Zaporozhian. “It is that blacksmith who paints so grandly. Hello, countryman! What has God brought you here for?”

“Oh, just wanted to have a look, they say...”

“Well, countryman,” said the Zaporozhian, drawing himself up and wishing to show that he too could speak in Russian. “Vot, a beeg city?”

The blacksmith did not want to disgrace himself and appear a novice either; besides, as we have had occasion to see above, he knew the learned language himself. “A notable province!” he answered indifferently. “Nothing to say, the houses are enormous, important paintings hang everywhere. Many houses are inscribed with letters of gold leaf, to the extreme. Nothing to say, a marvellous proportion!”

The Zaporozhians, hearing the blacksmith express himself so freely, drew a conclusion very advantageous to him.

“We will talk with you more later, countryman; now we are going at once to the Empress.”

“To the Empress? Be so kind, gentlemen, take me with you!”

“You?” said the Zaporozhian, with the look with which a nurse speaks to her four-year-old charge who asks to be put on a real, big horse. “What will you do there? No, it is not possible.” At this a significant expression appeared on his face. “We, brother, shall be talking with the Empress about our own affairs.”

“Take me!” insisted the blacksmith. “Ask!” he whispered quietly to the devil, striking his pocket with his fist. He had hardly said this when another Zaporozhian said: “Let us take him, really, brothers!”

“Let us take him, then!” said the others.

“Put on clothes like ours.”

The blacksmith hastened to pull on a green coat, when suddenly the door opened and a man with gold lace came in and said it was time to go.

It seemed strange to the blacksmith again when he rushed along in an enormous carriage, swaying on its springs, when on both sides four-storey houses ran backwards past him and the pavement, thundering, seemed to roll itself under the horses' feet.

“My God, what a light!” thought the blacksmith to himself. “With us it is never so bright by day.”

The carriages stopped in front of a palace. The Zaporozhians got out, entered a magnificent hall, and began to climb a brilliantly lit staircase.

“What a staircase!” whispered the blacksmith to himself. “It is a pity to tread on it with one's feet. What decorations! They say fairy tales lie; what the devil do they lie! My God, what banisters! What workmanship! Fifty roubles' worth of iron alone went into this!”

Having climbed the stairs, the Zaporozhians passed through the first hall. The blacksmith followed them timidly, fearing at every step to slip on the parquet. They passed through three halls, and the blacksmith did not cease to marvel. Entering the fourth, he involuntarily went up to a picture that hung on the wall. It was the Most Pure Virgin with the Child in her arms. “What a picture! What wonderful painting!” he reflected. “Look, it seems to speak! It seems alive! And the Holy Child! And he has pressed his little hands! And smiles, poor thing! And the colours! My God, what colours! There is not a kopeck's worth of ochre here, I think, all verdigris and crimson. And the blue fairly burns! A grand piece of work! The ground must have been laid with white lead. But however wonderful these paintings are, this copper handle,” he went on, going up to the door and feeling the lock, “deserves still greater wonder. What clean workmanship! All this, I think, was made by German blacksmiths at the dearest prices...” Perhaps the blacksmith would have gone on reasoning a long time if a footman with braid had not jogged his arm and reminded him not to fall behind the others. The Zaporozhians passed through two more halls and stopped. Here they were told to wait. In the hall a number of generals crowded, in gold-embroidered uniforms. The Zaporozhians bowed on all sides and stood in a heap. A minute later there came in, accompanied by a whole retinue, a rather stout man of majestic height, in a hetman's uniform, in yellow boots. His hair was dishevelled, one eye slightly squinting, on his face there was a kind of haughty grandeur, and in all his movements the habit of command could be seen. All the generals, who had been strolling about quite proudly in their gold uniforms, bustled and, with low bows, seemed to catch his every word and even his slightest movement, ready to fly at once to carry it out. But the hetman did not even pay attention, barely nodded his head and went up to the Zaporozhians.

The Zaporozhians all bowed to his feet.

“Are you all here?” he asked, drawling, pronouncing the words a little through his nose.

“All here, father!” answered the Zaporozhians, bowing again.

“You will not forget to speak as I taught you?”

“No, father, we will not forget.”

“Is that the Tsar?” the blacksmith asked one of the Zaporozhians.

“The Tsar, indeed! That is Potemkin himself,” answered the man.

Voices were heard in another room, and the blacksmith did not know where to put his eyes at the multitude of ladies who came in, in satin dresses with long trains, and courtiers in gold-embroidered coats with bunches of hair at the back. He saw only glitter and nothing more. The Zaporozhians suddenly all fell to the ground and cried with one voice: “Have mercy, Mother! Have mercy!” The blacksmith, seeing nothing, stretched himself out on the floor too, with all diligence.

“Rise!” a commanding and at the same time pleasant voice sounded above them. Some of the courtiers bustled and nudged the Zaporozhians.

“We will not rise, Mother! We will not rise! We will die, but we will not rise!” cried the Zaporozhians.

Potemkin bit his lips; at last he came up himself and whispered a command to one of the Zaporozhians. The Zaporozhians rose.

Then the blacksmith too dared to raise his head and saw standing before him a woman of small height, even somewhat stout, powdered, with blue eyes and at the same time a majestically smiling air which knew how to subdue everyone and could belong only to a reigning woman.

“His Serene Highness promised to acquaint me today with my people, whom I have never yet seen,” said the lady with the blue eyes, examining the Zaporozhians with curiosity. “Are you well kept here?” she went on, coming nearer.

“Thank you, Mother! They give good provisions, though the sheep here are quite unlike ours in Zaporozhia; but why not live somehow?...”

Potemkin frowned, seeing that the Zaporozhians were saying quite the opposite of what he had taught them...

One of the Zaporozhians, drawing himself up, stepped forward: “Have mercy, Mother! How has Thy faithful people angered Thee? Did we hold the hand of the filthy Tatar? Did we agree in anything with the Turk? Have we betrayed Thee in deed or in thought? Why then this displeasure? Before, we heard that Thou orderest fortresses to be built everywhere against us; later we heard that Thou wishest to turn us into carabineers; now we hear of new misfortunes. What is the fault of the Zaporozhian Host? That it took Thy army across Perekop and helped Thy generals cut down the Crimeans?...”

Potemkin was silent and carelessly cleaned with a small brush the diamonds with which his hands were studded.

“What is it that you want?” Catherine asked with concern.

The Zaporozhians looked meaningfully at each other.

“Now is the time! The Empress asks what you want!” the blacksmith said to himself and suddenly threw himself on the ground:

“Your Imperial Majesty, do not order me executed, order me pardoned! What, no offence meant to Your Imperial mercy, are the little shoes on Your feet made of? I think no cobbler in any kingdom in the world could make them so. My God, what if my wife wore such shoes!”

The Empress laughed. The courtiers laughed too. Potemkin both frowned and smiled. The Zaporozhians began to nudge the blacksmith's arm, wondering whether he had gone mad.

“Rise!” said the Empress kindly. “If you want so much to have such shoes, it is not hard to do. Bring him at once the dearest shoes, with gold! Truly, I like this simplicity very much! Here,” the Empress went on, fixing her eyes on a middle-aged man standing apart from the others, with a full but somewhat pale face, whose modest coat with large mother-of-pearl buttons showed that he did not belong to the courtiers, “is a subject worthy of your witty pen!”

“Your Imperial Majesty is too gracious. For this one would need at least a La Fontaine!” answered the man with the pearl buttons, bowing.

“Upon my honour I tell you: I am still beside myself over your 'Brigadier'. You read wonderfully well! But,” the Empress went on, turning again to the Zaporozhians, “I have heard that in the Sich you never marry.”

“Why, Mother! Surely a man, as You Yourself know, cannot live without a wife,” answered the same Zaporozhian who had talked with the blacksmith, and the blacksmith was surprised to hear that this Zaporozhian, who knew the learned language so well, was speaking to the Empress, as if on purpose, in the coarsest dialect, usually called peasant speech. “A cunning people!” he thought to himself. “Surely he does this for a reason.”

“We are not monks,” the Zaporozhian went on, “but sinful men. We are as prone, like all honest Christendom, to forbidden food. There are not a few among us who have wives, only they do not live with them in the Sich. There are some who have wives in Poland; there are some who have wives in Ukraine; there are some who have wives in Turkey too.”

At this time the shoes were brought to the blacksmith. “My God, what an ornament!” he cried joyfully, seizing the shoes. “Your Imperial Majesty! If shoes like these are on Your feet, and in them, most likely, Your Honour goes skating on the ice, what must the feet themselves be? I think, at the very least, of pure sugar.”

The Empress, who truly had the most shapely and charming feet, could not help smiling at hearing such a compliment from the lips of the simple blacksmith, who in his Zaporozhian dress might have passed for a handsome man, in spite of his dark face.

Delighted by such gracious attention, the blacksmith was about to question the Empress closely about everything: whether it is true that tsars eat nothing but honey and lard, and the like; but feeling the Zaporozhians nudge him in the ribs, he decided to be silent; and when the Empress, turning to the old men, began to ask how they lived in the Sich and what customs they had, he stepped back, bent to his pocket, said quietly: “Carry me out of here quickly!” and suddenly found himself beyond the barrier.

## VIII · Christmas Morning

“Drowned! By God, drowned! May I not move from this spot if he did not drown!” babbled the stout weaver's wife, standing in a knot of Dikanka women in the middle of the street.

“What, am I some kind of liar? Have I stolen someone's cow? Have I put the evil eye on someone, that I am not believed?” cried a woman in a Cossack coat with a violet nose, waving her hands. “May I never want to drink water again if old Pereperchikha did not see with her own eyes how the blacksmith hanged himself!”

“The blacksmith hanged himself? Well, there you are!” said the Headman, who was coming out from Chub's; he stopped and pushed his way closer to those who were talking.

“Say rather may you never want to drink vodka, you old drunkard!” answered the weaver's wife. “One would have to be as mad as you to hang oneself! He drowned! Drowned in an ice-hole! I know that as surely as that you were at the tavern-keeper's just now.”

“Shameless creature! Look what she is reproaching me with!” retorted the woman with the violet nose angrily. “Be quiet, you worthless thing! Don't I know that the deacon comes to you every evening?”

The weaver's wife flared up.

“What deacon? Who is the deacon coming to? What are you lying about?”

“The deacon?” sang out the deacon's wife, pressing toward the disputants, in a hareskin coat covered with blue Chinese cotton. “I will give you a deacon! Who is saying deacon?”

“There, that is who the deacon visits!” said the woman with the violet nose, pointing to the weaver's wife.

“So it is you, bitch,” said the deacon's wife, advancing on the weaver's wife, “so it is you, witch, who cast a fog on him and give him unclean potions to drink, so that he will come to you!”

“Leave me alone, Satan!” said the weaver's wife, backing away.

“Look at her, the accursed witch, may you never live to see your children, you worthless creature! Phew!” Here the deacon's wife spat right in the weaver's wife's eyes.

The weaver's wife wanted to do the same, but instead spat into the unshaven beard of the Headman, who, the better to hear everything, had worked his way up to the very disputants. “Ah, foul woman!” cried the Headman, wiping his face with the skirt of his coat and raising his whip. This movement made everyone scatter, swearing, in different directions. “What filth!” he kept repeating, still wiping himself. “So the blacksmith has drowned! My God, and what a fine painter he was! What strong knives, sickles and ploughs he knew how to forge! What strength he had! Yes,” he went on, thinking, “there are few such men in our village. That is why, while still sitting in the accursed sack, I noticed that the poor fellow was in a very bad mood. So much for the blacksmith! He was, and now he is no more! And I was just about to have my dappled mare shod!...” And, full of such Christian thoughts, the Headman slowly trudged to his cottage.

Oksana was confused when such news reached her. She put little faith in Pereperchikha's eyes and the women's talk: she knew that the blacksmith was pious enough not to decide to destroy his soul. But what if he really had gone away with the intention of never returning to the village? And it is doubtful whether another such fine fellow as the blacksmith could be found anywhere! And he had loved her so! He had endured her whims longer than anyone! All night, under her blanket, the beauty turned from her right side to her left, from her left to her right, and could not sleep. Now, tossing in a bewitching nakedness which the darkness of night hid even from herself, she almost aloud scolded herself; now, growing quiet, she resolved to think of nothing, and kept thinking. And she was all aflame; and by morning she was head over heels in love with the blacksmith.

Chub showed neither joy nor sorrow at Vakula's fate. His thoughts were occupied with one thing: he could not forget Solokha's treachery, and even asleep he did not cease to curse her.

Morning came. The whole church was full of people even before daylight. Elderly women in white head-cloths, in white cloth coats, crossed themselves piously at the very entrance of the church. Gentlewomen in green and yellow jackets, and some even in blue kuntushes with gold whiskers behind, stood in front of them. Girls with a whole shop of ribbons wound on their heads, and on their necks strings of beads, crosses and ducats, tried to push in still nearer to the iconostasis. But in front of all stood the gentlemen and plain peasants with moustaches, with forelocks, with thick necks and chins just shaved, mostly in cloaks, from under which a white, or on some a blue, coat was showing. On all faces, wherever you looked, the holiday could be seen: the Headman licked his lips, imagining how he would break his fast with sausage; the girls were thinking how they would skate on the ice with the lads; the old women whispered their prayers more zealously than ever. All over the church one could hear how the Cossack Svyerbyhuz made his bows. Only Oksana stood as if not herself. She prayed and did not pray. In her heart so many different feelings were crowded, one more vexing than another, one sadder than another, that her face expressed only strong confusion; tears trembled in her eyes. The girls could not understand the cause and did not suspect that the blacksmith was to blame. Yet Oksana was not the only one occupied with the blacksmith. All the villagers noticed that the holiday was somehow not a holiday; that something was somehow missing. As bad luck would have it, the deacon, after his journey in the sack, was hoarse and quavered in a barely audible voice; true, the visiting singer took the bass splendidly, but how much better if the blacksmith had been there, who always, as soon as they sang Our Father or Who the Cherubim, used to go up to the choir and lead from there in the same tune as they sing in Poltava. Besides, he alone performed the duties of church warden. Already morning service was over; after morning service, the liturgy was over... where, indeed, had the blacksmith got to?

Still faster through the rest of the night the devil sped back with the blacksmith. And in a moment Vakula found himself beside his cottage. At that moment a cock crowed. “Where to?” he cried, seizing by the tail the devil who wanted to run away. “Wait, my friend, not everything yet: I have not yet thanked you.” Then, seizing a switch, he gave him three blows, and the poor devil took to his heels like a peasant who has just been flogged by the assessor. So, instead of leading astray, tempting and making fools of others, the enemy of mankind was himself made a fool of. After this Vakula went into the porch, burrowed into the hay and slept till noon. Waking, he was frightened when he saw that the sun was already high: “I have slept through morning service and the liturgy!” The pious blacksmith sank into dejection, reasoning that surely God had purposely, as a punishment for his sinful intention of destroying his soul, sent him a sleep that did not even let him be in church on such a solemn holiday. But, comforting himself that next week he would confess this to the priest, and from today would begin to make fifty prostrations every day for a whole year, he looked into the cottage; but no one was in it. Evidently Solokha had not yet returned. Carefully he took the shoes from his bosom and again marvelled at the costly work and the wonderful adventure of the past night; he washed, dressed as well as he could, put on the very clothes which he had got from the Zaporozhians, took from the chest a new cap of Reshetilovka lambskin with a blue top, which he had not worn once since buying it back in Poltava; he also took a new belt of all colours; he put all this together with a whip in a kerchief and set off straight for Chub's.

Chub goggled when the blacksmith came in to him, and did not know what to wonder at: whether that the blacksmith had risen from the dead, or that the blacksmith dared to come to him, or that he had dressed up as such a dandy and a Zaporozhian. But he was still more astonished when Vakula untied the kerchief and laid before him a brand-new cap and a belt such as had never been seen in the village, and himself fell at his feet and said in a pleading voice: “Have mercy, father! Do not be angry! Here is a whip for you: beat me as much as your heart desires, I give myself up; I repent of everything; beat me, only do not be angry! You once were brothers in arms with my late father, ate bread and salt together and drank to the bargain.”

Not without secret pleasure Chub saw the blacksmith, who feared no one in the village and bent coins and horseshoes in his hand like buckwheat pancakes, lying at his feet. So as not to lower himself further, Chub took the whip and struck him three times on the back. “Well, that is enough for you, get up! Always listen to old people! Let us forget all that was between us! Now say, what do you want?”

“Give Oksana to me, father!”

Chub thought a little, looked at the cap and belt, the cap was a wonder, the belt was not inferior to it, remembered treacherous Solokha, and said resolutely: “Good! Send the matchmakers!”

“Ah!” cried Oksana, stepping over the threshold and seeing the blacksmith, and fixed her eyes on him with amazement and joy.

“Look what shoes I have brought you!” said Vakula. “The very ones the Empress wears.”

“No! No! I don't need shoes!” she said, waving her hands and not taking her eyes from him. “I will, even without shoes...” She did not finish and blushed.

The blacksmith came nearer and took her hand; the beauty lowered her eyes. Never before had she been so wonderfully pretty. The delighted blacksmith kissed her softly, and her face flamed more and she became lovelier still.

A bishop of blessed memory passed through Dikanka, praised the place where the village stands, and, driving along the street, stopped before a new cottage. “And whose is this painted cottage?” the bishop asked a handsome woman with a child in her arms, who was standing near the door. “The blacksmith Vakula's!” Oksana said to him, bowing, because it was she. “Splendid! Splendid work!” said the bishop, examining the doors and windows. And the windows were all outlined round with red paint; and on the doors everywhere were Cossacks on horses with pipes in their teeth. But the bishop praised Vakula still more when he learned that he had served the church penance and had painted the whole left choir for nothing, with green paint and red flowers. And that is not all: on the wall at the side, as you enter the church, Vakula painted the devil in hell, so ugly that everyone spat when they passed by; and the women, as soon as a child began to cry in their arms, would bring it to the picture and say: “Look, see what an ugly thing is painted!” and the child, holding back its little tears, squinted at the picture and pressed to its mother's breast.
