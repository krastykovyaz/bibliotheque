## I · The Poet and the Poem

Mikhail Lermontov was born in Moscow in 1814, two years after the battle you are about to hear about. His grandmother brought him up, and he began to write poems as a boy. He studied for a time at Moscow University, then trained as a cadet and became an officer in a Guards hussar regiment.

In the winter of 1837 Alexander Pushkin died after a duel. Lermontov, who was twenty-two, wrote a fierce poem about his death, and it spread across Russia in handwritten copies. For it he was arrested and sent to serve in the Caucasus.

Later that same year he wrote Borodino. The twenty-fifth anniversary of the battle fell in 1837, and the poem was printed in The Contemporary, the magazine Pushkin had founded. Lermontov had not seen the war, for he came after it. So he gave the story to someone who had. The poem is a conversation: a young man asks an old soldier, whom he calls uncle, why Moscow was given up, and the old soldier answers by telling him what the battle was like.

Writing about the war of 1812 was not new in Russian poetry. What Lermontov chose was to let an ordinary soldier speak, in plain, spoken words, with a soldier's jokes and a soldier's pride, instead of a poet describing generals from above.

Lermontov himself lived only a few years more. He wrote the novel A Hero of Our Time, and in 1841, at the age of twenty-six, he was killed in a duel near the town of Pyatigorsk in the Caucasus. Borodino remained one of the best loved poems in the Russian language.

But first, here is what happened on that field.

## II · The Battle of 1812

In June 1812 the army of the French emperor Napoleon crossed the river Neman and entered Russia. It was one of the largest armies Europe had ever seen, hundreds of thousands of men. The Russian armies were smaller and divided, and they fell back, mile after mile, towards the heart of the country. Soldiers and officers grew bitter. They wanted a fight, and instead they were ordered to retreat. This is the retreat the old soldier remembers.

In August the Tsar, Alexander the First, gave command of the whole army to Mikhail Kutuzov, an old general who had served for decades. Kutuzov chose a position on open ground near the village of Borodino, about a hundred and twenty kilometres west of Moscow, and the army dug in. Earth forts were raised on the hills, among them the great redoubt that became known as the Raevsky battery, and the works on the left wing that carried the name of General Bagration.

On the seventh of September, which was still the twenty-sixth of August in the Russian calendar of the time, the battle began at dawn. Each side had well over a hundred thousand men and more than a thousand guns. For about twelve hours they fought over the redoubts and the ground between them. Together the two armies lost tens of thousands of killed and wounded, by most estimates more than seventy thousand in a single day. General Bagration was mortally wounded. At nightfall the guns fell silent.

The fighting had begun two days earlier, on the fifth of September, at the Shevardino redoubt, which the Russians gave up at nightfall. On the day itself the first great struggle was for Bagration's works on the left, which changed hands several times before the French held them towards midday. Then the fight moved to the Raevsky battery in the centre. It fell to the French in the afternoon, after assaults by infantry and cavalry. Napoleon did not send in his Guard, his last reserve, and the Russian line, bent back but unbroken, held through the evening.

Neither side could say it had won. Napoleon had not destroyed the Russian army, and Kutuzov decided that the army must be saved even if the city could not. The Russians withdrew, and at a council in the village of Fili the order was given to leave Moscow without a fight.

The French entered Moscow on the fourteenth of September. Fires broke out and burned a large part of the city. Napoleon waited for peace talks that never came, and in October he began the retreat, and over the winter his army fell apart.

For Russians Borodino became a day of courage and of a terrible price. It is this day that the young man in the poem asks about. Here is the poem.

## III · The Question

“Tell me, uncle, was it not for nothing that Moscow, burned by the fire, was handed over to the Frenchman? There were battles, were there not? Yes, so they say, and what battles! Not for nothing does all Russia remember the day of Borodino!”

“Yes, there were men in our time, not like the tribe of today: heroes, not you! A hard lot fell to them: not many came back from the field. If it had not been God's will, they would never have given up Moscow!”

## IV · The Eve

“We retreated for a long time, in silence. It was bitter; we were waiting for a fight. The old soldiers grumbled: ‘What are we doing, going to winter quarters? Do the commanders not dare to tear foreign uniforms on Russian bayonets?’”

“And then we found a great field: room enough to roam free! We built a redoubt. Our ears were pricked up. The morning had hardly lit our cannon and the blue tops of the forests, and the French were there on the spot.”

“I rammed the charge tight into my gun and thought: I will treat my friend! Wait a bit, brother monsieur! Why scheme? Come on to the fight; we shall go and break you like a wall, we shall stand with our heads for our own country!”

“For two days we skirmished. What use is a trifle like that? We waited for the third day. Everywhere the talk was the same: ‘Time to get at the grapeshot!’ And then on the field of the terrible slaughter the shadow of night fell.”

“I lay down to doze by the gun carriage, and until dawn I could hear the Frenchman rejoicing. But our open camp was quiet: one man cleaned his battered shako, another sharpened his bayonet, grumbling angrily and biting his long moustache.”

## V · The Battle

“And hardly had the sky begun to glow when everything suddenly stirred and rustled, and rank after rank flashed out. Our colonel was born a daredevil: servant of the Tsar, father to his soldiers. Ah, I pity him: struck down by steel, he sleeps in the damp earth.”

“And he said, flashing his eyes: ‘Lads! Is not Moscow behind us? Let us die before Moscow, as our brothers died!’ And we promised to die, and we kept our oath of loyalty in the battle of Borodino.”

“Well, that was a day! Through the drifting smoke the French came on like clouds, and all of them at our redoubt. Lancers with bright pennants, dragoons with horsetail plumes, all flashed past us, all of them paid a visit here.”

“You will never see such battles! Banners swept past like shadows, fire gleamed in the smoke, steel rang, grapeshot shrieked, the fighters' hands grew tired of stabbing, and a mountain of bloody bodies kept the cannonballs from flying.”

“The enemy learned a good deal that day of what a bold Russian fight means, our hand-to-hand fight! The earth shook, just like our chests. Horses and men were heaped together, and the volleys of a thousand guns ran together into one long howl...”

## VI · The Evening

“Then dusk fell. We were all ready to begin a new battle in the morning and stand to the end. Then the drums rattled, and the heathens fell back. And then we began to count our wounds, to count our comrades.”

“Yes, there were men in our time, a mighty, daring tribe: heroes, not you. A hard lot fell to them: not many came back from the field. If it had not been God's will, they would never have given up Moscow!”

## VII · After the Poem

That was Borodino: ninety-eight lines, which many Russian schoolchildren still learn by heart. Every Russian knows its first words, Tell me, uncle.

It is not a poem about glory alone. The old soldier is proud, but he speaks of the dead, of the colonel who sleeps in the damp earth, and of how few came back from the field. He sets his own generation, the heroes, against the young man's, and the poem ends on the same stanza it began his answer with, as if there were nothing more to say. The battle was told again later, most famously by Leo Tolstoy in War and Peace, in 1869; but for many Russians the first picture of Borodino is this one, spoken by a soldier.
