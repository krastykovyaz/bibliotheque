## I · The Princess Is Born

Once there lived a good King Matthew
With his Queen, who loved him dear.
They were happy, but for children
They had waited many a year.

By a brook one day the Queen sat
On the green bank, all alone,
And she wept and wept so sadly
That her tears fell on a stone.

Suddenly a crab came creeping,
And he said with a gentle bow:
“Dear Queen, I am sorry for you,
But put away your woe.
This very night a baby girl
Will come to you. I know.”

“Thank you, thank you, good old crab!
I never thought...”
But the crab had slipped away
Into the brook, and did not hear.
He was a prophet, and his words
Came true within the year.

Then the Queen had a little daughter,
So beautiful and bright
That no story-teller's tongue,
No pen, could tell it right.

King Matthew gave a splendid feast,
A feast for all the land,
And he asked eleven young fairies
To come, a happy band.

There were twelve fairies in the country,
But the twelfth, who was old and lame
And cross, was not asked to the feast,
And it hurt her all the same.

Why did wise King Matthew do it?
There was a reason, you see:
He had twelve gold dishes, but only eleven
Were left. Where could the twelfth one be?
No one knew who took it.

“What can I do?”
said the King.
“Well, so be it!”
And he did not send to call the old one.
The feast went merrily on.

The fairies came, and ate, and drank,
And when the feast was through,
They thanked the generous King, and gave
Their gifts to the baby, too.

“You shall wear a gown of gold,
You shall be a marvel fair,
Gentle, quiet, good and kind,
A joy to all, everywhere.
I shall give you a handsome husband,
And your life shall pass like play
With your friends and with your family
Every happy day.”

## II · The Spell

Ten young fairies gave their gifts
And left, one after one.
The last was just about to speak
When, look! the Uninvited One
Stood over the baby, grumbling:

“I was not asked, but I have brought
A gift for you, my pretty one:
In your sixteenth year you will prick your hand
With a spindle, and your life is done.”

Muttering, the old one vanished.
But the youngest fairy still was there,
And she had not given her gift yet. She said:
“I shall not let that cruel word
Do its worst. I will take care.”

“It shall not be death, but sleep,
A sleep that three hundred years shall keep.
Then she shall wake, and live long years
Free from sorrow, free from tears,
And her grandchildren shall bring delight
To her father and mother, day and night.”

The fairy vanished. The King was sad;
He could not eat or sleep, poor man.
How could he save his daughter dear?
He thought, and made a plan:
“From today, in all our land,
No one shall sow flax, or spin.
Let no spindle stay in any house.
Send all the spinners out!”

When the law was made, the King
Could eat and sleep again.
The princess grew like a flower in May.
She was fifteen years old. And then, and then?

One day the King and Queen went walking
And left their daughter home.
She grew bored in the stuffy room,
Looking out, all alone.
“Let me go and see the palace,
Every hall and every stair!”

Through the palace she went wandering,
Admiring every hall,
Till she found a door left open
And a winding stair, round a pillar tall.
Up she climbed, and found an old woman
With a long, long nose, who spun
And sang as she spun her thread:

“Little spindle, do not tire,
Fine thin thread, do not break.
Soon the guest we long have waited for
Will be here, no mistake.”

In came the guest they had waited for.
The spinner, silent, held out the spindle.
The girl took it, and it pricked her finger,
And everything went dim before her eyes.
Sleep came over her, and with her
Over the whole great palace.

## III · The Long Sleep

Her father, coming home, grew drowsy
On the steps. He swayed and yawned,
And fell asleep beside his Queen.
The courtiers slept; the guards
Stood sleeping at their posts, spears in hand,
And the rider slept on his sleeping horse.

On the walls the sleepy flies
Sat without a hum or sound.
The dogs lay sleeping at the gate,
And the horses, in their stalls,
Bowed their heads, and their manes hung down.
They did not eat, but slept and slept.

The cook slept by his fire,
And the fire, caught in sleep,
Did not blaze and did not burn,
But stood there, a sleepy flame.
And the smoke above it, curled up like a ball,
Did not stir, but slept.

All the land, and the palace too,
Was wrapped in a deep, deep sleep.
A great wood grew, and a hedge of thorns
Went round it, and closed the way
To the King's house for ever.
No one could find a path to it,
And to go near was dangerous.

No bird flew over that forest,
No beast came near. Not even a breeze
Would send a cloud across the dark, thick wood.
And so a whole century went by.

It was as if King Matthew
Had never lived; the people
Had long forgotten him. They knew
Only this: that in the forest stands a house,
And in the house a Princess sleeps.
Three hundred years she has to sleep,
And no one knows the way.

Many brave men, the old folk said,
Went to the wood to wake the Princess.
Some even made a bet, and went,
But none of them came back. Since then,
To that wild and fearful wood
Neither old nor young would go.

And time flowed on, and on, and on,
Till three hundred years were gone.

## IV · The Prince and the Old Man

What happened then? One spring day
A Prince went hunting, and he rode
With all his huntsmen through the fields,
Till he fell behind, and found himself alone
Beside the forest.

The forest was dark and wild. An old man
Came walking by, and the Prince said:
“Good old father, tell me, please,
About this forest, dark and dread.”
The old man shook his head, and told
Him all that he knew.

“My grandfathers told me of a wonder in this wood.
A rich king's palace stands there,
And has stood for a long, long time.
In it a Princess sleeps,
A magic sleep of three hundred years.
She is waiting for someone
To come and wake her up.”

“But the way is full of danger.
Many young men tried to go.
The same thing happened to them all:
They rode into the forest
And they were never seen again.”

The brave young Prince blazed up
As if a fire had been lit.
He pressed his spurs; his horse leapt
Like an arrow into the wood,
And in a moment he was there.

And what did the Prince see? The hedge
That had guarded the dark wood
Was no thick thorn bush any more,
But young shrubs, with roses blooming!
And the bushes parted for him,
As if they were alive.

Into the wood rode the Prince.
All was fresh and bright and sweet.
Butterflies danced on the flowers,
Brooks wound by like silver ribbons,
Birds hopped and chattered in the branches.
The wood was fragrant, cool and still.

Nothing in it frightened him.
He rode along the smooth path
For an hour, two, and then at last
A palace stood before him,
A wonder of olden days,
With its gates wide open.

In he rode, and in the courtyard
Found a crowd of people, all asleep.
One sat as if he had taken root,
One was walking, but did not move,
One stood with his mouth wide open.
Their talk had stopped in sleep,
And on their lips the unfinished words
Stayed silent.

One had meant to lie down for a nap,
But before he could, a magic sleep
Had caught him, before the simple one.
And for three hundred years
He neither stood nor lay, but slept,
Just about to fall!

The Prince was amazed. He went
Among the sleepers to the palace,
Came to the steps, and started to climb
The wide stairs, but there
The King lay, with the Queen, asleep.

The way was blocked.
“What shall I do?”
he thought.
“How can I get inside?”
But at last he made up his mind,
Said a little prayer, and stepped
Gently over the King.

## V · The Waking

Through the palace the Prince wandered.
All was splendid, and all was sleep,
With a silence deep as snow.
Then he saw an open door,
And a winding stair around a pillar.
He climbed. And what was there?
His heart beat fast. Before him
The Princess lay sleeping.

She lay like a child, her cheeks
Flushed rosy with her sleep.
Her long dark lashes shone,
Her black braids wound about her brow
Like the night, only softer.
Her dress was light as air.

Her lips were red, her hands lay white,
Her little feet in little shoes
Were the prettiest thing to see.
At such a sight he stood quite still,
Dazzled, and she slept on, quite still.
What could break the magic of the sleep?

He knelt down by her side
And bent his face to hers.
He could not stop himself:
He kissed her, gently.

At once she woke, and all around her
Woke up too: the King, the Queen,
The whole of the palace. Voices, shouting,
Bustle, as before.
As if not even a day had passed
Since all the land fell asleep.

The King went up the stairs,
And led his Queen to their rooms,
After their walk. Behind them
Marched the courtiers in a crowd;
The guards clattered their spears,
The flies flew off in swarms,
At the gate the dog barked.

In the stable the good horse
Finished eating his oats.
The cook blew on the fire,
And the fire crackled, and the smoke
Streamed up. All was as it had been,
Except one thing: the Prince.

At last the Prince and the Princess
Came down, and her mother and father
Hugged them both. And what is there
Left to tell?

A wedding and a feast, and I
Was there, and drank the wine.
It ran all down my whiskers,
But not a drop was mine.
