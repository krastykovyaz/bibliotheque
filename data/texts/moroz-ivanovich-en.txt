## I · The Two Girls

Nothing comes to us for free, without hard work. That is why people have always said so.

Once upon a time, in one little house, two girls lived with their old nurse. One girl was called Rukodelnitsa, which means a girl who is clever with her hands. The other was called Lenivitsa, which means a girl who is lazy. Rukodelnitsa was a bright little girl. She got up early and dressed herself, without any help from the nurse. As soon as she was out of bed, she set to work. She lit the stove, she mixed the dough for the bread, she swept the floor, she fed the rooster, and then she went to the well for water.

Lenivitsa, meanwhile, lay in her little bed. The church bells had been ringing for a long time, and she was still stretching and rolling from side to side. When she was tired of lying down, she would say sleepily: “Nurse, put on my stockings. Nurse, tie my shoes.” And then: “Nurse, is there a little bun for me?” At last she got up, hopped about a bit, and sat down at the window to count the flies: how many flew in, and how many flew out. When she had counted them all, she did not know what to do with herself. She did not want to sleep, and she did not want to eat, and she was even tired of counting flies. So she sat there and cried, and complained to everyone that she was bored, as if it were somebody else's fault.

Rukodelnitsa came back from the well and poured the water through a strainer into the jugs. She was a very clever girl. If the water was not clear, she rolled a sheet of paper into a cone, put a few bits of coal and some coarse sand in it, and set it in the jug. The water ran through the sand and the coal and dripped into the jug as clean as crystal. Then she knitted stockings, or sewed shirts, and she hummed a little work song. She was never bored, because she never had time to be: first one thing, then another, and look, it was already evening, and the day was gone.

## II · The Well

One day Rukodelnitsa had a bit of trouble. She went to the well for water and let the bucket down on its rope. But the rope snapped, and the bucket fell into the well! What could she do? Poor Rukodelnitsa burst into tears and went to tell the nurse about her misfortune. The nurse, whose name was Praskovya, was strict and cross.

“You made the trouble, so you must mend it. You dropped the bucket, so you must fetch it out.”

There was nothing to be done. Poor Rukodelnitsa went back to the well, took hold of the rope, and climbed down it to the very bottom.

And there a wonder happened to her. She had hardly got down when she saw a stove in front of her, and in the stove sat a little pie, so rosy and golden. The pie sat there looking about and saying:

“I am quite ready, I am nicely browned, with sugar and raisins I am crowned. Whoever takes me from the stove, with me shall go.”

Rukodelnitsa did not wait a moment. She seized the little shovel, took the pie out of the stove, and tucked it safely inside her coat.

She walked on. In front of her was a garden, and in the garden stood a tree, and on the tree hung golden apples. The little apples rustled their leaves and talked among themselves:

“We are apples, ripe and round. The roots of the tree have fed us, and cold clear water has washed us. Whoever shakes us from the tree, will take us home with them.”

Rukodelnitsa went up to the tree and shook a branch, and the golden apples came tumbling down into her apron.

## III · Moroz Ivanovich

Rukodelnitsa walked on. And there in front of her sat old Moroz Ivanovich, white as snow from head to foot. He sat on a bench made of ice and nibbled snowballs. When he shook his head, frost fell from his hair, and when he breathed out, thick white steam rolled across the ground.

“Ah!” he said. “Good day to you, Rukodelnitsa! Thank you for bringing me a pie. It is a very long time since I had anything hot to eat.”

Then he sat her down beside him, and together they ate the little pie for breakfast, and the golden apples for pudding.

Then Moroz Ivanovich said: “I know why you have come. You let your bucket fall into my cold well. I will give it back to you, but you must serve me for three days. If you are clever, it will be the better for you. If you are lazy, it will be the worse. And now,” he added, “an old man like me needs a rest. Go and make my bed, and be sure to shake up the feather bed well.”

Rukodelnitsa did as she was told. They went into his house. The house of Moroz Ivanovich was made of ice: the doors, the windows and the floor were all ice, and the walls were decorated with little snow stars. The sun shone on them, and everything in the house sparkled like diamonds. On the bed, instead of a feather bed, lay soft fluffy snow. It was cold, but there was nothing to be done. Rukodelnitsa began to shake up the snow, so that the old man would sleep soft. Meanwhile her poor hands grew stiff and her fingers turned white, like the hands of poor people who wash clothes in a hole in the ice in winter. It is cold, the wind blows in their faces, the washing freezes as hard as a board, but there is nothing to be done, and the poor people go on working.

“Never mind,” said Moroz Ivanovich. “Just rub your fingers with snow, and they will be warm again. I am a kind old man, you know. Come and see what wonders I have.”

## IV · The Questions

He lifted up his snowy feather bed and the blanket, and Rukodelnitsa saw green grass pushing up beneath it. She felt sorry for the little blades of grass.

“You say you are a kind old man,” she said. “Then why do you keep the green grass under your snowy bed, and do not let it out into God's bright world?”

“I do not let it out because the time has not come yet. The grass is not strong enough. A good farmer sowed it in the autumn, and it sprouted. If it had grown tall, the winter would have caught it, and by summer it would not have ripened. So I covered the young green shoots with my snowy feather bed, and I lie on top of them myself, so that the wind does not blow the snow away. Then spring will come, and the snow bed will melt, and the grass will grow into ears of corn. After that the grain will come, and the farmer will gather it and take it to the mill. The miller will grind it into flour, and from the flour you, Rukodelnitsa, will bake your bread.”

“Tell me, Moroz Ivanovich,” said Rukodelnitsa, “why do you sit in the well?”

“I sit in the well because spring is coming,” said Moroz Ivanovich. “I begin to feel too warm. And you know that even in summer it is cool in a well. That is why the water is so cold, even in the hottest weather.”

“And why, Moroz Ivanovich,” asked Rukodelnitsa, “do you walk along the streets in winter and knock on the windows?”

“I knock on the windows,” answered Moroz Ivanovich, “so that people do not forget to light their stoves, and to close the chimney at the right time. There are careless people who light the stove, but forget to close the chimney, or close it too soon, while there are still hot embers glowing. Then the room fills with bad smoke, and people get headaches and feel dizzy. They can even become very ill. And I knock on the windows for another reason too. I want people to remember, when they are sitting in a warm room or putting on a warm coat, that there are poor children in the world who are cold in winter. They have no warm coats, and no money to buy firewood. So I knock on the window to remind people to help the poor.”

Then good Moroz Ivanovich stroked Rukodelnitsa on the head, and lay down to sleep on his snowy bed.

## V · Three Days

Meanwhile Rukodelnitsa tidied the whole house. She went to the kitchen and cooked the dinner. She mended the old man's coat and darned his shirts.

When the old man woke up, he was very pleased and thanked her. Then they sat down to dinner. The table was splendid, and best of all was the ice cream, which the old man had made himself.

So Rukodelnitsa lived with Moroz Ivanovich for three whole days.

On the third day Moroz Ivanovich said to her:

“Thank you, my clever girl. You have made an old man very happy, and I will not stay in your debt. You know that people are paid for their work. So here is your bucket, and I have put a whole handful of silver coins into it. And here, to remember me by, is a little diamond to pin your kerchief.”

Rukodelnitsa thanked him, pinned on the diamond, took her bucket, went back to the well, caught hold of the rope, and climbed out into God's bright world.

She had hardly begun to walk toward the house when the rooster, which she always fed, saw her. He was so glad that he flew up on the fence and crowed:

“Cock-a-doodle-doo, cock-a-doodle-doo! Rukodelnitsa's bucket is full of coins for you!”

When Rukodelnitsa came home and told everything that had happened to her, the nurse was amazed. Then she said:

## VI · Lenivitsa Goes

“There, Lenivitsa, you see what people get for good work. Go to the old man and serve him. Tidy his room, cook in his kitchen, mend his clothes and darn his shirts, and you too will earn a handful of coins. They will come in useful, because we have very little money for the holiday.”

Lenivitsa did not like the idea of going to work for the old man at all. But she did want the coins, and she wanted the diamond pin too.

So, following Rukodelnitsa's example, Lenivitsa went to the well, took hold of the rope, and plop, down she fell to the very bottom.

She looked, and there in front of her was a stove, and in the stove sat a little pie, so rosy and golden. It sat there looking about and saying:

“I am quite ready, I am nicely browned, with sugar and raisins I am crowned. Whoever takes me, with me shall go.”

And Lenivitsa answered him:

“Oh, yes, of course! I am not going to tire myself out, lifting a shovel and reaching into the stove. If you want to come out, jump out yourself.”

She went on. In front of her was a garden, and in the garden stood a tree, and on the tree hung golden apples. The little apples rustled their leaves and talked among themselves:

“We are apples, ripe and round. The roots of the tree feed us, and cold clear dew washes us. Whoever shakes us from the tree, will take us home with them.”

“Oh, yes, of course!” answered Lenivitsa. “I am not going to tire myself out, raising my little arms and pulling the branches. I shall pick you up when you fall down by yourselves.”

And Lenivitsa walked right past them. So she came to Moroz Ivanovich. The old man sat just as before, on his bench of ice, nibbling snowballs.

“What do you want, girl?” he asked.

“I have come to serve you,” answered Lenivitsa, “and to be paid for it.”

“You speak sensibly, girl,” answered the old man. “Work should be paid for. But let us see how good your work will be. Go and shake up my feather bed, then cook the dinner, and mend my clothes, and darn my shirts.”

## VII · The Dinner

Lenivitsa went off, and on the way she thought:

“Am I going to tire myself out and freeze my fingers? Maybe the old man will not notice, and will go to sleep on a bed that is not shaken up.”

And the old man really did not notice, or he pretended not to notice. He lay down and went to sleep, and Lenivitsa went to the kitchen.

She came to the kitchen and did not know what to do. She loved to eat, but she had never once wondered how dinner was made, and she was too lazy to look and see.

She looked around. Before her lay greens, and meat, and fish, and vinegar, and mustard, and sour kvass, everything in a row. She thought and thought. Then she cleaned the greens in a hurry, cut up the meat and the fish, and, so as not to give herself too much trouble, she put everything in one pot just as it was, washed or not washed: the greens, the meat, the fish, the mustard, the vinegar, and she even poured in some kvass. And she thought to herself: “Why should I tire myself out cooking each thing separately? It will all be together in the stomach anyway.”

Then the old man woke up and asked for his dinner. Lenivitsa dragged the pot over to him just as it was, without even laying a tablecloth. Moroz Ivanovich tasted it and made a face, and sand crunched between his teeth.

“You are a fine cook,” he said with a smile. “Let us see how your other work goes.”

Lenivitsa tasted the dinner and spat it out at once, she felt quite sick. The old man groaned a little, and then he began to cook himself, and made a splendid dinner, so splendid that Lenivitsa licked her fingers, eating the food somebody else had cooked.

After dinner the old man lay down to rest again, and reminded Lenivitsa that his coat was not mended and his shirts were not darned.

Lenivitsa sulked, but there was nothing to be done. She began to look through the clothes. And here was another trouble. She had worn clothes all her life, but she had never asked how they were sewn. She picked up a needle, pricked herself because she was not used to it, and threw it down.

And the old man, again, seemed to notice nothing. He called Lenivitsa to supper and then put her to bed.

And Lenivitsa was pleased. She thought to herself:

“Perhaps it will pass like this. It was silly of my sister to work so hard. The old man is kind, and he will give me the coins for nothing.”

## VIII · The Reward

On the third day Lenivitsa came to Moroz Ivanovich and asked him to let her go home and to pay her for her work.

“And what work was that?” asked the old man. “If we are going to speak the truth, you ought to pay me, because you did not work for me. I served you.”

“Oh, indeed!” answered Lenivitsa. “I lived with you for three whole days.”

“Do you know, my dear, what I shall tell you? Living in a place and serving in a place are not the same thing, and one kind of work is not the same as another. Remember that. It will be useful to you in the future. But still, if your conscience does not trouble you, I will pay you. As your work was, so your pay shall be.”

With these words Moroz Ivanovich gave Lenivitsa a very big bar of silver in one hand, and a very big diamond in the other. Lenivitsa was so delighted that she seized them both and ran home without even thanking the old man.

She came home and began to boast.

“Look what I have earned! Not a handful of little coins, like my sister, and not a tiny diamond, but a whole bar of silver, see how heavy it is, and a diamond almost as big as my fist! We can buy new clothes for the holiday with this...”

She had not finished speaking when the silver bar melted and ran down onto the floor. It was nothing but quicksilver, which had frozen in the great cold. At the same time the diamond began to melt too, and the rooster jumped on the fence and crowed out loud:

“Cock-a-doodle-doo, cock-a-doodle-doo! In Lenivitsa's hand is an icicle, that is all that is true!”

And now, my dear children, think about it and guess: what in this story is true and what is not true; what was said plainly and what was said slyly; what was a joke, what a lesson, and what a hint. And remember, too, that not every work and every good deed is rewarded. Sometimes the reward comes unexpectedly, because work and goodness are good in themselves and useful in everything. That is how God has arranged it. Only do not leave another person's goodness and work without a reward. And for now, your reward is learning and obedience.

And meanwhile, do not forget old Grandfather Irenei, who has prepared many more stories for you. Only let the old man gather his strength and his health for the spring.
