## I · The Queen and the Mirror

The Tsar kissed his queen goodbye
And rode away beneath the sky.
At the window, all alone,
She sat and waited, still as stone.
From dawn till dusk she watched the plain
Till her eyes ached, but all in vain.
No dear one came. There was only snow,
Whirling and swirling, to and fro,
Falling on the fields all through,
Till the whole wide world was white and new.

Nine months went by. On Christmas night
God sent a daughter, small and bright.
At dawn the Tsar came riding home,
After so long a time to roam.
The queen looked up at him, and sighed,
Too full of joy, and gently died.

The Tsar grieved long. But a year went by
Like an empty dream, and by and by
He married again. The new queen was fair,
Tall and slender, with shining hair,
Clever, too, and lovely to see.
But proud she was, as proud could be,
Wilful and jealous, and vain as well.

Among her treasures was a mirror,
A little magic mirror, that could talk.
To it alone she was kind and gay.
She joked with it in a friendly way,
And, smiling at herself, she'd say:
“Little mirror, tell me true,
Tell me all the truth, I pray:
Am I the fairest in the world,
The rosiest and whitest, say?”
“You, O queen, are the fairest, true.
None is rosier or whiter than you.”

Then the queen would laugh with glee,
Shrug her shoulders merrily,
Wink, and snap her fingers, and twirl,
Hands on her hips, in a proud little whirl,
Gazing and gazing into the glass.

But the young princess, quietly,
Grew and grew, as sweet as can be,
Till she bloomed, so fair and kind,
White of face, with dark brows, and gentle of mind.
A bridegroom soon was found for her:
Prince Yelisey, a king's own son.
The matchmaker came, the Tsar said yes,
And her wedding gifts were ready, no less:
Seven market towns, and more,
And a hundred and forty fine houses in store.

## II · Into the Forest

Before the wedding party, the queen
Dressed up in all her finest sheen,
And asked her mirror once again:
“Little mirror, tell me true,
Tell me all the truth, I pray:
Am I the fairest in the world,
The rosiest and whitest, say?”
“You are lovely, that is true,
But the princess is lovelier than you,
Rosier, and whiter, too.”

The queen leapt up in a dreadful rage,
Stamped her foot, and slapped the glass, and said:
“You wicked mirror! You lie to spite me!
How can she be fairer than I?
Look how she has grown! No wonder she is pale:
Her mother sat and stared at the snow all day!”

“But tell me, how can she be lovelier than I?
Say that I am the fairest,
In the whole kingdom, in the whole wide world!
Is it not so?”
But the mirror answered, calm and clear:
“The princess is still the fairest here,
Still the rosiest and the whitest.”

There was nothing to be done. The queen,
Full of dark envy, hot and keen,
Threw the mirror under a bench,
And called her maid, Chernavka, to her side.
She told her: “Take the princess deep
Into the forest, and leave her there alone,
Tied to a pine tree, far from home.”

Who can argue with an angry queen?
So Chernavka took the princess to the forest green,
And led her so far, and so deep, that at last
The princess guessed what she had in mind,
And was frightened, and begged:
“Dear Chernavka, what have I done?
Please do not harm me, kind one!
When I am queen, I will not forget.
I will thank you, you will see.”

But Chernavka loved her in her heart.
She did not tie her, nor do her harm.
She let her go, and said:
“Do not grieve. God be with you.”
And she went home. The queen cried out:
“Well? Where is that pretty girl?”
“She stands alone in the wood,” said Chernavka,
“Tied up tightly.
She will not suffer for long.”

Then the news rang out through the land:
The Tsar's daughter was lost! The poor Tsar grieved.
And Prince Yelisey said a prayer,
And rode away, to seek her everywhere:
His lovely bride, his own heart's friend,
Whom he would search for to the end.

## III · The House in the Wood

But the princess, lost, walked on and on
Through the forest, from the early dawn,
And at last she came to a house.
Out ran a dog, barking, then fell quiet,
And wagged his tail, and began to play.
She went in through the gate. The yard was still.

The dog ran after her, kind and glad.
She climbed the steps, and took the ring,
And the door swung softly open.
She found a bright room all around:
Benches covered with rugs, and in the corner
An oak table, and a stove with tiles.
She saw that good people lived here.
But no one was to be seen.
She tidied the whole house, and lit a candle,
And made the stove glow warm and bright,
Then climbed up on the sleeping bench,
And lay down quietly for the night.

The dinner hour came near. Hoofbeats rang
Out in the yard, and in came seven knights,
Seven brothers, rosy and bearded.
The eldest said:
“What a wonder! All is clean and bright.
Someone has tidied the house and waited for us.
Who is it? Come out, show yourself,
And be our friend, as is only right.
If you are an old man, be our uncle.
If a rosy lad, our brother true.
If an old lady, be our mother.
If a pretty maiden, be our sister, dear.”

The princess came down to them,
Bowed low, and, blushing, said she was sorry
To have come without being asked.
By her words, the knights knew at once
That she was a princess.
They sat her in the corner, and gave her a pie,
And poured her a cup, and she would not drink.
She broke the pie, and took a little bite,
And begged to rest, after her long way.
They led her up to a bright room,
And left her there, to sleep until the light.

## IV · The Seven Brothers

Day after day went by, flashing past,
And the young princess stayed in the forest,
And she was not lonely, with the seven knights.
Before the early dawn, the brothers
Rode out together, to see the fields,
To shoot the grey ducks, and ride, and play,
And the princess stayed at home, all day,
To cook, and sweep, and keep the house.
She never argued with them,
And they never argued with her.
So the days went by, one after another.

The brothers came to love the maiden.
One morning, at the break of day,
All seven came to her room.
The eldest said:
“Maiden, you are a sister to us all.
There are seven of us, and we all love you,
And each would gladly marry you.
But that cannot be. So, for heaven's sake,
Settle it between us:
Be the wife of one, and a kind sister to the rest.
Why do you shake your head?
Are you refusing us?”

The princess answered gently:
“Dear brothers, honest and good,
If I lie, may I not leave this place alive.
What can I do? I am promised.
To me you are all equal, brave and wise,
And I love you all with all my heart.
But I am promised to another, forever.
The dearest to me is Prince Yelisey.”

The brothers stood in silence,
And scratched their heads.
The eldest bowed and said:
“To ask is no sin. Forgive us.
If that is so, we will not ask again.”
“I am not angry,” she said softly.
“And my refusal is no fault.”
The suitors bowed, and quietly went away,
And all together, as before,
They went on living, day by day.

## V · The Old Beggar-Woman

Meanwhile the wicked queen
Could not forgive the princess.
She sulked at her mirror for a long time,
And then she remembered it,
Sat down before it, and her anger passed.
She preened herself again, and smiled, and said:
“Little mirror, tell me true,
Tell me all the truth, I pray:
Am I the fairest in the world,
The rosiest and whitest, say?”

“You are lovely, that is true,
But one lives far away from here,
In the green oak wood, with seven knights,
And she is lovelier than you.”
The queen flew at Chernavka in a rage:
“How dare you deceive me!”
Chernavka told her everything.
The wicked queen was very angry,
And she decided that, one way or another,
She would put an end to the princess.

One day the young princess,
Waiting for her dear brothers,
Was spinning, sitting by the window.
Suddenly, under the porch, the dog
Began to bark, and she saw a poor old nun
Walking in the yard, driving the dog off with a staff.
The princess called to her through the window:
“Wait a little, granny, wait!
I will scold the dog,
And I will bring you something to eat.”
The nun replied:
“Oh, my dear child!
The horrid dog has nearly eaten me.
Look how he fusses! Come out to me.”

The princess took some bread, and came out.
But the dog jumped in front of her feet,
Barking, and would not let her near the old woman.
He was fiercer than a wild beast.
The princess said:
“How strange! He must have slept badly.
Here, catch!”
And she threw the bread.
The old woman caught it, and said:
“Thank you, and God bless you.
And here is something for you. Catch!”
And a ripe golden apple flew
Straight toward the princess.
The dog leapt up and howled.
But the princess caught it in both hands.
The old woman said:
“Just to pass the time, my dear, eat the apple.
And thank you for the dinner.”
She bowed, and vanished.

The dog ran with the princess to the porch,
And looked sadly into her face, and howled,
As if his dog's heart ached,
As if he wanted to say: “Drop it!”
But she only petted him gently.

“What is it, Sokolko? What is wrong with you?
Lie down!”
She went in, quietly closed the door,
Sat down by the window with her spinning,
And waited for the knights.
But she kept looking at the apple.
It was full of ripe juice,
So fresh, so sweet, so rosy and golden,
As if filled with honey.
The seeds showed through.
She meant to wait until dinner, but she could not.
She took the apple in her hand,
Lifted it to her little red lips,
And took a gentle bite.
She swallowed a little piece.
Then suddenly she swayed, hardly breathing,
Her white hands dropped, the apple fell,
Her eyes closed, and she lay down,
With her head on the bench, under the icons,
And became quiet, and did not stir.

## VI · The Crystal Coffin

At that very time the brothers
Were riding home together.
The dog ran toward them, howling,
And showed them the way to the house.
The brothers said: “This is not good.
We shall not escape sorrow.”
They galloped up, and came in, and gasped.
The dog rushed to the apple, barking,
Swallowed it, fell down, and was gone.
The apple must have been poisoned.

The brothers bowed their heads
Before the sleeping princess,
In their sorrow, and with a prayer
They lifted her from the bench, and dressed her,
And wanted to bury her, but changed their minds.
She lay there so still and fresh,
As if under the wing of sleep,
Only she did not breathe.
They waited three days, but she did not wake.

Then, with a sad ceremony,
They laid her in a crystal coffin,
And carried it, all together,
To a hollow mountain, far away.
At midnight, they hung it carefully
On iron chains between six pillars,
And fenced it round with a grating.
Then the brothers bowed to the ground
Before their sleeping sister,
And the eldest said:
“Sleep in your coffin.
Because of someone's spite,
Your beauty has faded from the earth.
Heaven will receive your spirit.
We loved you, and kept you safe for your beloved.
You were given to no one,
Only to the coffin.”

That very day the wicked queen,
Hoping for good news,
Secretly took the mirror
And asked it, as before:
“Little mirror, tell me true,
Tell me all the truth, I pray:
Am I the fairest in the world,
The rosiest and whitest, say?”
And she heard in answer:
“You, O queen, are the fairest, true.
None is rosier or whiter than you.”

## VII · The Sun, the Moon and the Wind

Meanwhile Prince Yelisey
Rode all over the world, looking for his bride.
But she was nowhere to be found.
He wept bitterly.
And whoever he asked, they could not answer him.
Some laughed in his face, and some turned away.
At last the young man
Looked up to the red sun.

“Bright sun, our light! You walk the sky
The whole year round, and bring us spring from winter.
You see us all below.
Will you not answer me?
Have you seen anywhere in the world
The young princess? I am her bridegroom.”
The red sun answered:
“Dear friend, I have not seen the princess.
She is perhaps no longer alive.
But my neighbor, the moon,
May have met her, or seen her trace.”

Yelisey waited for the dark night,
In his sorrow. When the moon appeared,
He hurried after it, pleading:
“Moon, moon, my friend,
Golden horn!
You rise in the deep dark,
Round-faced, and bright-eyed,
And the stars love to look at you.
Will you not answer me?
Have you seen the young princess?
I am her bridegroom.”
The clear moon answered:
“Brother, I have not seen the lovely maiden.
I stand on guard only in my turn.
The princess must have passed me by.”
“What a pity!” said the prince.
The bright moon went on:
“Wait. Perhaps the wind knows about her.
He will help you. Go to him now.
Do not grieve. Goodbye.”

Yelisey did not lose heart.
He ran to the wind, crying out:
“Wind, wind! You are mighty!
You drive the flocks of clouds,
You stir the blue sea,
You blow everywhere in the wide world.
You fear no one, except God alone.
Will you not answer me?
Have you seen the young princess?
I am her bridegroom.”
The wild wind answered:
“Wait. Beyond the quiet river
There is a high mountain,
And in it a deep cave.
In that cave, in the sad dark,
A crystal coffin swings on chains
Between the pillars.
No footprints are to be seen
Around that empty place.
In that coffin lies your bride.”

## VIII · The Wedding

The wind ran on. The prince wept,
And went to that empty place,
To look at his lovely bride
Once more, at least.
He came to a steep mountain,
With a barren country all around,
And a dark entrance at its foot.
He hurried in. There, in the sad gloom,
The crystal coffin swung,
And in it the princess slept her long sleep.
He struck the coffin of his dear bride
With all his strength.

The coffin broke. The princess
Woke up at once, and looked about,
With wondering eyes,
And swaying on the chains, she sighed,
And said: “How long I have slept!”
She rose from the coffin.
And oh! They both wept for joy.
He took her in his arms,
And carried her out from the dark to the light.
They talked happily, and set off home,
And the news rang out:
The Tsar's daughter is alive!

At home, the wicked queen sat idle
Before her mirror, talking to it.
She said:
“Little mirror, tell me true,
Tell me all the truth, I pray:
Am I the fairest in the world,
The rosiest and whitest, say?”
And she heard in answer:
“You are lovely, there is no doubt.
But the princess is lovelier still,
Rosier, and whiter, too.”
The wicked queen jumped up,
Broke the mirror on the floor,
Ran straight to the door,
And met the princess.
Then such sorrow seized her
That the queen died.

As soon as she was laid to rest,
They held the wedding feast at once.
Yelisey married his bride,
And no one, since the world began,
Saw such a feast.
I was there, I drank mead and beer,
But only wet my moustache, I fear.
