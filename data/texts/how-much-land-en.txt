## I · Two Sisters

An elder sister came from the town to visit her younger sister in the country. The elder was married to a merchant in town, the younger to a peasant in the village. The sisters sat drinking tea and talking. The elder sister began to put on airs and to praise her life in town: how spaciously and cleanly she lived and went about, how she dressed her children, how sweetly she ate and drank, and how she went to drives, strolls and the theatre.

The younger sister was hurt, and she began to run down the merchant’s life and to raise up her own, the peasant’s.

“I would not trade my way of living for yours,” she said. “We may live plainly, but we know no fear. You live more neatly, but you either make a great deal in trade or lose everything. And as the proverb says: loss is profit’s big brother. It happens, too: rich today, and tomorrow you are going about under people’s windows. But our peasant business is surer: a peasant’s life is thin, but it is long. We shall never be rich, but we shall have enough to eat.”

The elder sister began to say:

“Enough to eat, indeed — with the pigs and the calves! No furnishings, no manners! However hard your husband works, you live in the dung, and so you will die, and your children will be the same.”

“Well, what of it?” says the younger. “That is how it is with us. But we live on firm ground, we bow to no one, we fear no one. And you in town live among temptations all the time; today it is well, and tomorrow the unclean one turns up — look, and he tempts your husband to cards, or to drink, or to some pretty girl. And everything goes to dust. Does that not happen?”

Pakhom, the master of the house, lay on the stove, listening to what the women were chattering about.

“That is true,” he said, “the real truth. From childhood our kind turn her over, Mother Earth, so foolishness does not get into our heads. Only one trouble — there is too little land! But if I had land enough, I would fear no one, not even the devil himself!”

The women finished their tea, chattered a little more about clothes, cleared away the dishes and lay down to sleep.

But the devil was sitting behind the stove and heard everything. He was pleased that the peasant’s wife had led her husband into boasting: he boasts that if only he had land, not even the devil could take him.

“Very well,” he thinks, “let us have a contest, you and I; I will give you plenty of land. With land I will take you.”

## II · Land of His Own

A small lady of the manor lived near the peasants. She had a hundred and twenty desyatinas of land. She had lived peacefully with the peasants before and had never wronged them. But a retired soldier hired himself to her as steward and began to plague the peasants with fines. However careful Pakhom was, either a horse strayed into the oats, or a cow wandered into the garden, or the calves got out into the meadows — and for everything there was a fine.

Pakhom paid, and scolded his household and beat them. And Pakhom took on a great deal of sin because of that steward during the summer. He was even glad when the cattle were back in the yard — though he was sorry for the fodder, at least there was no fear.

In winter a rumour went round that the lady was selling her land and that the keeper of the inn on the highway was arranging to buy it. When the peasants heard, they gasped. “Well,” they thought, “the land will go to the innkeeper, and he will torment us with fines worse than the lady. We cannot live without this land; we are all within her circle.” The peasants came to the lady as a commune and began to ask her not to sell to the innkeeper but to give it to them. They promised to pay more. The lady agreed. The peasants began to arrange to buy all the land together, as a commune; they gathered once and twice in assemblies — the matter would not come together. The unclean one was dividing them, and they could not agree at all. And the peasants resolved to buy separately, each as much as he could manage. The lady agreed to that as well. Pakhom heard that a neighbour had bought twenty desyatinas from the lady and that she had let him pay half the money over a number of years. Pakhom grew envious. “They will buy up all the land,” he thought, “and I shall be left with nothing.” He began to take counsel with his wife.

“People are buying, so we must buy too,” he said, “about ten desyatinas. Otherwise we cannot live: the steward has worn us out with fines.”

They thought over how to buy. They had a hundred roubles put by, and they sold a foal, and half their bees, and put their son out as a hired labourer, and borrowed some more from a brother-in-law, and so half the money was gathered.

Pakhom gathered the money, picked out a piece of land, fifteen desyatinas with a little wood, and went to the lady to bargain. He bargained for the fifteen desyatinas, struck hands on it and gave a deposit. They went to town, signed the deed of sale, he paid half the money and undertook to pay the rest in two years.

And Pakhom had land of his own. Pakhom borrowed seed and sowed the bought land; it bore well. In one year he paid off his debt to both the lady and his brother-in-law. And Pakhom became a landowner: he ploughed and sowed his own land, mowed hay on his own land, cut stakes from his own land and fed his cattle on his own land. When Pakhom drove out to plough his land, his for ever, or came to look at the young shoots and the meadows, he could not rejoice enough. The grass, it seemed to him, grew differently there, and the flowers that bloomed on it were quite different. Before, when he drove across this land, it was land like any land, but now it had become quite a special land.

## III · Cramped

So Pakhom lived, and was glad. All would have been well, only the peasants began to trample Pakhom’s grain and meadows. He asked them politely, but they would not stop: now the herdsmen let the cows into the meadows, now the horses came from the night pasture onto the grain. And Pakhom drove them off, and forgave them, and did not go to law; then he grew weary of it and began to complain to the district court. And he knew that the peasants did it from lack of room, not on purpose, but he thought: “I cannot let it pass, or they will trample everything down. They must be taught a lesson.”

He taught them by law once, he taught them twice; one was fined, then another. The peasant neighbours began to bear a grudge against Pakhom; now and then they even trampled on purpose. Someone got into the little wood one night and cut down ten lime-trees for bast. Pakhom drove through the wood — look, something showed white. He came up — the stripped poles lay thrown about and the stumps stuck up. If only he had cut the ones at the edge of the thicket and left one standing, but no, the villain had cleared them all in a row. Pakhom grew furious. “Ah,” he thought, “if only I could find out who did this; I would take my revenge on him.” He thought and thought who it was: “There is no one else,” he thought, “but Semka.” He went to Semka’s yard to search; he found nothing, they only quarrelled. And Pakhom became still more certain that Semyon had done it. He submitted a petition. They were summoned to court. They judged and judged — and acquitted the peasant: there was no evidence. Pakhom was still more offended; he quarrelled with the elder and with the judges.

“You,” he said, “side with thieves. If you lived by the truth yourselves, you would not acquit thieves.”

Pakhom quarrelled with the judges and with his neighbours. They even began to threaten him with the red cock. And Pakhom now had more room on the land, but less room among people.

And at that time a rumour went round that people were going off to new places. And Pakhom thought: “For myself there is no need to leave my land, but if some of our people went, we should have more room. I would take their land for myself and add it to my own; life would be better. As it is, it is all cramped.”

Once Pakhom was sitting at home, and a peasant passing by came in. They let the peasant stay the night, fed him, and got talking — where, they asked, was God bringing him from? The peasant said that he was coming from below, from beyond the Volga, where he had been working. Word by word, the peasant told how people were going there to settle. He told how their people had settled there, had been entered into the commune, and ten desyatinas a head had been cut out for them.

“And the land is such,” he said, “that when they sowed rye, the straw grew so high you could not see the horse, and so thick that five handfuls made a sheaf. One peasant,” he said, “came quite poor, with nothing but his hands, and now he has six horses and two cows.”

Pakhom’s heart took fire. He thought: “Why suffer here in the crowding when one can live well? I will sell the land and the yard here; there I will build and set up a whole farm with the money. And here in this crowding there is nothing but sin. Only I must find everything out for myself.”

He got ready for the summer and went. He sailed down the Volga by steamer as far as Samara, then went four hundred versts on foot. He reached the place. It was all exactly as described. The peasants live spaciously, ten desyatinas of land are cut out for each soul, and they are glad to take people into the commune. And if anyone has a little money, he can buy, besides his allotment, as much as he likes to hold for ever, at three roubles for the very best land; as much as you like can be bought!

Pakhom found out everything, returned home by autumn and began to sell everything. He sold his land at a profit, sold his yard, sold all his cattle, withdrew from the commune, waited for spring, and set out with his family for the new places.

## IV · A Thousand Roubles

Pakhom arrived at the new places with his family and was entered into a large village commune. He treated the elders to drink and put all his papers in order. They accepted Pakhom and cut out for him, for five souls, fifty desyatinas of allotment land in various fields, besides pasture. Pakhom built a house and acquired cattle. He now had three times as much land per soul alone as before. And the land bore grain. Life was ten times better than in the old place. He had plenty of ploughland and fodder. He could keep as many cattle as he liked.

At first, while he was building and settling in, Pakhom was pleased, but when he had settled in, even this land began to seem cramped. In the first year Pakhom sowed wheat on his allotment — it came up well. He took a liking to sowing wheat, but there was little allotment land. And what there was was not suitable. Wheat is sown there on feather-grass or fallow land. They sow it for a year or two and then leave it until feather-grass grows over it again. And there are many who want such land, and there is not enough for all. There are disputes over it too; the richer people want to sow it themselves, and the poor give it to merchants for their taxes. Pakhom wanted to sow more. The next year he went to a merchant and bought land for a year. He sowed more — it bore well; but it was far from the village — he had to haul it fifteen versts. He saw that the merchant-peasants round about lived on separate farms and were growing rich. “That is the thing,” thought Pakhom, “if only I too could buy land for ever and build a farm. Then everything would be within my own circle.” And Pakhom began to think how to buy land for ever.

So Pakhom lived for three years. He rented land and sowed wheat. The years were good, and the wheat bore well, and he had idle money. One could live and live, but Pakhom grew bored with buying land every year from other people and fussing over land: wherever there was a nice little piece of land, peasants would fly down at once and take it all; if he was not in time to buy, he had nothing to sow on. And in the third year he bought a pasture from the peasants jointly with a merchant; and they had already ploughed it, but the peasants went to law, so the work was lost. “If only I had land of my own,” he thought, “I should bow to no one, and there would be no sin.”

And Pakhom began to inquire where to buy land for ever. And he came across a peasant. Five hundred desyatinas had been bought by this peasant, but he had gone to ruin and was selling cheaply. Pakhom began to bargain with him. They talked and talked — agreed on fifteen hundred roubles, with half the money to wait. They had almost settled it, when a passing merchant happened to stop at Pakhom’s yard to feed his horses. They drank tea and talked. The merchant said that he was coming from the far Bashkirs. There, he said, he had bought five thousand desyatinas of land from the Bashkirs. And it had cost altogether a thousand roubles. Pakhom began to ask questions. The merchant told him.

“I only,” he said, “pleased the old men. I gave away about a hundred roubles’ worth of robes and carpets, and a chest of tea, and gave wine to those who drink. And I took it at twenty kopecks a desyatina.” He showed the deed of sale. “The land,” he said, “is along a river, and the steppe is all feather-grass.”

Pakhom began to ask how and what.

“The land,” said the merchant, “you could not walk round in a year: it is all Bashkir. And the people are simple, like sheep. You can take it almost for nothing.”

“Well,” thought Pakhom, “why should I buy five hundred desyatinas for my thousand roubles and take a debt on my neck besides? And there, for a thousand roubles, what shall I get into my hands!”

## V · The Bashkirs

Pakhom asked how to get there and, as soon as he had seen the merchant off, got ready to go himself. He left the house to his wife, got ready with a hired man and set out. They stopped in town, bought a chest of tea, gifts, wine — everything as the merchant had said. They travelled and travelled and covered five hundred versts. On the seventh day they came to a Bashkir encampment. It was all as the merchant had said. They all live in the steppe, by a river, in felt tents. They do not plough and do not eat bread. And in the steppe cattle graze, and horses in herds. Foals are tied up behind the tents, and twice a day the mares are driven to them; they milk the mares and make kumis from the milk. The women churn the kumis and make cheese, and the men know nothing but drinking kumis and tea, eating mutton and playing on pipes. They are all sleek and cheerful, and make holiday all summer. They are quite an unlettered people, and do not know Russian, but they are friendly.

As soon as they saw Pakhom, the Bashkirs came out of their tents and surrounded the guest. An interpreter was found. Pakhom told him that he had come about land. The Bashkirs were delighted, took Pakhom, led him into a fine tent, sat him on carpets, put down-filled cushions under him, sat around and began to entertain him with tea and kumis. They slaughtered a sheep and fed him mutton. Pakhom took the gifts out of the cart and began to give them to the Bashkirs. Pakhom gave the Bashkirs presents and shared out the tea. The Bashkirs were delighted. They chattered and chattered among themselves, then told the interpreter to speak.

“They tell me to say to you,” said the interpreter, “that they have taken a liking to you and that it is our custom to do a guest every kindness and to repay presents with presents. You have given us gifts; now say what of ours you would like, so that we may repay you.”

“What I liked most of all among you,” said Pakhom, “is the land. With us,” he said, “land is cramped, and the land is worn out, while you have a great deal of land and the land is good. I have never seen such.”

The interpreter passed it on. The Bashkirs talked and talked. Pakhom did not understand what they said, but he saw that they were cheerful, shouting something, laughing. Then they fell silent and looked at Pakhom, and the interpreter said:

“They tell me to say to you,” he said, “that in return for your kindness they are glad to give you as much land as you want. Just show it with your hand — whichever you show — it will be yours.”

They talked some more and began to argue about something. And Pakhom asked what they were arguing about. And the interpreter said:

“Some say that they must ask the elder about the land, and that without him it cannot be done. And others say that it can be done without him.”

## VI · The Price of a Day

The Bashkirs were arguing, when suddenly a man in a fox-fur cap came up. All fell silent and stood. And the interpreter said:

“That is the elder himself.”

At once Pakhom took out his best robe and presented it to the elder, and five pounds more of tea. The elder accepted it and sat down in the place of honour. And at once the Bashkirs began to tell him something. The elder listened and listened, nodded his head for them to be silent, and began to speak to Pakhom in Russian.

“Well,” he said, “it can be done. Take where you like. There is plenty of land.”

“How can I take as much as I want,” thinks Pakhom. “It must somehow be made firm. Otherwise they will say it is yours, and then take it away.”

“We thank you,” he said, “for your kind word. You have a great deal of land, and I need only a little. I only want to know which will be mine. It must somehow be measured off and made fast to me. For life and death are in God’s power. You, good people, give it, but when the time comes, your children may take it away.”

“You are right,” said the elder, “it can be made fast.”

Pakhom began to say:

“I heard that a merchant was here with you. You gave him a bit of land as well and made out a deed of sale; I should like the same.”

The elder understood everything.

“All that can be done,” he said. “We have a clerk too, and we will drive to town and put on all the seals.”

“And what will the price be?” said Pakhom.

“Our price is one: a thousand roubles a day.”

Pakhom did not understand.

“What sort of measure is a day? How many desyatinas will there be in it?”

“We do not know how to count that,” he said. “We sell by the day; as much as you can walk round in a day, that is yours, and the price of a day is a thousand roubles.”

Pakhom was astonished.

“But to walk round in a day,” he said, “there would be a great deal of land.”

The elder laughed.

“All yours!” he said. “Only one condition: if you do not come back in the day to the place from which you started, your money is lost.”

“And how,” said Pakhom, “is the place where I walk to be marked?”

“We will stand at the place you choose, we will stand there, and you go and make a circle; and take a spade with you and, where needed, make marks, dig a hole at the corners, lay turves in it, then we will go with a plough from hole to hole. Take whatever circle you like, only before the sun sets come back to the place from which you began. Whatever you walk round, all of it is yours.”

Pakhom was delighted. They resolved to set out early. They talked, drank more kumis, ate mutton, drank more tea; night came on. They laid Pakhom down to sleep on a feather bed, and the Bashkirs dispersed. They promised to gather tomorrow at dawn and to ride to the place before sunrise.

## VII · The Dream

Pakhom lay down on the feather beds, and could not sleep, thinking all the time of the land. “I shall seize a great tract,” he thinks. “I shall walk round fifty versts in the day. A day now is like a year; in fifty versts, what a lot of land there will be. The worse part I shall sell or let to peasants, and the best I shall choose and settle on myself. I shall get two ploughs with oxen, hire two labourers; I shall plough some fifty desyatinas, and on the rest I shall pasture cattle.”

Pakhom did not sleep all night. Only before dawn did he doze off. Hardly had he dozed off when he saw a dream. He sees that he is lying, as it were, in this very tent, and hears someone guffawing outside. And he wanted to see who it was that was laughing, and he got up, went out of the tent and sees — the very same Bashkir elder sitting in front of the tent, clutching his belly with both hands, rocking with laughter, guffawing at something. He went up and asked: “What are you laughing at?” And he sees that it is not the Bashkir elder but the merchant of the other day, who had stopped at their place and told about the land. And he had only asked the merchant: “Have you been here long?” — and it was no longer the merchant, but that very peasant who had come from below, back at the old place. And Pakhom sees that it is not even the peasant, but the devil himself, with horns and hooves, sitting and roaring with laughter, and before him lies a man barefoot, in a shirt and trousers. And Pakhom, as it were, looked more closely: what man was this? And he sees that the man is dead and that it is himself. Pakhom was horrified and woke up. He woke up. “What things one dreams,” he thinks. He looked around; through the open door he sees it is already growing white, dawn is beginning. “I must wake the people,” he thinks, “it is time to go.” Pakhom got up, woke the hired man in the cart, told him to harness, and went to wake the Bashkirs.

“It is time,” he said, “to drive out to the steppe and measure.”

The Bashkirs got up, all gathered, and the elder came. The Bashkirs began to drink kumis again and wanted to treat Pakhom to tea, but he would not wait.

“If we are going, let us go,” he said, “it is time.”

## VIII · The Long Walk

The Bashkirs gathered, mounted — some on horseback, some in carts — and set out. And Pakhom with his hired man went in their own little cart, and took a spade with them. They came to the steppe; dawn was breaking. They drove up onto a small mound, in Bashkir a shikhan. They climbed down from the carts, got off their horses and gathered in a group. The elder came up to Pakhom and showed with his hand.

“Here,” he said, “all ours, as far as the eye can see. Choose any.”

Pakhom’s eyes kindled: the land was all feather-grass, flat as a palm, black as poppy-seed, and where there was a hollow, there were mixed grasses, grass breast-high.

The elder took off his fox-fur cap and set it on the ground.

“Here,” he said, “will be the mark. Go from here, come back here. Whatever you walk round will be yours.”

Pakhom took out his money, put it on the cap, took off his coat, stayed in his short jacket, tied his sash tighter under his belly, pulled himself together; he put a little bag of bread inside his shirt, tied the flask of water to his sash, pulled up his boot-tops, took the spade from the hired man and got ready to go. He thought and thought which way to take — everywhere was good. He thinks: “It is all the same: I shall go toward the sunrise.” He stood facing the sun, limbered up, waiting for it to show itself over the edge. He thinks: “I shall let no time slip by. In the cool it is easier to walk.” As soon as the sun flashed out over the edge, Pakhom threw the spade onto his shoulder and went out into the steppe.

Pakhom went neither slowly nor fast. He went off about a verst; stopped, dug a hole and laid turves one on another, so that it would be more noticeable. He went on. He began to limber up, began to quicken his step. He went farther and dug another hole.

Pakhom looked back. In the sun the shikhan was plain to see, and the people standing, and the tires on the wheels of the carts shone. Pakhom guessed that he had gone five versts. He began to get warm, took off his jacket, threw it over his shoulder, went on. He went another five versts. It grew warm. He glanced at the sun — it was already time for breakfast.

“One stretch is done,” thinks Pakhom. “And there are four in a day; it is too early to turn. Just let me take off my boots.” He sat down, took off his boots, tucked them in his sash, went on. Walking became easy. He thinks: “Let me go another five versts or so, then I shall begin to turn left. The place is very good, it would be a pity to leave it out. The farther, the better.” He went on straight ahead. He looked back — the shikhan was barely visible, and the people on it showed black like ants, and something gleamed faintly.

“Well,” thinks Pakhom, “I have taken enough in this direction; I must turn. And I am in a sweat — I am thirsty.” He stopped, dug a bigger hole, laid turves, untied the flask, drank and turned sharply left. He went and went; the grass became tall, and it was hot.

Pakhom began to tire; he looked at the sun and saw it was just midday. “Well,” he thinks, “I must rest.” Pakhom stopped and sat down. He ate a little bread with water, but did not lie down: he thought — if you lie down, you will fall asleep. He sat a little while and went on. At first he went easily. The food had added to his strength. But it had become very hot, and sleep began to bend him down; yet he kept going, thinking — endure for an hour, live for a lifetime.

He went on a good way on this side too, and was just about to turn left, when look — a damp hollow came up; he was sorry to leave it. He thinks: “Flax would come up well here.” Again he went straight. He took in the hollow, dug a hole beyond the hollow, and turned the second corner. Pakhom looked back at the shikhan: from the heat it had misted over, something wavered in the air, and through the haze the people on the shikhan could barely be seen — it would be fifteen versts to them. “Well,” thinks Pakhom, “I have taken long sides; I must make this one shorter.” He went along the third side, began to quicken his step. He looked at the sun — it was already coming to the time of the afternoon meal, and on the third side he had gone only two versts in all. And to the place it was still the same fifteen versts. “No,” he thinks, “even if the plot is crooked, I must make straight for it in time. I must not take too much. And there is already a great deal of land.” Pakhom dug a hole quickly and turned straight toward the shikhan.

## IX · Sunset

Pakhom goes straight toward the shikhan, and it was already hard for him. He was in a sweat, and had cut and bruised his bare feet, and his legs began to give way. He longed to rest, but it was impossible — he would not get there before sunset. The sun does not wait; it goes down and down. “Ah,” he thinks, “have I not made a mistake, have I not taken too much? What if I am not in time?” He looks ahead at the shikhan, he looks at the sun: the place is far off, and the sun is already not far from the edge.

So Pakhom goes, it is hard for him, and still he quickens and quickens his step. He went and went — it was still far; he broke into a trot. He threw away his jacket, his boots, the flask, threw away his cap, only held the spade and leaned on it. “Ah,” he thinks, “I was greedy, I have ruined everything, I shall not run there before sunset.” And fear made it still worse for him to catch his breath. Pakhom runs, his shirt and trousers stick to his body with sweat, his mouth is dry. His chest swells like a blacksmith’s bellows, and his heart beats like a hammer, and his legs, as if not his own, give way. Pakhom grew frightened and thought: “I may die of the strain.”

He is afraid of dying, but cannot stop. “After running so far,” he thinks, “to stop now — they will call me a fool.” He ran and ran, was coming close now, and hears the Bashkirs shrieking and shouting at him, and at their shouting his heart burns still more. Pakhom runs with his last strength, and the sun is already coming to the edge, it has gone into the mist: large, red, bloody. Any moment it will begin to set. The sun is near, and the place is not very far now. Pakhom already sees the people on the shikhan waving their arms at him, urging him on. He sees the fox-fur cap on the ground and the money on it; he sees the elder too, sitting on the ground, holding his belly with his hands. And Pakhom remembered the dream. “There is a great deal of land,” he thinks, “but will God bring me to live on it? Oh, I have ruined myself,” he thinks, “I shall not run there.”

Pakhom looked at the sun, and it had reached the earth, was already beginning to go under with its rim, and was cut out in an arc at the edge. Pakhom put on speed with his last strength, threw his body forward, his legs barely managing to catch him so that he would not fall. Pakhom ran up to the shikhan, and suddenly it grew dark. He looked back — the sun had already set. Pakhom gasped. “My labours are lost,” he thinks. He was about to stop, but hears the Bashkirs still shouting, and remembered that from below it seems to him that the sun has set, but from the shikhan the sun has not yet set. Pakhom drew himself up and ran up onto the shikhan. On the shikhan it was still light. Pakhom ran up, sees the cap. Before the cap sits the elder, guffawing, holding his belly with his hands. Pakhom remembered the dream, gasped, his legs gave way, and he fell forward, reaching the cap with his hands.

“Ah, well done!” cried the elder. “You have taken a great deal of land!”

Pakhom’s hired man ran up, wanted to lift him, but blood was running from his mouth, and he lay dead.

The Bashkirs clicked their tongues and pitied him.

The hired man picked up the spade, dug a grave for Pakhom — exactly as much as he had covered from his feet to his head — three arshins, and buried him.
