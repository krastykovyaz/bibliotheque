## I · The Forester's Lessons

My servant, cook and hunting companion, the forester Yarmola, came into the room bent under a bundle of firewood, threw it down on the floor with a crash, and blew on his frozen fingers.

“Phew, what a wind there is outside, young master,” he said, squatting down in front of the stove door. “We must get the stove good and hot. Give me a splinter, young master.”

“So we shan't go after hares tomorrow, eh? What do you think, Yarmola?”

“No... it can't be done... just listen to that blizzard. The hare lies low now and never so much as squeaks... Tomorrow you won't find a single track.”

Fate had thrown me for a whole six months into a remote little village in the province of Volhynia, on the edge of Polesye, and hunting was my only occupation and pleasure. I confess that when I was offered the trip to the country, I never thought I should be so unbearably bored. I even went gladly. “Polesye... the backwoods... the bosom of nature... simple ways... primitive characters,” I thought, sitting in the railway carriage, “a people quite unknown to me, with strange customs and a language of their own... and surely what a number of poetic legends, traditions and songs!” And at that time (if I am to tell the story, I may as well tell it all) I had already managed to squeeze into one little newspaper a tale with two murders and one suicide, and I knew in theory that it is useful for writers to observe the ways of the people.

But... either the peasants of Perebrod were distinguished by some special, obstinate unsociability, or I did not know how to go about it, and my relations with them were limited to this: seeing me, they took off their caps from afar, and, drawing level with me, they said glumly, “Hai bug,” which was meant to stand for “God help you.” And when I tried to get into conversation with them, they looked at me in astonishment, declined to understand the simplest questions, and kept trying to kiss my hands, an old custom left over from Polish serfdom.

The few books I had I very soon read through. Out of boredom (although at first it seemed unpleasant to me) I made an attempt to get acquainted with the local intelligentsia, in the persons of the priest who lived fifteen versts away, his “Mr. Organist,” the local constable, and the clerk of the neighbouring estate, a retired sergeant, but nothing came of it.

Then I tried my hand at doctoring the people of Perebrod. I had at my disposal castor oil, carbolic acid, boric acid and iodine. But here, besides my scanty knowledge, I ran into a complete impossibility of making a diagnosis, because the symptoms of all my patients were always the same: “it hurts in the middle,” and “I can't eat or drink.”

An old peasant woman, for example, comes to me. Having wiped her nose with a shamefaced air with the forefinger of her right hand, she takes a pair of eggs from inside her bodice, so that for a second I see her brown skin, and lays them on the table. Then she begins to catch at my hands to imprint a kiss on them. I hide my hands and urge the old woman: “Come, granny... stop that... I am not a priest... it isn't proper for me... What hurts you?”

“It hurts in the middle, young master, right in the very middle, so that I can't even drink or eat.”

“Has it been so for long?”

“How should I know?” she answers with a question of her own. “It just burns and burns. I can't drink and I can't eat.”

And however I struggle, no more definite symptoms of the illness are to be found.

“Don't you worry,” the clerk, the old sergeant, once advised me, “they'll cure themselves. It'll dry up, like a sore on a dog. I'll tell you, I use only one medicine: sal ammoniac. A peasant comes to me. ‘What do you want?’ ‘I'm sick,’ he says... I put a bottle of smelling-salts under his nose at once. ‘Sniff!’ He sniffs... ‘Sniff again... harder!’ He sniffs... ‘Well, is it easier?’ ‘It seems to have eased a bit...’ ‘Then go with God.’”

Besides, I found this kissing of hands repugnant (and some positively fell at my feet and strained with all their might to kiss my boots). It was by no means a movement of a grateful heart but simply a loathsome habit, grafted on by centuries of slavery and violence. And I could only wonder at that same clerk, the old sergeant, and at the constable, seeing with what unruffled gravity they thrust their enormous red paws to the peasants' lips...

Only hunting was left to me. But at the end of January such weather set in that hunting became impossible. A terrible wind blew every day, and in the night a hard, icy crust of frozen snow formed on the surface, over which the hare ran without leaving tracks. Sitting shut up indoors and listening to the howling of the wind, I pined dreadfully. Naturally I seized greedily on so innocent an amusement as teaching the forester Yarmola to read and write.

It began, however, in a rather original way. I was writing a letter one day and suddenly felt that someone was standing behind my back. Turning round, I saw Yarmola, who had come up, as always, soundlessly in his soft bast shoes.

“What do you want, Yarmola?” I asked.

“Why, I wonder at how you write. If only I could do the same... No, no... not as you do,” he hurried on in confusion, seeing that I was smiling. “Only my own surname...”

“What do you need it for?” I wondered... (It must be noted that Yarmola is considered the poorest and laziest peasant in the whole of Perebrod; he drinks away his wages and his peasant earnings; nowhere in the neighbourhood are there such bad oxen as his. In my opinion, he could in no circumstances have any need of literacy.) I asked once more, doubtfully: “But why must you be able to write your surname?”

“Well, you see how it is, young master,” Yarmola answered with extraordinary gentleness, “there isn't one literate man in our village. When some paper has to be signed, or there's business at the district office, or anything... nobody can. The elder only puts the seal on, and doesn't know himself what's printed in it... It would be good for everybody if someone could sign his name.”

This concern of Yarmola's, a notorious poacher and careless vagabond, whose opinion the village assembly would never even have thought of taking into account, this concern of his for the public interest of his native village somehow touched me. I myself offered to give him lessons. And what heavy work it was, all my attempts to teach him to read and write with understanding! Yarmola, who knew every path of his forest to perfection, almost every tree, who could find his bearings day or night in any place whatever, who distinguished by their tracks all the wolves, hares and foxes of the neighbourhood, this very Yarmola could not by any means grasp why, for example, the letters “m” and “a” together make “ma.” As a rule he brooded over such a problem painfully for ten minutes or more, and his swarthy, thin face with its sunken black eyes, all lost in a stiff black beard and large moustache, expressed the extreme of mental strain.

“Come, say it, Yarmola, ‘ma.’ Just say ‘ma,’” I pestered him. “Don't look at the paper, look at me, like this. Come, say ‘ma’...”

Then Yarmola would sigh deeply, lay the pointer on the table and say sadly and resolutely:

“No... I can't...”

“How can't you? It's so easy. Just say ‘ma,’ as I do.”

“No... I can't, young master... I've forgotten...”

All methods, devices and comparisons were shattered against this monstrous obtuseness. But Yarmola's thirst for enlightenment did not weaken at all.

“Only my surname!” he begged me bashfully. “I need nothing more. Only the surname: Yarmola Popruzhuk, and nothing more.”

Having given up altogether the idea of teaching him to read and write intelligently, I began to teach him to sign his name mechanically. To my great surprise, this method turned out to be the most accessible to Yarmola, so that by the end of the second month we had very nearly mastered the surname. As for the first name, in order to make the task easier we decided to drop it altogether.

In the evenings, having finished stoking the stoves, Yarmola waited impatiently for me to call him.

“Well, Yarmola, let's study,” I would say.

He came up to the table sideways, propped himself on his elbows, thrust the pen between his black, hardened, unbending fingers, and asked me, raising his eyebrows:

“Write?”

“Write.”

Yarmola drew the first letter quite confidently, the “P” (this letter was known among us as “two posts and a crossbeam on top”); then he looked at me questioningly:

“Why don't you write? Have you forgotten?”

“Forgotten...” Yarmola shook his head in vexation.

“Ah, what a fellow you are! Well, put the wheel.”

“Ah! The wheel, the wheel!.. I know...” Yarmola brightened up and carefully drew on the paper a figure stretched upward, very like the Caspian Sea in outline. When he had finished this labour, he admired it for a while in silence, tilting his head now to the left, now to the right, and screwing up his eyes.

“Why have you stopped? Write on.”

“Wait a little, young master... in a moment.”

For two minutes he pondered and then timidly asked:

“The same as the first?”

“Right. Write.”

So little by little we reached the last letter, the “k” (we rejected the hard sign), which was known to us as “a stick, and in the middle of the stick a crooked thing with its tail to one side.”

“And what do you think, young master,” Yarmola would say sometimes when he had finished his work and was looking at it with loving pride, “if I could study another five or six months, I should know it quite well. What do you say?”

## II · The Witch of the Marsh

Yarmola squatted in front of the stove door, stirring the coals in the stove, and I walked up and down along the diagonal of my room. Of all the twelve rooms of the enormous manor house I occupied only one, the former sitting-room. The others stood locked with a key, and in them old damask furniture, curious bronzes and portraits of the century mouldered motionless and solemn.

The wind outside the house raged like an old, chilled, naked devil. In its roar one heard groans, squeals and wild laughter. Towards evening the blizzard spread out still more strongly. Outside, someone was furiously flinging handfuls of fine dry snow against the window panes. The nearby forest muttered and hummed with a continuous, hidden, hollow menace...

The wind crept into the empty rooms and into the howling chimneys, and the old house, all rickety, full of holes, half collapsed, suddenly came alive with strange sounds, to which I listened with involuntary alarm. Now something seemed to sigh in the white hall, sigh deeply, brokenly, sadly. Now the dry, rotten floorboards began to move and creak somewhere far away under someone's heavy, noiseless steps. Then it seems to me that next to my room, in the corridor, someone is carefully and insistently pressing on the door handle, and then, suddenly enraged, rushes through the whole house, furiously shaking all the shutters and doors, or, crawling into a chimney, whines so plaintively, drearily and unceasingly, now raising its voice higher and thinner to a pitiful squeal, now dropping it down to an animal growl.

At times God knows from where this dreadful guest burst into my room too, ran a sudden chill down my spine and made the flame of the lamp waver, which burned dimly under its green paper shade, scorched at the top.

A strange, vague uneasiness came over me. Here I am, I thought, sitting on a dark and stormy winter night in a decrepit house, in the midst of a village lost in forests and snowdrifts, hundreds of versts from town life, from society, from a woman's laughter, from human talk... And I began to imagine that this stormy evening would drag on for years and tens of years, would drag on until my death, and the wind would roar in just this way outside the windows, the lamp would burn just as dimly under its wretched green shade, I should walk to and fro in my room just as anxiously, and by the stove would sit, just the same, the silent, absorbed Yarmola, a strange creature, alien to me, indifferent to everything in the world: both to the fact that at home his family has nothing to eat, and to the raging of the wind, and to my vague, gnawing misery.

I suddenly longed unbearably to break this wearisome silence with some semblance of a human voice, and I asked:

“What do you think, Yarmola, where does such a wind come from today?”

“The wind?” Yarmola responded, lazily raising his head. “Doesn't the young master know?”

“Of course I don't. How should I know?”

“You really don't know?” Yarmola suddenly perked up. “I'll tell you this,” he went on with a mysterious shade in his voice: “I'll tell you this: either a witch has been born, or a wizard is holding a feast.”

“A ‘vedmaka’ is a sorceress in your language?”

“Just so, just so... a sorceress.”

I fell on Yarmola greedily. “Who knows,” I thought, “perhaps I shall manage to squeeze out of him at once some interesting story connected with witchcraft, with buried treasure, with werewolves?..”

“Well, and do you have witches here in Polesye?” I asked.

“I don't know... Maybe there are,” Yarmola answered with the same indifference, and bent to the stove again. “Old people say there once were... Maybe it's not true...”

I was disappointed at once. A characteristic trait of Yarmola was an obstinate taciturnity, and I no longer hoped to get anything more out of him on this interesting subject. But, to my surprise, he suddenly began to speak with lazy carelessness, and as if addressing not me but the humming stove:

“There was such a witch among us five years ago... Only the lads drove her out of the village!”

“Where did they drive her?”

“Where!.. To the forest, of course... Where else? And they pulled down her hut, so that not a chip should be left of that accursed nest... And she herself they led out beyond the village gate and gave her a clout on the neck.”

“Why did they treat her so?”

“There was a lot of harm from her: she quarrelled with everybody, she poured her potions under people's huts, she tied twists in the rye... Once she asked our young wife for a zlot (fifteen kopecks). The woman says to her: ‘I have no zlot, leave me alone.’ ‘Well, good,’ she says, ‘you'll remember how you wouldn't give me a zlot...’ And what do you think, young master: from that very time the woman's child began to sicken. It ailed and ailed and then died altogether. Then the lads drove the witch out, may her eyes drop out...”

“Well, and where is this witch now?” I went on with my curiosity.

“The witch?” Yarmola repeated slowly, in his usual manner. “How should I know?”

“Didn't she leave some kin in the village?”

“No, none. She was a stranger, from the Muscovites or the gypsies... I was still a little lad when she came to our village. And a girl was with her: a daughter or a granddaughter... They drove out both...”

“And now does nobody go to her, to have their fortune told or to ask for some potion?”

“The women run to her,” Yarmola dropped contemptuously.

“Aha! So it is known, after all, where she lives?”

“I don't know... People say she lives somewhere near Bisov Kut. You know, the marsh beyond the Irinovka road. So in that marsh she sits, the fever take her mother!”

“A witch living some ten versts from my house... a real, living Polesye witch!” This thought at once interested and excited me.

“Listen, Yarmola,” I turned to the forester, “how might I make the acquaintance of this witch?”

“Faugh!” Yarmola spat in indignation. “What a treasure to find.”

“Treasure or no treasure, I shall go to her all the same. As soon as it gets a little warmer, I shall set out at once. You will go with me, of course?”

The last words struck Yarmola so much that he even jumped up from the floor.

“I?!” he exclaimed in indignation. “Not on any account! Let there be God knows what there, but I won't go.”

“Come, nonsense, you'll go.”

“No, young master, I won't go... not on any account... That I?!” he exclaimed again, seized by a new surge of resentment. “That I should go to the witch's nest? May God defend me. And I don't advise you either, master.”

“As you like... but I shall go all the same. I am very curious to see her.”

“There's nothing curious there,” Yarmola grumbled, slamming the stove door crossly.

An hour later, when he had cleared away the samovar and drunk his tea in the dark entry and was getting ready to go home, I asked:

“What is this witch's name?”

“Manuilikha,” Yarmola answered with rough gloom.

Although he never expressed his feelings, he seemed to have become very attached to me; attached by our common passion for hunting, by my simple manner, by the help I occasionally gave to his perpetually hungry family, and chiefly because I alone in the world did not reproach him for his drinking, which Yarmola could not bear. And so my resolve to make the acquaintance of the witch put him in an abominable frame of mind, which he expressed only by heavy snorting and by this: going out onto the porch, he kicked his dog, Ryabchik, in the side with all his might. Ryabchik squealed desperately and sprang aside, but at once ran after Yarmola, not ceasing to whimper.

## III · The Cabin on Hen's Legs

Three days later it grew warmer. One morning, very early, Yarmola came into my room and announced casually:

“The guns must be cleaned, young master.”

“What for?” I asked, stretching under the blanket.

“The hare walked about a lot in the night: plenty of tracks. Perhaps we'll go out on a hunt?”

I saw that Yarmola was impatient to be off to the forest, but hid this passionate desire of the hunter under a pretended indifference. Indeed, in the hall his single-barrelled gun was already standing, from which not one snipe had yet escaped, in spite of the fact that near the muzzle it was adorned with several pewter patches laid on at the places where rust and powder gases had eaten through the iron.

No sooner had we entered the forest than we struck a hare's track: two paws side by side and two behind, one after the other. The hare had come out onto the road, gone along it for some two hundred yards, and made an enormous leap from the road into a young pine wood.

“Well, now we'll go round him,” said Yarmola. “Having made that leap, he'll lie down right there. You, young master, go...” He pondered, working out by some signs known to him alone where to send me... “You go to the old tavern. And I'll go round him from Zamlyn. As soon as the dog drives him out, I'll holler to you.”

And he vanished at once, as if he had dived into the thick scrub. I listened. Not a single sound betrayed his poacher's gait, not a twig cracked under his feet shod in bast shoes.

I walked unhurriedly to the old tavern, an uninhabited, tumbledown hut, and stood at the edge of the pine forest under a tall pine with a straight bare trunk. It was as quiet as it can only be in a forest in winter on a windless day. The heavy lumps of snow hanging on the branches pressed them downward, giving them a wonderful, festive and cold look. From time to time a thin twig broke from a top, and one heard very distinctly how, falling, it touched other branches with a light crackle. The snow was pink in the sun and blue in the shade. I was overcome by the quiet enchantment of this solemn, cold silence, and it seemed to me that I could feel time slowly and noiselessly passing by me...

Suddenly, far off, in the very thick of the wood, rang out the barking of Ryabchik, the characteristic bark of a dog following game: thin, ringing and nervous, almost passing into a squeal. At once I heard also the voice of Yarmola, shouting fiercely after the dog: “U-by! U-by!” the first syllable in a long sharp falsetto and the second in an abrupt bass note (only long afterwards did I find out that this Polesye hunting cry comes from the verb “to kill”).

It seemed to me, judging by the direction of the barking, that the dog was driving to the left of me, and I ran hurriedly across a clearing to cut the animal off. But before I had made twenty paces, an enormous grey hare leaped out from behind a stump and, as if in no hurry, laying its long ears back, crossed the road in high, rare bounds and vanished into the young growth. After him Ryabchik burst out headlong. Seeing me, he waved his tail weakly, bit hurriedly at the snow a few times, and drove the hare on again.

Yarmola suddenly dived out of the thicket just as noiselessly.

“Why didn't you stand in his way, young master?” he cried, and clicked his tongue reproachfully.

“But it was far... more than two hundred paces.”

Seeing my confusion, Yarmola softened.

“Well, never mind... He won't get away from us. Go to the Irinovka road: he'll come out there directly.”

I went in the direction of the Irinovka road, and two minutes later I heard the dog driving again somewhere not far from me. Seized with hunting excitement, I ran, holding my gun at the ready, through the thick brushwood, breaking branches and paying no heed to their cruel blows. I ran like this for quite a long time and was beginning to be out of breath, when suddenly the dog's barking stopped. I went more slowly. It seemed to me that if I went straight on I should certainly meet Yarmola on the Irinovka road. But soon I became convinced that during my run, skirting bushes and stumps and thinking nothing of the way, I had lost myself. Then I began to shout to Yarmola. He did not answer.

Meanwhile I went on mechanically, farther and farther. The forest thinned little by little, the ground sank and became tussocky. The track left in the snow by my foot quickly darkened and filled with water. Several times I had already fallen in up to my knees. I had to leap from hummock to hummock; in the thick brown moss that covered them my feet sank as in a soft carpet.

The brushwood soon ended altogether. Before me lay a large round marsh, drifted over with snow, from under whose white sheet rare hummocks stuck up. At the opposite end of the marsh, between the trees, the white walls of some hut peeped out. “Probably the Irinovka forester lives here,” I thought. “I must go in and ask him the way.”

But to reach the hut was not so easy. Every minute I stuck in the bog. My boots took in water and squelched loudly at every step; it became unbearable to drag them after me.

At last I got across this marsh, climbed a small knoll, and could now see the hut well. It was not even a hut but precisely the fairy-tale cabin on hen's legs. Its floor did not touch the ground; it was built on piles, probably because of the spring flood which inundates the whole of the Irinovka forest. But one side of it had settled with time, and this gave the cabin a lame and mournful look. In the windows several panes were missing; they were replaced by some dirty rags, bulging out like humps.

I pressed the latch and opened the door. It was very dark in the hut, and since I had been looking at the snow for so long, violet circles swam before my eyes; so for a long time I could not make out whether anyone was in the hut.

“Hey, good people, who is at home?” I asked loudly.

Something stirred near the stove. I went closer and saw an old woman sitting on the floor. Before her lay an enormous heap of hen's feathers. The old woman took each feather separately, stripped the barbs from it and put the down into a basket, while the quills she threw straight onto the ground.

“Why, that is Manuilikha, the Irinovka witch,” flashed through my head as soon as I looked more attentively at the old woman. All the features of Baba Yaga, as folk epic portrays her, were there: the thin cheeks, sunk inward, passed below into a sharp, long, flabby chin, almost touching the hanging nose; the fallen-in toothless mouth moved incessantly, as if chewing something; the faded, once-blue eyes, cold, round, protruding, with very short red lids, looked like the eyes of some unheard-of, sinister bird.

“Good day, granny!” I said as pleasantly as I could. “Are you not called Manuilikha?”

In answer something bubbled and rattled in the old woman's chest; then from her toothless, mumbling mouth escaped strange sounds, now like the choking croak of an old crow, now suddenly passing into a hoarse, breaking falsetto:

“Once, maybe, good people called me Manuilikha... And now they call me ‘called,’ and they honour me as ‘duck.’ What do you want?” she asked unfriendly, and without stopping her monotonous occupation.

“Why, granny, I have lost my way. Perhaps you have some milk?”

“There's no milk,” the old woman snapped angrily. “Many of you tramp through the forest... You can't water and feed them all...”

“Well, granny, you are not very kind to guests.”

“And true, my dear: not kind at all. We keep no delicacies for you. If you're tired, sit down, nobody is driving you out of the hut. You know how the proverb says: ‘Come and sit on our bank of earth, listen to the bells of our holiday, and as for dining at yours, we shall guess that ourselves.’ So it is...”

These turns of speech convinced me at once that the old woman was indeed an incomer in this region; here they neither like nor understand the sharp speech, strewn with rare words, which the northern gossip so readily shows off. Meanwhile the old woman, mechanically continuing her work, still muttered something under her breath, but ever quieter and more indistinctly. I made out only separate words which had no connection with one another: “So here's granny Manuilikha for you... And who is she, no one knows... My years are not few... She dances about on her legs, chatters, hops, a perfect magpie...”

For some time I listened in silence, and the sudden thought that before me was a mad woman aroused in me a feeling of squeamish fear.

However, I managed to look around me. The greater part of the cabin was taken up by an enormous peeling stove. There were no icons in the front corner. On the walls, instead of the usual huntsmen with green moustaches and violet dogs and portraits of generals no one knew, hung bunches of dried herbs, strings of shrivelled roots and kitchen utensils. I noticed neither an owl nor a black cat, but from the stove two speckled, solid starlings looked at me with astonished and distrustful air.

“Granny, can one at least have a drink of water here?” I asked, raising my voice.

“There, in the tub,” the old woman nodded.

The water tasted of marsh rust. Having thanked the old woman (to which she paid not the slightest attention), I asked her how to get out onto the road.

She suddenly raised her head, looked at me fixedly with her cold bird's eyes, and began to mutter hurriedly:

“Go, go... Go, young man, your own way. You've nothing to do here. A good guest is good in a guest-hut... Off with you, my dear, off...”

Indeed there was nothing left for me but to go. But suddenly it occurred to me to try a last means of softening the harsh old woman a little. I took from my pocket a new silver quarter-rouble and held it out to Manuilikha. I was not mistaken: at the sight of money the old woman stirred, her eyes opened still wider, and she reached for the coin with her crooked, knotted, trembling fingers.

“Ah, no, granny Manuilikha, I won't give it for nothing,” I teased her, hiding the coin. “Come now, tell my fortune.”

The brown wrinkled face of the sorceress gathered into a discontented grimace. She evidently hesitated and looked uncertainly at my fist where the money was clenched. But greed won.

“Well, well, come then, come,” she mumbled, rising from the floor with difficulty. “I tell no one's fortune now, my dear... I've forgotten... I've grown old, my eyes don't see. Only for you, perhaps.”

Holding on to the wall, her bent body shaking at every step, she came to the table, took out a pack of brown cards swollen with age, shuffled them and pushed them toward me.

“Cut... With your left hand cut... From the heart...”

Having spat on her fingers, she began to lay out the cabal. The cards fell on the table with a sound as if they had been rolled out of dough, and lay in a regular eight-pointed star. When the last card lay face down on the king, Manuilikha held out her hand to me.

“Gild my palm, good master... You'll be happy, you'll be rich...” she sang in a begging, purely gypsy tone.

I thrust into her the coin I had ready. The old woman hid it nimbly, like a monkey, in her cheek.

“A great interest comes to you through a distant journey,” she began in a practised patter. “A meeting with the queen of diamonds and some pleasant talk in an important house. Soon you will receive unexpected news from the king of clubs. Some troubles fall to you, and then again some small money falls. You'll be in a large company, you'll be drunk... Not so very much, but still a drinking-bout comes out for you. Your life will be long. If you don't die at sixty-seven, then...”

Suddenly she stopped and raised her head, as if listening for something. I too pricked up my ears. Someone's woman's voice, fresh, ringing and strong, was singing, drawing near the hut. I recognised the words of a graceful little Ukrainian song:

Is it blossom, or no blossom, that bends the guelder-rose bough? Is it sleep, or no sleep, that bows my little head down?

“Well, go, go now, my falcon,” the old woman fussed anxiously, pushing me away from the table with her hand. “You've nothing to loiter about in strangers' huts for. Go where you were going...”

She even caught me by the sleeve of my jacket and pulled me to the door. Her face expressed some sort of animal anxiety.

The voice that sang the song broke off suddenly quite close to the hut, an iron latch clanged loudly, and in the opening of the swiftly thrown-open door appeared a tall laughing girl. With both hands she carefully held up a striped apron, from which looked out three tiny bird heads with red throats and black shining eyes.

“Look, granny, the chaffinches have followed me again,” she cried, laughing loudly, “look how funny they are... Quite hungry. And I, as if on purpose, had no bread with me.”

But, seeing me, she suddenly fell silent and flushed a deep red. Her thin black brows knit in displeasure, and her eyes turned questioningly to the old woman.

“A gentleman has come in... He's asking the way,” the old woman explained. “Well, my dear,” she turned to me with a resolute air, “that's enough lounging. You've drunk your water, you've talked, and it's time to know when to go. We are no company for you...”

“Listen, beauty,” I said to the girl. “Show me, please, the way to the Irinovka road, or I shall never get out of your marsh.”

It must have been the soft, pleading tone I gave these words that affected her. She carefully set her chaffinches on the stove beside the starlings, threw the short coat she had already taken off onto the bench, and went out of the hut in silence.

I followed her.

“Are all these tame birds of yours?” I asked, catching up with the girl.

“Tame,” she answered shortly, and without even looking at me. “Well, look,” she said, stopping at the wattle fence. “Do you see the little path there, there, between the pines? Do you see?”

“I see...”

“Go along it straight on. When you come to an oak log, turn left. So straight on, always through the forest, the forest. There you'll come at once to the Irinovka road.”

While she showed me the direction of the road with her outstretched right hand, I could not help admiring her. There was nothing in her like the local “girls,” whose faces, under the ugly kerchiefs that cover the forehead at the top and the mouth and chin at the bottom, wear such a monotonous, frightened expression. My stranger, a tall brunette of about twenty or twenty-five, carried herself lightly and gracefully. A roomy white blouse wound freely and beautifully about her young, healthy bosom. The original beauty of her face, once seen, could not be forgotten, but it was hard even to get used to it, or to describe it. Its charm lay in those large, shining, dark eyes, to which the thin brows, broken in the middle, gave an elusive shade of slyness, imperiousness and naivety; in the dusky-rose tint of the skin, in the wilful curve of the lips, of which the lower, somewhat fuller, stuck out with a resolute and capricious look.

“Are you really not afraid to live alone in such a wilderness?” I asked, stopping by the fence.

She shrugged her shoulders indifferently.

“What have we to be afraid of? Wolves don't come here.”

“But wolves are not the only thing... You might be snowed in, a fire might break out... and many other things. You are here alone, and no one would be in time to help.”

“And thank God!” she waved her hand disdainfully. “If only they would leave me and granny altogether in peace, it would be better, but otherwise...”

“Otherwise what?”

“If you know too much you'll grow old soon,” she cut me short. “And who are you yourself?” she asked anxiously.

I guessed that probably both the old woman and this beauty feared some persecution from “the authorities,” and hastened to reassure her.

“Oh! Please don't worry. I am not a constable, not a clerk, not an excise man; in a word, I am no kind of official.”

“No, are you telling the truth?”

“I give you my word of honour. By God, I am a perfectly outside person. I have simply come here to stay for a few months, and then I shall go away. If you like, I shall not even tell anyone that I was here and saw you. Do you believe me?”

The girl's face brightened a little.

“Well, then, if you're not lying, you're telling the truth. And how was it: did you hear of us before or did you come in by yourself?”

“I don't know myself how to tell you... I did hear of you, I admit, and even wanted to wander over to you some day, but today I came in by chance, I lost my way... Well, and now tell me, why are you afraid of people? What harm do they do you?”

She looked at me with testing distrust. But my conscience was clear, and I bore this fixed look without blinking. Then she began to speak with growing agitation:

“It goes badly for us with them... Plain people are still all right, but the authorities... The constable comes, he carries something off; the district officer comes, he carries something off. And before taking, he jeers at granny: you're a witch, he says, a she-devil, a convict... Ah! But what's the use of talking!”

“And they don't touch you?” an incautious question slipped from me.

She tossed her head from below upward with haughty self-assurance, and in her narrowed eyes flashed a malicious triumph...

“They don't touch me... Once a surveyor of some kind poked himself in on me... He wanted a bit of petting, you see... So I expect to this day he hasn't forgotten how I petted him.”

In these mocking but peculiarly proud words there rang so much rough independence that I involuntarily thought: “It is not for nothing that you grew up in the Polesye pine forest; it is indeed dangerous to joke with you.”

“And do we touch anyone!” she went on, being filled with more and more confidence in me. “We don't need people either. Only once a year I go to the little town to buy soap and salt... And for granny some tea; she loves her tea. Otherwise I'd be glad not to see anyone at all.”

“Well, I see that you and your granny do not favour people... But may I look in some time for a minute?”

She laughed, and how strangely, how unexpectedly her beautiful face changed! Of the former sternness not a trace remained: it suddenly became bright, shy, childlike.

“But what have you to do with us? Granny and I are dull... Well, come in, then, if you really are a good man. Only this... if you ever wander over to us, then better without a gun...”

“Are you afraid?”

“What have I to be afraid of? I am afraid of nothing,” and in her voice again was heard confidence in her strength. “Only I don't like it. Why kill little birds, or hares either? They do no harm to anyone, and they want to live just as we do. I love them: they are little, so foolish... Well, goodbye now,” she hurried, “I don't know what to call you by name... I'm afraid granny will start scolding.”

And she ran lightly and quickly into the hut, her head bent down, holding with her hands her hair, tousled by the wind.

“Wait, wait!” I shouted. “What is your name? Let us be properly acquainted.”

She stopped for a moment and turned to me.

“My name is Alyona... Here they call me Olesya.”

I threw my gun over my shoulder and went in the direction shown me. Having climbed a small hillock where a narrow, barely noticeable forest path began, I looked back. Olesya's red skirt, slightly stirred by the wind, was still visible on the porch of the hut, standing out as a bright spot against the dazzling white, even background of the snow.

An hour after me Yarmola came home. With his usual dislike of idle talk, he did not ask me a word about how and where I had lost my way. He only said, as though in passing:

“There... I carried the hare into the kitchen... shall we roast it or will you send it to someone?”

“But you don't know, Yarmola, where I was today,” I said, imagining in advance the forester's astonishment.

“Why shouldn't I know?” Yarmola growled roughly. “It's well known, you went to the witches...”

“How did you find that out?”

“And why shouldn't I find out? I heard you gave no voice, so I came back on your track... Ah, young ma-aster!” he added with reproachful vexation. “It's not fitting for you to be doing such things... It's a sin!..”

## IV · The Cards

Spring came early that year, all at once and, as always in Polesye, unexpectedly. Turbulent, brown, glittering rivulets ran down the village streets, foaming angrily around stones in their way and swiftly spinning chips of wood and goose down; in the huge puddles was reflected the blue sky with round white clouds floating across it as if rolling over; frequent ringing drops showered from the roofs. The sparrows that covered the roadside willows in flocks cried so loudly and excitedly that nothing could be heard for their cries. Everywhere one felt the glad, hurried restlessness of life.

The snow was gone, remaining here and there only in dirty, loose patches in the hollows and shady copses. From under it peeped the bare, wet, warm earth, rested through the winter and now full of fresh juices, full of thirst for new motherhood. Over the black fields a light vapour curled, filling the air with the smell of thawed earth, that fresh, insinuating and mighty, heady smell of spring which one recognises even in town among hundreds of other smells. It seemed to me that with this scent there poured into my soul a spring sadness, sweet and tender, full of restless expectations and vague presentiments, a poetic sadness that makes all women pretty in your eyes and is always seasoned with indefinite regrets for past springs. The nights grew warmer; in their thick damp darkness one felt the unseen, hurried creative work of nature...

In those spring days the image of Olesya did not leave my head. I liked, when I was left alone, to lie down, close my eyes the better to concentrate, and ceaselessly call up in my imagination her face, now stern, now sly, now radiant with a tender smile, her young body, which had grown up in the freedom of the old pine forest as slender and as strong as young fir trees grow, her fresh voice with its unexpected low velvet notes... “In all her movements, in her words,” I thought, “there is something noble (in the best sense, of course, of this rather vulgar word), some inborn graceful moderation...” I was drawn to Olesya also by a certain halo of mystery surrounding her, by the superstitious reputation of a witch, by life in the forest thicket amid a marsh, and especially by that proud confidence in her powers which showed through the few words she addressed to me.

It is not surprising that as soon as the forest paths dried a little, I set off for the cabin on hen's legs. In case I should need to appease the grumbling old woman, I took with me half a pound of tea and a few handfuls of lump sugar.

I found both women at home. The old woman was busy by the brightly blazing stove, and Olesya was spinning flax, sitting on a very high bench; when I banged the door coming in, she turned round, the thread broke in her hands, and the spindle rolled across the floor.

For some time the old woman peered at me attentively and angrily, wrinkling up her face and shielding it with her palm from the heat of the stove.

“Good day, granny!” I said in a loud, cheerful voice. “You don't recognise me, I suppose? Do you remember, last month I looked in to ask the way? You told my fortune, too.”

“I remember nothing, my dear,” the old woman mumbled, shaking her head in displeasure, “nothing at all. And what you've forgotten here, I can't make out. What company are we for you? We are simple, grey folk... You've nothing to do here. The forest is big, there is room to go your own ways... so it is...”

Taken aback by the unfriendly reception, I was quite lost and found myself in that foolish position when you do not know what to do: whether to turn rudeness into a joke, or to be angry myself, or, finally, without saying a word, to turn and go back. Involuntarily I turned with a helpless expression to Olesya. She smiled a very little, with a touch of good-natured mockery, rose from her distaff and went up to the old woman.

“Don't be afraid, granny,” she said soothingly, “he is not a bad man, he won't do us any harm. You are welcome to sit down,” she added, pointing me to the bench in the front corner and paying no more attention to the old woman's grumbling.

Encouraged by her attention, I thought of bringing out the most decisive remedy.

“How cross you are, granny... The guests are hardly on the doorstep and you are already scolding. And I had brought you a little present,” I said, taking my parcels out of my bag.

The old woman cast a quick glance at the parcels, but at once turned away to the stove.

“I need none of your presents,” she grumbled, raking the coals fiercely with the poker. “We know these guests too. First they creep into your soul without soap, and then... What have you got in the bag?” she suddenly turned to me.

I at once handed her the tea and sugar. This had a softening effect on the old woman, and although she went on grumbling, it was no longer in her former implacable tone.

Olesya sat down again to her yarn, and I placed myself near her on a low, short and very rickety stool. With her left hand Olesya rapidly twisted the white tow, soft as silk, and in her right the spindle spun with a light hum; she let it fall almost to the ground and then deftly caught it up again and with a short movement of her fingers set it spinning once more. This work, so simple at first sight but in reality requiring enormous, centuries-old skill and dexterity, fairly boiled in her hands. I involuntarily noticed these hands: they were coarsened and blackened by work, but small and of such a beautiful shape that many well-bred young ladies would have envied them.

“And you didn't tell me then that granny had told your fortune,” Olesya said. And seeing that I glanced back apprehensively, she added: “It's all right, she is a little hard of hearing, she won't hear. She makes out only my voice well.”

“Yes, she did. Why?”

“Oh, nothing... I'm just asking... And do you believe it?” she cast a quick sidelong glance at me.

“What? That your granny told my fortune, or in general?”

“No, in general...”

“How shall I say it? It would be truer to say that I do not believe; but then, who knows? They say there are cases... It is even printed about them in clever books. But what your granny said, that I don't believe at all. Any village woman can tell fortunes like that.”

Olesya smiled.

“Yes, it is true that she tells fortunes badly now. She has grown old, and she is very afraid, too. And what did the cards tell you?”

“Nothing interesting. I don't even remember now. What they usually say: a distant journey, an interest of clubs... I've even forgotten.”

“Yes, yes, she's become a poor fortune-teller. She has forgotten many words from old age... How could she? And she is afraid, too. Only if she sees money will she agree.”

“What is she afraid of?”

“Well known what: the authorities. When the constable comes, he always threatens: ‘I can lock you up at any time. Do you know,’ he says, ‘what is laid down for the likes of you for sorcery? Exile to hard labour, without term, to Falcon Island.’ What do you think, is he lying or not?”

“No, he is not lying; something is indeed laid down for it, but not so dreadful... Well, and you, Olesya, can you tell fortunes?”

She seemed to hesitate a little, but only for a moment.

“I do... Only not for money,” she added hastily.

“Perhaps you will lay the cards for me too?”

“No,” she answered quietly but resolutely, shaking her head.

“Why won't you? Well, not now, then some time later... For some reason it seems to me you will tell me the truth.”

“No. I won't. I won't on any account.”

“Come, that is not nice, Olesya. For the sake of our first acquaintance you ought not to refuse... Why are you unwilling?”

“Because I have already cast the cards on you; a second time it is not allowed...”

“Not allowed? Why? I don't understand that.”

“No, no, it's not allowed... not allowed...” she whispered with superstitious fear. “You mustn't tempt fate twice... It's not right... It will find out, it will overhear... Fate doesn't like to be asked. That's why all fortune-tellers are unhappy.”

I wanted to answer Olesya with some joke and could not: there was too much sincere conviction in her words, so that even when, mentioning fate, she looked back at the door with a strange fear, I involuntarily repeated the movement.

“Well, if you don't want to tell my fortune, then tell me what came out for you then,” I asked.

Olesya suddenly dropped her distaff and touched my hand with hers.

“No... Better not,” she said, and her eyes took on a pleading, childlike expression. “Please don't ask... It came out badly for you... Better not ask...”

But I went on insisting. I could not make out whether her refusal and dark hints about fate were a put-on trick of a fortune-teller, or whether she herself really believed what she said, but I felt somehow ill at ease, almost uncanny.

“Well, all right, I will tell you, then,” Olesya agreed at last. “Only look, an agreement is better than money: don't be angry if you don't like something. This is what came out for you: you are a man, though kind, but weak... Your kindness is not good, not from the heart. You are not master of your word. You like to take the upper hand over people, yet yourself, though you don't wish it, you submit to them. You like wine, and also... Well, never mind, if I'm to speak, then let it be all in order... You are very fond of our sister, and through this you will have much evil in life... You don't value money and don't know how to save it: you will never be rich... Shall I go on?”

“Speak, speak! Tell everything you know!”

“Further it came out that your life will not be a merry one. You will love no one with your heart, because your heart is cold, lazy, and to those who love you, you will bring much grief. You will never marry, and so you will die a bachelor. You will have no great joys in life, but much boredom and burden... A time will come when you will want to lay hands on yourself... Such a thing will turn up for you... But you will not dare, so you will bear it... You will endure great want; but towards the end of your life your fate will change through the death of someone close to you, and quite unexpectedly for you. Only all this will be many years from now, but in this year... I don't know when exactly, the cards say very soon... perhaps even this month...”

“What will happen this year?” I asked when she stopped again.

“I am even afraid to say more. A great love falls to you from some lady of clubs. Only I can't guess whether she is married or a girl, but I know that she has dark hair...”

I involuntarily cast a quick glance at Olesya's head.

“What are you looking at?” she suddenly blushed, feeling my glance with the understanding peculiar to some women. “Well, yes, like mine,” she went on, mechanically smoothing her hair and blushing still more.

“So you say a great love of clubs?” I joked.

“Don't laugh, you mustn't laugh,” Olesya remarked seriously, almost sternly. “I tell you only the truth.”

“Well, all right, I won't, I won't. What then?”

“Then... Oh! It comes out badly for this lady of clubs, worse than death. She will take great shame through you, such that she can never forget it all her life, long sorrow comes out for her... And for you in her planet nothing bad comes out.”

“Listen, Olesya, could the cards have deceived you? Why should I cause so much trouble to a lady of clubs? I am a quiet, modest man, and you have told so many terrors about me.”

“Well, that I don't know. And it came out that it is not you who will do it: not on purpose, that is, but only through you will all this trouble befall... When my words come true, you will remember me then.”

“And did the cards tell you all this, Olesya?”

She answered not at once, evasively and as if unwillingly:

“And the cards... But I find out much without them, even by the face, for instance. If, for example, some man is soon to die an evil death, I read it on his face at once; I needn't even talk with him.”

“And what do you see in his face?”

“I don't know myself. I suddenly feel afraid, as though he were not alive, standing before me. Ask granny, she'll tell you I speak the truth. Trofim the miller hanged himself in his mill the year before last, and I had seen him only two days earlier and said at the time to granny: ‘Look, granny, Trofim will die an evil death in a day or two.’ And so it came out. And last Christmastide the horse thief Yashka came to us and asked granny to tell his fortune. Granny laid out the cards for him and began to tell. And he asks jokingly: ‘Tell me, granny, what death I shall die?’ And he himself laughs. As I looked at him, I couldn't even stir: I see Yakov sitting there and his face is dead, green... His eyes closed and his lips black...”

“Then, a week later, we hear that the peasants caught Yakov when he was trying to lead off some horses... All night they beat him... A wicked people we have here, pitiless. They drove nails into his heels, broke all his ribs with stakes, and towards morning the life went out of him.”

“Why didn't you tell him that trouble awaited him?”

“And why tell?” Olesya objected. “What is laid down by fate, can one run away from it? A man would only worry in vain over his last days... And I myself am disgusted that I see so; I become loathsome to myself... But what then? It is from fate that I have this. My granny, when she was younger, also knew death beforehand, and my mother too, and granny's mother: it is not from us... it is so in our blood.”

She stopped spinning and sat with her head bent low, her hands laid quietly along her knees. In her fixed, motionless eyes with their widened pupils was reflected some dark horror, some involuntary submission to mysterious powers and supernatural knowledge that overshadowed her soul.

## V · The Charm

At this time the old woman spread on the table a clean towel with embroidered ends and set a steaming pot on it.

“Come to supper, Olesya,” she called her granddaughter and, after a moment's hesitation, added, turning to me: “Perhaps you, sir, will take a bite with us? You are welcome... Only our dishes are poor: we don't cook soups, just plain field groat-stew...”

It could not be said that her invitation smacked of any special insistence, and I was already about to decline it, but Olesya in her turn asked me with such sweet simplicity and with such a kind smile that I agreed in spite of myself. She herself poured me a full plate of krupnik, a pottage of buckwheat groats with bacon fat, onion, potatoes and chicken, an extremely tasty and nourishing dish. Sitting down to table, neither grandmother nor granddaughter crossed herself. During supper I did not cease to watch both women, because, by my deep conviction, which I preserve to this day, nowhere does a person reveal himself so clearly as when eating. The old woman swallowed the stew with hurried greed, smacking loudly and stuffing enormous pieces of bread into her mouth, so that big lumps swelled and moved under her flabby cheeks. In Olesya, even in her manner of eating, there was a certain inborn decency.

An hour after supper I took leave of the mistresses of the cabin on hen's legs.

“Would you like me to see you off a little way?” Olesya offered.

“What sort of seeing-off have you thought up now!” the old woman mumbled angrily. “You can't sit still, dragonfly...”

But Olesya had already thrown a red cashmere kerchief over her head and suddenly, running up to her grandmother, embraced her and kissed her resoundingly.

“Granny! Dear, darling, golden... only for a minute, I'll be right back.”

“Well, all right, all right, you giddy one,” the old woman weakly fought her off. “Don't take offence, sir: she is quite a fool, my girl.”

Having gone along the narrow path, we came out on a forest road, black with mud, all trampled with hoof prints and furrowed with ruts full of water in which the blaze of the evening glow was reflected. We walked along the verge of the road, covered all over with brown last-year leaves not yet dried after the snow. Here and there through their dead yellowness the large bluebells of the “sleep-herb,” the first flower of Polesye, raised their lilac heads.

“Listen, Olesya,” I began, “I should very much like to ask you something, but I'm afraid you will be angry... Tell me, is it true what they say, that your granny... how shall I put it?..”

“Is a sorceress?” Olesya calmly helped me.

“No... Not a sorceress...” I faltered. “Well, yes, if you like, a sorceress... Of course, one never knows what people chatter. Why shouldn't she simply know certain herbs, remedies, charms?.. However, if it is unpleasant for you, you needn't answer.”

“No, why not,” she answered simply, “what is unpleasant about it? She really is a sorceress. But now she has grown old and can't do what she did before.”

“What could she do?” I asked curiously.

“Various things. She could cure, she treated teeth, she charmed away blood, she recited prayers over anyone bitten by a mad dog or a snake, she pointed out treasures... you can't list everything.”

“Do you know what, Olesya?.. Forgive me, but I don't believe all this. Come, be frank with me, I won't give you away to anyone: isn't it all pretence, merely to fool people?”

She shrugged her shoulders indifferently.

“Think as you like. Of course it costs nothing to fool a village woman, but you I wouldn't try to deceive.”

“So you firmly believe in witchcraft?”

“How can I not believe? In our family there are spells... I myself know a good deal.”

“Olesya, my dear... If you knew how interesting this is to me... Will you really show me nothing?”

“Why not, I'll show you, if you like,” Olesya agreed readily. “Do you want it now?”

“Yes, if possible, now.”

“And you won't be afraid?”

“Come, nonsense. At night, perhaps, I should be, but now it's still light.”

“Good. Give me your hand.”

I obeyed. Olesya quickly turned up the sleeve of my coat and unfastened the cuff-link, then took from her pocket a small Finnish knife, about three inches long, and drew it out of its leather sheath.

“What are you going to do?” I asked, feeling a mean little misgiving stir in me.

“Why, right now... You said you wouldn't be afraid!”

Suddenly her hand made a barely noticeable light movement, and I felt in the flesh of my arm, a little above the place where one feels the pulse, the irritating touch of a sharp blade. Blood at once welled up along the whole width of the cut, ran down my arm and dripped on the ground in frequent drops. I barely kept myself from crying out, but I think I turned pale.

“Don't be afraid, you'll live,” Olesya smiled.

She firmly gripped my arm above the wound and, bending her face low over it, began to whisper something quickly, bathing my skin with her hot, broken breath. When Olesya straightened up and loosed her fingers, only a red scratch remained on the wounded spot.

“Well? Is that enough for you?” she asked with a sly smile, hiding her knife. “Do you want more?”

“Of course I do. Only, if it could be, not so terrifying and without bloodshed, please.”

“What shall I show you?” she thought. “Well, perhaps this: walk ahead of me along the road... Only mind you don't turn round.”

“And won't it be frightening?” I asked, trying to cover with a careless smile my timid expectation of an unpleasant surprise.

“No, no... A trifle... Go.”

I walked ahead, much interested in the experiment, feeling Olesya's intent gaze at my back. But after going some twenty paces I suddenly stumbled on quite level ground and fell flat on my face.

“Go on, go on!” Olesya cried. “Don't turn round! It's nothing, it will heal before your wedding... Hold on tight to the ground when you fall.”

I went on. Ten paces more, and I stretched myself out at full length a second time.

Olesya burst out laughing and clapped her hands.

“Well? Are you satisfied?” she cried, flashing her white teeth. “Do you believe now? It's nothing, nothing!.. You flew not up, but down.”

“How did you do that?” I asked in surprise, brushing off the twigs and dry blades of grass that clung to my clothes. “Is it not a secret?”

“Not a secret at all. I'll gladly tell you. Only I'm afraid that perhaps you won't understand... I shan't be able to explain...”

I really did not quite understand her. But, if I am not mistaken, this peculiar trick consists in this: she, following me step by step, foot in foot, and gazing at me unremittingly, at the same time tries to imitate my every smallest movement, so to speak identifies herself with me. Having gone a few steps in this way, she begins to imagine in her mind, at some distance ahead of me, a rope stretched across the road an arshin above the ground. At the moment when I must touch this imaginary rope with my foot, Olesya suddenly makes a falling movement, and then, according to her, even the strongest man must fall without fail... Only long afterwards did I recall Olesya's confused explanation when I read Doctor Charcot's account of experiments he made on two patients of the Salpêtrière, professional sorceresses suffering from hysteria.

And I was very much surprised to learn that the French witches of the common people resorted in such cases to exactly the same knack that the pretty Polesye witch put to use.

“Oh! I can do much more,” Olesya declared self-confidently. “For instance, I can send fear upon you.”

“What does that mean?”

“I'll make you feel afraid. You are sitting, for example, in your room in the evening, and suddenly such fear comes over you, for no reason at all, that you tremble and dare not look behind you. Only for that I must know where you live and first see your room.”

“Well, that is quite simple,” I doubted. “You will go up to the window, knock, shout something.”

“Oh, no, no... I shall be in the forest at that time, I shan't go out of the hut anywhere... But I shall sit and keep thinking that here I am going down the street, entering your house, opening the doors, entering your room... You are sitting somewhere... say, at the table... I creep up behind you softly... you don't hear me... I seize you by the shoulder with my hands and begin to press... tighter, tighter, tighter... and I myself look at you... like this: look...”

Her thin brows suddenly drew together, her eyes fixed on me point-blank with a threatening and attracting expression, her pupils widened and turned dark blue. At once I remembered the head of Medusa that I had seen in Moscow, in the Tretyakov Gallery, the work of I no longer remember what artist. Under this fixed, strange gaze a cold horror of the supernatural seized me.

“Come, come, Olesya... enough,” I said with forced laughter. “I like it much better when you smile; then you have such a sweet, childlike face.”

We went on. I suddenly recalled the expressiveness, and for a simple girl even the refinement, of the phrases in Olesya's conversation, and I said:

“Do you know what surprises me in you, Olesya? You grew up in a forest, seeing nobody... And you naturally could not read much either...”

“Why, I can't even read.”

“Well, all the more... And yet you speak so well, no worse than a real young lady. Tell me, where did you get that? Do you understand what I am asking?”

“Yes, I understand. It's all from granny... Don't go by her looks. Oh! how clever she is! Perhaps she'll talk with you too when she is more used to you. She knows everything, simply everything in the world, whatever you ask. True, she has aged now.”

“So she has seen a great deal in her time? Where does she come from? Where did she live before?”

It seems these questions did not please Olesya. She answered not at once, evasively and unwillingly:

“I don't know... She doesn't like to talk about it. And if she ever does say anything, she always asks me to forget it and not to remember it again... Well, but it's time for me,” Olesya hurried, “granny will be angry. Goodbye... Excuse me, I don't know your name.”

I gave my name.

“Ivan Timofeyevich? Well, very good. Then goodbye, Ivan Timofeyevich! Don't scorn our hut, come and see us.”

At parting I held out my hand to her, and her small, strong hand answered me with a firm, friendly pressure.

## VI · Evenings in the Forest

From that day I became a frequent guest at the cabin on hen's legs. Every time I came, Olesya met me with her usual reserved dignity. But always, by the first involuntary movement she made on seeing me, I noticed that she was glad of my coming. The old woman, as before, did not cease to grumble something under her breath, but showed no open ill-will, thanks to her granddaughter's intercession, invisible to me but undoubted; the presents I now and then brought also had no small influence in my favour: now a warm shawl, now a jar of jam, now a bottle of cherry liqueur. As if by a silent mutual agreement, it became our custom for Olesya to see me as far as the Irinovka road when I went home.

And always at that time such a lively, interesting talk began between us that we both tried, involuntarily, to prolong the way, walking as slowly as possible along the silent forest edges. When we reached the Irinovka road, I would walk her back half a verst, and even so, before we said goodbye, we would talk for a long time standing under the fragrant canopy of pine boughs.

It was not Olesya's beauty alone that enchanted me in her, but also her whole, original, free nature, her mind, at once clear and wrapped in unshakable hereditary superstition, childishly innocent but not without the sly coquetry of a beautiful woman. She never tired of questioning me in detail about everything that occupied and stirred her primitive, vivid imagination: about countries and peoples, about natural phenomena, about the structure of the earth and the universe, about learned men, about big cities... Much seemed to her amazing, fabulous, improbable. But from the very beginning of our acquaintance I had taken with her such a serious, sincere and simple tone that she willingly took all my stories on trust without question.

Sometimes, finding it hard to explain something too incomprehensible, in my opinion, for her half-savage head (and at times not quite clear to myself), I objected to her eager questions: “You see... I can't tell you that... You won't understand me.”

Then she would begin to implore me:

“No, please, please, I'll try... Tell me somehow, even if it's not clear...”

She forced me to launch into monstrous comparisons, the boldest examples, and if I had difficulty finding an expression, she herself helped me with a whole rain of impatient questions, like those we put to a stammerer painfully stuck on one word. And indeed in the end her supple, mobile mind and fresh imagination triumphed over my pedagogical impotence. I became convinced in spite of myself that for her surroundings, for her upbringing (or rather the lack of it), she possessed astonishing abilities.

Once I mentioned St Petersburg in passing. Olesya at once became interested:

“What is Petersburg? A little town?”

“No, it is not a little town; it is the biggest Russian city.”

“The biggest? The very, very biggest there is? And there is none bigger?” she pestered me.

“Well, yes... All the chief authorities live there... great gentlemen... The houses there are all of stone, none of wood.”

“Surely much bigger than our Stepan?” Olesya asked confidently.

“Oh, yes... a little bigger... say, five hundred times. There are houses there in each of which more people live than in the whole of Stepan.”

“Ah, my God! What sort of houses are those?” Olesya asked, almost in fright.

I had, as usual, to resort to a comparison.

“Terrible houses. Five, six, even seven storeys. Do you see that pine?”

“The biggest one? I see it.”

“Well, houses as high as that. And crammed from top to bottom with people. These people live in little kennels, like birds in cages, ten in each, so that there isn't even enough air for them all. And others live below, right under the ground, in damp and cold; it happens that they do not see the sun in their room all the year round.”

“Well, I wouldn't exchange my forest for your city for anything,” said Olesya, shaking her head. “Even when I go to the market in Stepan, I feel disgusted. They push, make a noise, quarrel... And such a longing for the forest comes over me that I'd like to drop everything and run without looking back... God be with it, your city; I would never live there.”

“Well, and if your husband were from the city?” I asked with a light smile.

Her brows frowned, and her thin nostrils quivered.

“Fancy that!” she said with disdain. “I want no husband at all.”

“You say so only now, Olesya. Almost all girls say the same and yet marry. Wait a little: you will meet someone, you will fall in love; then you will go with him not only to the city but to the end of the earth.”

“Ah, no, no... please, let's not talk of it,” she waved it away in vexation. “Why this conversation?.. I beg you, don't.”

“How funny you are, Olesya. Do you really think you will never in your life love a man? You are so young, beautiful, strong. When your blood takes fire, there will be no question of vows.”

“Well, so what, I'll love!” Olesya answered defiantly, her eyes flashing. “I shall ask nobody's leave...”

“So you will marry too,” I teased.

“Perhaps you mean the church?” she guessed.

“Of course, the church... The priest will lead you round the lectern, the deacon will sing ‘Rejoice, O Isaiah,’ they will set a wreath on your head...”

Olesya lowered her lids and shook her head with a faint smile.

“No, my dear... Perhaps you won't like what I say, but in our family nobody has been married in church: both my mother and my granny lived without it... We may not even enter a church...”

“All because of your sorcery?”

“Yes, because of our sorcery,” Olesya answered with calm seriousness. “How should I dare to show myself in church if from my very birth my soul has been sold to him?”

“Olesya... Dear... Believe me, you are deceiving yourself. It is wild, it is absurd, what you say.”

On Olesya's face appeared again the strange expression I had once noticed, of convinced and gloomy submission to her mysterious destiny.

“No, no... You cannot understand this, but I feel it... Here,” she pressed her hand hard to her breast, “in my soul I feel it. Our whole family is cursed for ever and ever. Judge for yourself: who helps us, if not he? Can an ordinary person do what I can? All our power comes from him.”

And every time our conversation, hardly touching this extraordinary subject, ended in the same way. In vain I exhausted all arguments accessible to Olesya's understanding, in vain I spoke in simple form of hypnotism, of suggestion, of doctors of mental illness and Indian fakirs, in vain I tried to explain to her physiologically some of her experiments, for example the charming away of blood, which is so simply achieved by a skilful pressure on a vein: Olesya, so trusting to me in everything else, with obstinate persistence refuted my proofs and explanations... “Well, all right, all right, the charming of blood I'll give up to you, so be it,” she would say, raising her voice in the heat of the argument, “but where does the rest come from? Do I know only how to charm blood? Do you want me to drive all the mice and cockroaches out of your hut in one day? Do you want me to cure with plain water in two days the strongest fever, even if all your doctors have given up the patient? Do you want me to make you forget some one word altogether? And why do I interpret dreams? And why do I know the future?”

This dispute always ended with both Olesya and me falling silent, not without inward irritation against each other. In truth, for much of her black art I could not find an explanation in my small science. I do not know and cannot say whether Olesya possessed even half of those secrets of which she spoke with such naive faith, but what I myself was not seldom a witness of instilled in me an unshakable conviction that Olesya had access to those unconscious, instinctive, hazy, strange kinds of knowledge, gained by chance experience, which, having outstripped exact science by whole centuries, live, mixed with ridiculous and wild superstitions, in the dark, closed mass of the people, passed from generation to generation as the greatest secret.

In spite of the sharp disagreement on this one point, we grew more and more strongly attached to each other. Not a word of love had yet been said between us, but being together had already become a need for us, and often in silent moments, when our glances met suddenly and at the same time, I saw Olesya's eyes grow moist and a thin blue vein beat at her temple...

On the other hand, my relations with Yarmola quite spoiled. For him, evidently, my visits to the cabin on hen's legs and my evening walks with Olesya were no secret: he always knew with astonishing exactness everything that happened in his forest. For some time I had noticed that he began to avoid me. His black eyes followed me from afar with reproach and displeasure every time I was going to the forest, though he did not express his disapproval by a single word. Our comically serious lessons in literacy ceased. And if sometimes in the evening I called Yarmola to study, he only waved his hand.

“What's the use! It's an empty business, young master,” he said with lazy contempt.

We stopped going hunting too. Every time I raised the subject, Yarmola found some pretext for refusing: now his gun was out of order, now the dog was sick, now he himself had no time: “I've no time, young master... I must plough the field today,” Yarmola most often answered my invitation, and I knew perfectly well that he would not be “ploughing the field” at all but would spend the whole day near the liquor shop in the doubtful hope of someone's treat. This silent, hidden enmity began to weary me, and I already thought of dispensing with Yarmola's services, using for it the first suitable pretext... I was stopped only by a feeling of pity for his enormous destitute family, which Yarmola's four roubles' wages helped to save from starving to death.

## VII · Notice to Quit

One day, when I came as usual towards evening to the cabin on hen's legs, the dejected state of mind of its inhabitants struck me at once. The old woman sat with her feet on the bed and, hunched over, her head clasped in her hands, rocked to and fro and mumbled something indistinctly. She paid no attention at all to my greeting. Olesya greeted me, as always, kindly, but our talk would not knit. She evidently listened to me absently and answered at cross purposes. On her beautiful face lay the shadow of some incessant inward care.

“I see something bad has happened to you, Olesya,” I said, carefully touching with my hand her hand lying on the bench.

Olesya quickly turned to the window, as if examining something there. She tried to seem calm, but her brows drew together and trembled, and her teeth bit hard on her lower lip.

“No... What could have happened to us in particular?” she said in a dull voice. “Everything is as it was.”

“Olesya, why do you tell me an untruth? That is not nice of you... And I thought we had become quite friends.”

“Truly, there is nothing... Just... our own cares... trifling ones...”

“No, Olesya, they must not be trifling. Look, you have become unlike yourself.”

“It only seems so to you.”

“Be frank with me, Olesya. I don't know whether I can help you, but perhaps I can give some advice... Well, at last, you will simply feel easier when you share your grief.”

“Ah, truly it is not worth speaking of,” Olesya objected impatiently. “You can't help us in any way here.”

The old woman suddenly interrupted our conversation with unwonted heat:

“What are you being stubborn for, fool! They tell you something sensible and you turn up your nose. As if there were no one cleverer than you in the world. Allow me, sir, to tell you the whole story in order,” she turned in my direction.

The extent of the trouble turned out to be much greater than I could have supposed from the words of proud Olesya. Yesterday evening the local constable had called at the cabin on hen's legs.

“First he sat down quite properly and asked for vodka,” Manuilikha said, “and then he began, and began. ‘Get out of the hut,’ he says, ‘in twenty-four hours with all your belongings. If, I come next time,’ he says, ‘and find you here, know that you won't escape transport by stages. With two soldiers,’ he says, ‘I'll send you, you curse, to your homeland.’ And my homeland, my dear, is far, the town of Amchensk... I haven't a living soul I know there now, and our passports are overdue and overdue, and not in order besides. Ah, Lord, my misfortune!”

“Why did he let you live here earlier, and think of it only now?” I asked.

“There now, go and see... He was babbling something, but I confess I didn't understand. You see how it is: this hovel in which we live is not ours but the landowner's. We used to live in the village with Olesya before, and then...”

“I know, I know, granny, I have heard about that... The peasants got angry with you...”

“Well, well, that's just it. I begged this shack then from the old landowner, Mr. Abrosimov. And now they say a new landowner has bought the forest and wants to drain some marshes or other. But how have I got in their way?”

“Granny, perhaps it's all just a lie?” I remarked. “The constable simply wanted to get a ‘red one,’ a ten-rouble note.”

“I gave it, my dear, I gave it. He won't tak-ke! There's a story... I gave a twenty-five-rouble note, he won't take it... Not a bit! He turned on me so fiercely that I didn't know where I was standing. He kept on in one breath: ‘Out and out!’ What shall we do now, we poor orphans! Dear father, if only you would help us somehow, shame him, the insatiable belly. I should be grateful to you all my life, it seems.”

“Granny!” Olesya pronounced with reproach, deliberately.

“What 'granny' is there!” the old woman got angry. “I've been ‘granny’ to you for twenty-five years now. What, in your opinion, is it better to go begging with a bag? No, sir, don't listen to her. Be so kind, if you can do anything, do it.”

In vague terms I promised to see to it, although, to tell the truth, there was little hope. If even our constable refused “to take,” the matter was too serious. That evening Olesya took leave of me coldly and, contrary to her custom, did not go to see me off. I saw that the proud girl was angry with me for my interference and a little ashamed of her grandmother's tearfulness.

## VIII · The Constable and the Gun

It was a grey, warm morning. Several times already a heavy, short, blessed rain had begun to fall, after which young grass grows before one's eyes and new shoots stretch up. After the rain the sun peeped out for a minute, flooding with joyful sparkle the rain-washed, young, still tender green of the lilacs that completely filled my front garden; the saucy cry of the sparrows on the loose kitchen-garden beds grew louder; the sticky brown buds of the poplar smelled stronger. I was sitting at the table drawing a plan of a forest tract when Yarmola came into the room.

“The constable is here,” he said gloomily.

At that moment the order I had given two days before, to inform me in case of the constable's arrival, had quite flown out of my head, and I could not at once make out what relation this representative of authority had to me at the moment.

“What is it?” I asked in bewilderment.

“I say the constable has come,” Yarmola repeated in the same hostile tone which he had in general taken with me in recent days. “I've just seen him on the dam. He's coming here.”

The rattle of wheels was heard in the street. I hastily rushed to the window and opened it. A long, thin, chocolate-coloured gelding, with a drooping lower lip and an offended muzzle, drew at a staid trot a high, jolting wicker cart, to which it was joined by only one shaft; the other shaft was replaced by a thick rope (malicious county tongues asserted that the constable had purposely got this sorry “turnout” to forestall all manner of undesirable interpretations). The constable himself drove the horse, occupying both seats with his monstrous body, clothed in a gray greatcoat of dandified officer's cloth.

“My respects, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich!” I called, leaning out of the window.

“Ah, my respects, sir! How is your health?” he responded in an amiable, rolling, official baritone.

He reined in the gelding and, touching the peak of his cap with a straightened palm, bent his trunk forward with ponderous grace.

“Step in for a minute. I have a little matter for you.”

The constable spread his arms wide and shook his head.

“Cannot, sir! In the performance of official duty. I am going to Volosha for a dead body: a drowned man, sir.”

But I already knew Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich's weak points, and therefore said with feigned indifference:

“Pity, pity... And I got a couple of such little bottles from Count Vortsel's estate...”

“Cannot, sir. Duty of service...”

“The butler sold them to me as a friend. He brought them up in the cellar like his own children... You should step in... And I'll order some oats given to your nag.”

“Well, really, what a man you are,” the constable said reproachfully. “Don't you know that service comes first?... And what are they, those bottles? Plum brandy?”

“What plum brandy!” I waved my hand. “Old vodka, my dear fellow, that's what!”

“We, to tell the truth, have already had a bite,” the constable scratched his cheek regretfully, wrinkling his face incredibly as he did so.

I went on with the same calm:

“I don't know whether it's true, but the butler swore that it is two hundred years old. The smell is just like cognac, and of the very amber yellow.”

“Ah! What are you doing to me!” the constable exclaimed in comic despair. “Who will take my horse?”

I really did have several bottles of old vodka, though not as ancient as I boasted, but I counted on the force of suggestion to add a few tens of years to it... At any rate it was genuine home-made, stunning old vodka, the pride of the cellar of a ruined magnate (Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich, who came from the clergy, at once begged a bottle from me against, as he put it, a possible cold). And I had a gastronomic snack too: young radish with fresh, just-churned butter.

“Well, sir, and what sort is your little matter?” asked the constable after the fifth glass, leaning back in an old armchair which creaked under him.

I began to set out for him the position of the poor old woman, mentioned her helplessness and despair, and in passing took a dig at needless formalism. The constable listened to me with his head bowed, methodically peeling the roots from a red, firm, plump radish and chewing it with an appetising crunch. Now and then he quickly cast up at me his indifferent, dull, ridiculously small blue eyes, but on his huge red face I could read nothing: neither sympathy nor resistance. When I finally fell silent, he only asked:

“Well, then, what do you want of me?”

“What do I want?” I grew agitated. “Do enter into their position, please. Two poor, defenceless women live...”

“And one of them is a real garden bud!” the constable put in slyly.

“Well, bud or no bud, that is the ninth matter. But why, tell me, should you not take an interest in them? Is it really so urgent for you to evict them? At least wait a little, until I myself make an appeal to the landowner. What do you risk if you wait a month?”

“What do I risk, sir?!” the constable leaped up from the chair. “Good Lord, I risk everything, and first of all my post, sir. God knows what sort of man this Mr. Ilyashevich, the new landowner, is. He might be a troublemaker, sir... of the sort who at the first little thing take paper, pen and a denunciation to Petersburg, sir! We have such too, sir!”

I tried to calm the fuming constable.

“Come, come, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich. You exaggerate all this. After all, what? Risk is risk, but gratitude is still gratitude.”

“Whew-w-w!” the constable gave a long whistle and thrust his hands deep into the pockets of his trousers. “Calls that gratitude! What do you think, that for some twenty-five roubles I shall stake my official position? No, sir, you think poorly of me.”

“Why get so hot, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich. It is not a matter of the sum at all, but just so... Well, at least out of humanity...”

“Out of hu-ma-ni-ty?” he repeated ironically, enunciating every syllable. “If you please, sir, I've got these humans right here, sir!”

He struck himself vigorously on his mighty bronzed nape, which hung over his collar in a fat hairless fold.

“Well, that seems to be too much, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich.”

“Not a bit too much, sir. ‘This is the plague of these parts,’ in the words of the famous fabulist, Mr. Krylov. That is what these two ladies are, sir! Have you not been so kind as to read the excellent work of His Excellency Prince Urusov entitled ‘The Police Constable’?”

“No, I haven't had occasion to.”

“And very much to your loss, sir. An excellent and highly moral work. I advise you to acquaint yourself with it at leisure...”

“Good, good, I shall gladly. But I still don't understand what this book has to do with two poor women?”

“What? A very direct connection, sir. Point one (Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich bent down the thick, hairy forefinger of his left hand): ‘The constable shall keep unremitting watch that all attend God's temple with zeal, remaining therein, however, without effort...’ Allow me to ask whether this... what's her name... Manuilikha, is it?.. whether she ever goes to church?”

I was silent, astonished at the unexpected turn of speech. He looked at me with triumph and bent down the second finger.

“Point two: ‘False predictions and false omens are forbidden everywhere...’ Do you feel it, sir? Then point three, sir: ‘It is forbidden to pass oneself off as a wizard or sorcerer and to use similar deceptions, sir.’ What have you to say to that? And what if all this comes out or reaches the authorities by a roundabout way? Who answers? I. Who is sacked from the service? I. You see what a business it is.”

He sat down in the armchair again. His eyes, raised upward, wandered absently over the walls of the room, and his fingers drummed loudly on the table.

“Well, and if I ask you, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich,” I began again in an ingratiating tone. “Of course your duties are complicated and troublesome, but your heart, I know, is very kind, a golden heart. What does it cost you to promise me not to touch these women?”

The constable's eyes suddenly fixed on something above my head.

“A pretty little gun you have,” he dropped carelessly, without ceasing to drum. “A fine little gun. Last time when I called on you and did not find you at home, I kept admiring it... A wonderful little piece!”

I too turned my head back and looked at the gun.

“Yes, the gun is not bad,” I praised it. “It is an old one, by the factory of Gastinne-Renette; I only converted it to centre-fire last year. Pay attention to the barrels.”

“Of course, sir, of course, sir... it was chiefly the barrels I admired. A magnificent thing... Simply, one may say, a treasure.”

Our eyes met, and I saw a slight but significant smile quiver at the corners of the constable's lips. I rose from my place, took the gun down from the wall and went up to Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich with it.

“The Circassians have a very nice custom of giving a guest whatever he praises,” I said graciously. “We are not Circassians, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich, but I ask you to accept this thing from me as a keepsake.”

The constable pretended to be ashamed.

“Good Lord, such a beauty! No, no, this is too generous a custom!”

However, I did not have to persuade him long. The constable accepted the gun, carefully stood it between his knees and lovingly wiped with a clean handkerchief the dust that had settled on the trigger guard. I calmed down a little on seeing that the gun had at least passed into the hands of a lover and connoisseur. Almost at once Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich stood up and began to hurry off.

“Business doesn't wait, and here I've been gabbing with you,” he said, banging loudly on the floor with his overshoes, which would not go on. “When you are in our parts, you're welcome to visit me.”

“Well, and what about Manuilikha, Mr. Chief?” I reminded him delicately.

“We'll see, we'll see...” Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich grunted vaguely. “There's something else I wanted to ask you... Your radishes are remarkable...”

“I grew them myself.”

“Ama-azing radishes! And I, you know, have a wife who is a terrible adorer of every vegetable. So if, you know, that... one little bunch.”

“With pleasure, Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich. I shall consider it my duty... This very day I'll send a little basket by a messenger. And allow me to add some butter... My butter is exceptional.”

“Well, and some butter...” the constable graciously permitted. “And you give these women a sign that I shall not touch them for the present. Only let them know,” he suddenly raised his voice, “that they won't get off with a mere thank-you from me. And now I wish you health. Once more thanks for the present and for the hospitality.”

He clicked his heels in military fashion and with the heavy gait of a well-fed, important man walked to his carriage, near which, in respectful poses, bareheaded, already stood the village constable, the elder and Yarmola.

## IX · The Fever

Yevpsikhy Afrikanovich kept his promise and left the inhabitants of the forest hut in peace for an indefinite time. But my relations with Olesya changed sharply and strangely. In her manner towards me there was no trace left of her former trusting and naive tenderness, of her former liveliness, in which the coquetry of a beautiful girl was so sweetly mixed with a frisky childish playfulness. An insurmountable awkward constraint appeared in our conversation... With hasty timidity Olesya avoided the lively themes which had earlier given such boundless scope to our curiosity.

In my presence she gave herself to work with tense, stern businesslike zeal, but often I observed how in the midst of this work her hands suddenly dropped powerless along her knees and her eyes fixed motionless and vague on the floor. If at such a moment I called Olesya by name or put some question to her, she started and slowly turned her face to me, in which fright and an effort to grasp the sense of my words were reflected. Sometimes it seemed to me that my company weighed upon and cramped her, but this supposition ill agreed with the immense interest which only a few days before every remark of mine, every phrase of mine, had roused in her... There remained only to think that Olesya did not want to forgive me my patronage in the matter of the constable, which had so outraged her independent nature.

But this guess too did not satisfy me: whence indeed could a simple girl, grown up in the forest, have such excessively scrupulous pride?

All this required explanation, and Olesya stubbornly avoided every favourable chance for a frank talk. Our evening walks ceased. In vain every day, getting ready to leave, I cast eloquent, pleading glances at Olesya: she pretended not to understand their meaning. And the old woman's presence, in spite of her deafness, disturbed me.

Sometimes I rebelled against my own impotence and against the habit that drew me every day to Olesya. I myself did not suspect with what fine, strong, invisible threads my heart was tied to this enchanting girl, incomprehensible to me. I was not yet thinking of love, but I was already living through the restless period that precedes love, full of vague, wearisomely sad sensations. Wherever I was, whatever I tried to amuse myself with, all my thoughts were occupied with the image of Olesya, my whole being strained toward her, each memory of her sometimes most insignificant words, of her gestures and smiles squeezed my heart with a quiet and sweet pain. But evening would come, and I would sit long beside her on the low rickety stool, feeling with vexation that I was more and more shy, awkward and at a loss for words.

One day I spent a whole day in this way near Olesya. From the morning I had felt unwell, though I could not yet define clearly in what my indisposition consisted. Towards evening I grew worse. My head became heavy, there was a noise in my ears, in the crown of my head I felt a dull incessant pain, as if someone were pressing on it with a soft but strong hand. My mouth was dry, and all over my body a sort of lazy, languid weakness constantly spread, from which every minute I wanted to yawn and stretch. In my eyes I felt such pain as if I had just been looking fixedly and close at a shining point.

And when late in the evening I was returning home, right in the middle of the way I was suddenly seized and shaken by a violent fit of shivering. I walked almost without seeing the road, almost without realising where I was going, staggering like a drunk, while my jaws knocked against each other in a rapid, loud rattle.

To this day I do not know who brought me home... For exactly six days the relentless, terrible Polesye fever beat me. By day the illness seemed to subside, and consciousness returned to me. Then, completely exhausted by the disease, I barely wandered about the room with pain and weakness in my knees; at every stronger movement the blood rushed in a hot wave to my head and veiled all objects before my eyes with darkness. In the evening, usually at about seven o'clock, the fit of illness swept upon me like a storm, and I spent in bed a terrible night, long as a century, now shaking under the blanket with cold, now burning with unbearable heat. No sooner did drowsiness lightly touch me than strange, absurd, painfully motley dreams began to play with my inflamed brain.

All my visions were full of petty, microscopic details, piling up and clinging to one another in hideous confusion. Now it seemed to me that I was taking apart some many-coloured boxes of fanciful shapes, taking small ones out of big ones, and still smaller ones out of the small ones, and I could not stop this endless work, which had long seemed loathsome to me. Now long bright strips of wallpaper flashed before my eyes with stupefying speed, and on them, instead of patterns, I saw with amazing distinctness whole garlands of human faces, at times beautiful, kind and smiling, at times making dreadful grimaces, putting out their tongues, baring their teeth and rolling enormous eyeballs. Then I entered with Yarmola into a tangled, unusually complicated abstract dispute.

With every minute the arguments we put to each other grew subtler and deeper; separate words and even letters of words suddenly took on a mysterious, immeasurable meaning, and at the same time a squeamish horror seized me more and more strongly before the unknown, unnatural force that wrung out of my head one after another hideous sophisms and did not let me break off the dispute, long since grown loathsome...

It was some boiling whirlwind of human and animal figures, landscapes, objects of the most amazing forms and colours, words and phrases, whose meaning was perceived by all the senses... But, strange to say, at the same time I did not cease to see on the ceiling a bright even circle thrown by the lamp with the green scorched shade. And I knew for some reason that in this calm circle with blurred edges there lurked a silent, monotonous, mysterious and menacing life, still more uncanny and oppressive than the furious chaos of my dreams.

Then I would wake, or rather not wake, but suddenly find myself awake. Consciousness almost returned to me. I understood that I was lying in bed, that I was ill, that I had just been raving, but the bright circle on the dark ceiling still frightened me with a hidden sinister menace. With a weak hand I reached for my watch, looked at it and with wistful bewilderment became convinced that the whole endless procession of my hideous dreams had taken no more than two or three minutes. “Lord! When will the dawn come!” I thought in despair, tossing my head on the hot pillows and feeling my own heavy and short breath scorch my lips... But then thin drowsiness would seize me again, and again my brain became the plaything of a motley nightmare, and again after two minutes I woke, gripped by deadly anguish...

After six days my strong constitution, with the help of quinine and an infusion of plantain, conquered the illness. I rose from bed all broken, barely keeping on my feet. Recovery went on with greedy swiftness. In my head, tired by six days of feverish delirium, I now felt a lazy and pleasant absence of thoughts. My appetite appeared doubled, and my body grew stronger by the hour, drinking in with every particle health and the joy of life. At the same time I was drawn with new force to the forest, to the lonely crooked hut. My nerves had not yet recovered, and every time, calling to memory Olesya's face and voice, I felt such a tender emotion that I wanted to weep.

## X · The Moonlit Clearing

Five more days passed, and I grew so strong that I walked on foot, without the slightest fatigue, to the cabin on hen's legs. When I stepped on its threshold, my heart beat in my breast with anxious fear. For almost two weeks I had not seen Olesya, and now I understood especially clearly how near and dear she was to me. Holding the door handle, I lingered a few seconds and could hardly breathe. In my irresolution I even closed my eyes for some time before pushing the door...

Impressions like those which followed my entrance can never be sorted out... Can one remember the words spoken in the first moments of meeting by a mother and son, a husband and wife, or two lovers? The simplest, most everyday phrases are said, even ridiculous if recorded exactly on paper. But here every word is fitting and infinitely sweet already because it is said by the dearest voice in the world.

I remember, I remember very clearly only that Olesya's pale face turned quickly to me, and that on this lovely face, new to me, there was reflected in one moment, replacing one another, bewilderment, fright, alarm and a tender, radiant smile of love... The old woman was mumbling something, shuffling beside me, but I did not hear her greetings. Olesya's voice reached me like sweet music:

“What has happened to you? Were you ill? Oh, how thin you have grown, my poor one.”

For a long time I could not answer her anything, and we stood silently facing each other, holding hands, looking straight, deeply and joyfully into each other's eyes. These few silent seconds I always consider the happiest in my life: never, never, neither before nor after, did I experience such pure, full, all-absorbing rapture. And how much I read in Olesya's large dark eyes: both the agitation of meeting, and reproach for my long absence, and a hot confession of love... I felt that with this look Olesya gave me joyfully, without any conditions or hesitation, her whole being.

She was the first to break this enchantment, pointing me with a slow movement of her eyelids to Manuilikha. We sat down side by side, and Olesya began to question me in detail and with care about the course of my illness, about the medicines I had taken, about the words and opinions of the doctor (who had come twice to me from the little town). She made me tell her about the doctor several times in a row, and at times I noticed a fleeting mocking smile on her lips.

“Ah, why did I not know that you had fallen ill!” she exclaimed with impatient regret. “I would have put you on your feet in one day... Well, how can one trust them when they understand nothing, no-thing? Why did you not send for me?”

I faltered.

“You see, Olesya... it happened so suddenly... and besides, I was afraid to trouble you. Lately you have become somehow strange with me, as if you were always angry with me or I had become a bore to you... Listen, Olesya,” I added, lowering my voice, “we have much, much to talk about, you and I... only us two... you understand?”

She quietly lowered her eyelids in sign of consent, then glanced fearfully at her grandmother and whispered quickly:

“Yes... I wanted to myself... later... wait...”

As soon as the sun set, Olesya began to hurry me to go home.

“Get ready, get ready quickly,” she said, drawing me by the hand from the bench. “If the damp seizes you now, the illness will come back at once.”

“And where are you going, Olesya?” Manuilikha asked suddenly, seeing that her granddaughter had hastily thrown a large grey woollen shawl over her head.

“I'll go... see him off a little way,” Olesya answered.

She said this indifferently, looking not at her grandmother but out of the window, but in her voice I caught a barely noticeable shade of irritation.

“You'll go after all?” the old woman asked again with emphasis.

Olesya's eyes flashed and fixed straight on Manuilikha's face.

“Yes, and I'll go!” she retorted haughtily. “It has been spoken of long ago, and spoken over... My business, and my answer.”

“Ah, you!..” the old woman exclaimed with vexation and reproach.

She wanted to add something more but only waved her hand, shuffled on her trembling gait into the corner and, groaning, began to fuss over some basket there.

I understood that this quick, displeased exchange, to which I had just been a witness, was the continuation of a long series of mutual quarrels and outbursts. Going down beside Olesya to the pine forest, I asked her:

“Granny doesn't want you to go walking with me? Is that so?”

Olesya shrugged her shoulders with vexation.

“Please don't pay attention to it. Well, yes, she doesn't want... So what!.. Am I not free to do what I like?”

An irresistible desire to reproach Olesya for her former sternness suddenly rose in me.

“So even before my illness you could, too, but did not want to be alone with me... Ah, Olesya, if you knew what pain you caused me... I waited so, I waited so every evening for you to go with me again... And you, as a rule, were so inattentive, dull, cross... Oh, how you tormented me, Olesya!..”

“Come, stop, my dear... Forget it,” Olesya begged with a soft apology in her voice.

“No, I am not saying it as a reproach to you, only it came up in speaking... Now I understand why it was so... But at first, truly, it is even funny to recall, I thought you were offended with me over the constable. And this thought grieved me greatly. It seemed to me that you considered me such a distant, strange man that even a simple friendly service from me was hard for you to accept... That was very bitter to me... I did not suspect, Olesya, that it all came from your granny...”

Olesya's face suddenly flushed a bright red.

“And not at all from granny!.. I myself did not want it!” she exclaimed hotly, with spirit.

I looked at her from the side, so that I could see the pure, delicate profile of her slightly bowed head. Only now did I notice that Olesya herself had grown thinner during this time and bluish shadows lay around her eyes. Feeling my glance, Olesya raised her eyes to me but at once dropped them and turned away with a shy smile.

“Why did you not want it, Olesya? Why?” I asked in a voice breaking with agitation, and, seizing Olesya by the hand, made her stop.

We were at that moment exactly in the middle of a long, narrow forest clearing, straight as an arrow. Tall, slender pines stood close around us on both sides, forming a gigantic corridor stretching away into the distance with a vault of fragrant interwoven branches. The bare, peeling trunks were tinted with the crimson reflection of the dying sunset...

“Why? Why, Olesya?” I repeated in a whisper, pressing her hand ever harder.

“I could not... I was afraid,” Olesya said barely audibly. “I thought one could get away from fate... And now... now...”

She gasped, as if she lacked air, and suddenly her arms wound quickly and tightly round my neck, and Olesya's hurried, trembling whisper sweetly burned my lips:

“Now I don't care, I don't care!.. Because I love you, my dear one, my happiness, my beloved!..”

She pressed closer to me, and I felt how her strong, firm, hot body trembled under my hands, how often her heart beat against my breast. Her passionate kisses poured into my head, not yet strengthened after illness, like heady wine, and I began to lose self-control.

“Olesya, for God's sake, don't... leave me,” I said, trying to unclasp her arms. “Now I am afraid too... afraid of myself... Let me go, Olesya.”

She raised her face upward, and all of it was lit with a languid, slow smile.

“Don't be afraid, my dearest,” she said with an inexpressible look of tender caress and touching boldness. “I shall never reproach you, I shall be jealous of no one... Only say: do you love me?”

“I love you, Olesya. I have loved you long and I love you strongly. But... don't kiss me any more... I am growing weak, my head is spinning, I can't answer for myself...”

Her lips again clung to mine long and tormentingly sweetly, and I did not hear but rather guessed her words:

“Well, then don't be afraid and don't think of anything else... Today is our day, and no one will take it from us...”

And all that night melted into some magic, enchanting fairy tale. The moon rose, and its radiance coloured the forest fantastically, motley and mysteriously, lay in the midst of the gloom in nervous, bluish-pale patches on the gnarled trunks, on the crooked boughs, on the moss soft as a plush carpet. The slender trunks of the birches shone white, sharp and distinct, and over their sparse leaves silvery, transparent gauze veils seemed to be thrown. In places the light did not penetrate at all under the thick canopy of pine branches. There stood full, impenetrable darkness, and only in the very middle of it a ray, slipping from who knows where, suddenly lit up brightly a long row of trees and threw a narrow regular path on the ground, so bright, adorned and lovely, like an alley decked by elves for the solemn procession of Oberon and Titania. And we walked, embracing, amid this smiling living legend, without a single word, overwhelmed by our happiness and the uncanny silence of the forest.

“My dear, I had quite forgotten that you must hurry home,” Olesya suddenly recollected herself. “How nasty of me! You have only just recovered, and I have kept you in the forest all this time.”

I embraced her and put back the shawl from her thick dark hair and, bending to her ear, asked barely audibly:

“Do you not regret it, Olesya? You don't repent?”

She slowly shook her head.

“No, no... Whatever may happen afterwards, I shall not regret it. I am so happy...”

“And must something necessarily happen?”

In her eyes flashed the reflection of the mystic horror familiar to me.

“Oh, yes, necessarily... Do you remember, I told you about the lady of clubs? That lady of clubs is I; it is I that the misfortune will befall of which the cards spoke... You know, I wanted to ask you to stop coming to us altogether. And just then you fell ill, and for nearly a fortnight I did not see you... And such longing for you seized me, such sadness, that I think I'd have given everything in the world just to be with you one little minute more... That is when I made up my mind. Let what will be, I thought, be, but I'll give my joy to no one...”

“That is true, Olesya. It was so with me too,” I said, touching my lips to her temple. “I did not know that I loved you until I parted from you. Not for nothing, it seems, did someone say that separation is to love what wind is to fire: it puts out a small love and fans a great one still stronger.”

“How did you say it? Repeat it, repeat it, please,” Olesya was interested.

I repeated once more this saying, whose author I do not know. Olesya pondered, and I saw by the movement of her lips that she was repeating my words.

I gazed closely into her pale, thrown-back face, into her large black eyes with bright flashes of moonlight shining in them, and a vague presentiment of approaching trouble suddenly crept like a sudden chill into my soul.

## XI · A Proposal

Almost a whole month the naive, enchanting fairy tale of our love went on, and to this day, together with Olesya's beautiful image, there live in my soul with undying force those blazing evening sunsets, those dewy mornings fragrant with lilies of the valley and honey, full of brisk freshness and the ringing din of birds, those hot, languid, lazy June days... Not once in all this time did boredom, or weariness, or my eternal passion for a wandering life stir in my soul. Like a pagan god or like a young, strong animal, I enjoyed light, warmth, the conscious joy of life and a calm, healthy, sensual love.

After my recovery old Manuilikha became so insufferably grumpy, met me with such open spite and, while I sat in the hut, moved the pots in the stove with such noisy fury, that Olesya and I preferred to meet every evening in the forest... And the majestic green loveliness of the pine forest, like a precious setting, adorned our untroubled love.

Every day I found with ever greater surprise that Olesya, this girl who had grown up in the forest and could not even read, showed in many circumstances of life a keen delicacy and a special, inborn tact. In love, in its direct, coarse sense, there are always terrible sides, which form torment and shame for nervous, artistic natures. But Olesya knew how to avoid them with such naive chastity that not once did a single bad comparison, a single cynical moment, offend our bond.

Meanwhile the time of my departure was approaching. Strictly speaking, all my official duties in Perebrod were already finished, and I deliberately put off the date of my return to town. I had not yet said a word of this to Olesya, afraid even to imagine how she would take my announcement of the need to leave. In general I was in a difficult position. Habit had put down too deep roots in me. To see Olesya daily, to hear her sweet voice and ringing laughter, to feel the tender charm of her caress, had become for me more than a necessity. On the rare days when bad weather prevented us from meeting, I felt as if lost, as if deprived of something most essential, most important in my life. Every occupation seemed tedious, superfluous, and my whole being strained to the forest, to warmth, to light, to Olesya's dear familiar face.

The thought of marrying Olesya came to my head more and more often. At first it presented itself to me only occasionally as a possible honourable way out of our relations, in the last resort. One circumstance only frightened and stopped me: I did not dare even to imagine what Olesya would be like dressed in fashionable clothes, talking in a drawing-room with the wives of my colleagues, torn out of this charming frame of the old forest full of legends and mysterious forces.

But the nearer the time of my departure came, the greater the dread of loneliness and the greater the anguish that seized me. The resolve to marry grew stronger in my soul every day, and in the end I no longer saw in it a bold challenge to society. “Good and educated men marry seamstresses and maids,” I comforted myself, “and live splendidly and to the end of their days bless the fate that pushed them to this decision. Shall I really be unhappier than others?”

One day in the middle of June, towards evening, I was waiting as usual for Olesya at a bend of the narrow forest path between bushes of flowering hawthorn. From afar I recognised the light, quick sound of her steps.

“Good evening, my dear,” said Olesya, embracing me and breathing hard. “Have you been waiting long? And I could barely, barely get away... I've been fighting with granny all the time.”

“She has not quieted down yet?”

“Not at all! ‘You,’ she says, ‘will perish because of him... He'll amuse himself with you to his heart's content and then throw you over. He doesn't love you at all...’”

“She says that about me?”

“About you, darling... Anyway, I don't believe one word of hers.”

“And does she know everything?”

“I can't say for certain... I think she knows. I say nothing to her about it, though; she guesses herself. Well, what is the use of thinking about it... Come.”

She broke off a hawthorn branch with a luxuriant cluster of white flowers and stuck it in her hair. We went slowly along the path, which was just turning pink in the evening sun.

Only the night before I had resolved to speak out by all means that evening. But a strange timidity weighed on my tongue. I thought: if I tell Olesya about my departure and about marriage, will she believe me? Will it not seem to her that by my proposal I only lessen, soften the first pain of the wound being dealt her? “As soon as we reach that maple with the stripped trunk, I'll begin at once,” I appointed to myself mentally. We came level with the maple, and I, turning pale with agitation, was already drawing breath to begin to speak, but suddenly my courage weakened, resolving itself into a nervous, painful beating of the heart and a cold feeling in my mouth.

“Twenty-seven is my fateful number,” I thought a few minutes later, “I'll count to twenty-seven, and then!..” And I began to count in my head, but when I reached twenty-seven, I felt that resolution had not yet ripened in me. “No,” I said to myself, “better I'll go on counting to sixty (that will make exactly a whole minute), and then without fail, without fail...”

“What is the matter with you today?” Olesya asked suddenly. “You are thinking of something unpleasant. What has happened to you?”

Then I spoke, but spoke in a tone loathsome to myself, with an assumed unnatural carelessness, as though the matter concerned the most trifling subject.

“Indeed, there is a small unpleasantness... you have guessed, Olesya... You see, my service here is finished, and my superiors are summoning me to town.”

In a glance, from the side, I looked at Olesya and saw how the colour drained from her face and how her lips trembled. But she did not answer me a word. For a few minutes I walked silently beside her. In the grass grasshoppers shrilled loudly, and from somewhere far away came the monotonous, strained creaking of a corncrake.

“You understand yourself, of course, Olesya,” I began again, “that it is inconvenient and impossible for me to stay here, and, after all, one cannot neglect one's service...”

“No... well... there's nothing to say,” Olesya responded, seemingly calmly, but in such a dull, lifeless voice that I felt uncanny. “If it is service, then of course... you must go...”

She stopped by a tree and leaned her back against its trunk, quite pale, her arms dropped powerless along her body, with a pitiful, tormented smile on her lips. Her pallor frightened me. I rushed to her and squeezed her hands tight.

“Olesya... what is it? Olesya... dear!..”

“Nothing... forgive me... it will pass. It's... my head swam...”

She made an effort over herself and went forward, not taking her hand from mine.

“Olesya, you have thought ill of me now,” I said reproachfully. “Shame on you! Do you too think that I could leave, abandoning you? No, my dear. That is why I began this talk: I want this very day to go to your grandmother and tell her that you will be my wife.”

Quite unexpectedly for me, my words hardly surprised Olesya.

“Your wife?” She slowly and sadly shook her head. “No, Vanechka, dear, that is impossible!”

“Why, Olesya? Why?”

“No, no... You yourself understand that it is ridiculous even to think of it. What kind of wife should I really be to you? You are a gentleman, you are clever, educated, and I? I can't even read and don't know where to put my foot... You'll have nothing but shame because of me...”

“That is all nonsense, Olesya!” I objected hotly. “In half a year you will not recognise yourself. You do not even suspect how much innate intelligence and observation there is in you. We shall read many good books together, we shall get to know kind, clever people, we shall see the whole wide world, Olesya... We shall go till old age, till death itself, hand in hand, as we go now, and not be ashamed but proud of you I shall be, and thank you!..”

To my ardent speech Olesya answered with a grateful pressure of the hand, but went on standing her ground.

“And is that the only thing?.. Perhaps you don't know yet?.. I have never told you... I have no father... I am illegitimate...”

“Stop, Olesya... That least of all stops me. What do I care about your relatives if you yourself are dearer to me than father and mother, dearer than the whole world? No, these are all trifles, all these are empty excuses!..”

Olesya pressed her shoulder to mine with quiet, submissive tenderness.

“My dear... It would be better if you had not begun this talk at all... You are young, free... Could I have the heart to bind you hand and foot for your whole life... And what if you take a liking to another afterwards? Then you would hate me, you would curse the day and hour when I agreed to go with you. Don't be angry, my dear!” she exclaimed pleadingly, seeing by my face that these words were unpleasant to me. “I do not want to hurt you. I think only of your happiness. Finally, you have forgotten granny. Come, judge for yourself: would it be right of me to leave her alone?”

“Well... there will be room for granny with us too. (To confess, the thought of granny made me wince badly.) And if she doesn't want to live with us, there are in every town such houses... they are called almshouses... where such old women are given both peace and attentive care...”

“No, what are you saying! She won't go anywhere out of the forest. She is afraid of people.”

“Well, then you must think yourself, Olesya, what is best. You will have to choose between me and granny. But know one thing: that without you life will be loathsome to me too.”

“My sunshine!” Olesya said with deep tenderness. “Thanks to you for these words alone... You have warmed my heart... But still I won't marry you... Rather I'll go with you just so, if you don't drive me away... Only don't hurry, please, don't rush me. Give me a day or two, I'll think all this over well... And I must talk with granny too.”

“Listen, Olesya,” I asked, struck by a new guess. “Perhaps you are again... afraid of the church?”

Perhaps I should have begun with this question. Almost daily I had argued with Olesya, trying to dissuade her from the imagined curse that weighed on her family together with the possession of magic powers. In truth, in every Russian intellectual there sits a little of the enlightener. It is in our blood, it has been instilled in us by all Russian fiction of the last decades. Who knows? If Olesya had been deeply devout, had strictly kept the fasts and missed no church service, it is quite possible that I should then have been ironical (only slightly, for I have always been a believer) about her religiousness and developed in her a critical inquiring turn of mind. But she professed with firm and naive conviction her communion with the dark powers and her estrangement from God, of whom she was even afraid to speak.

In vain I attempted to shake Olesya's superstition. All my logical conclusions, all my sometimes coarse and spiteful mockery, shattered against her submissive certainty of her mysterious fatal vocation.

“Are you afraid of the church, Olesya?” I repeated.

She bowed her head in silence.

“Do you think that God will not receive you?” I went on with growing heat. “That He will not have enough mercy for you? He who, commanding millions of angels, nevertheless came down to earth and accepted a terrible, shameful death to redeem all men? He who did not disdain the repentance of the very last of women and promised the murderous robber that this very day he would be with Him in paradise?..”

All this was not new to Olesya in my interpretation, but this time she did not even listen to me. With a quick movement she threw off her kerchief and, crumpling it, threw it in my face. A scuffle began. I tried to take the hawthorn flower from her. Resisting, she fell on the ground and drew me down after her, laughing joyfully and stretching out to me her lips, moist and sweet, opened by her quick breathing...

Late at night, when we had said goodbye and had already parted by quite a distance, I suddenly heard behind me Olesya's voice:

“Vanechka! Wait a minute... I'll tell you something!”

I turned and went to meet her. Olesya ran hurriedly up to me. In the sky already stood a thin silver jagged sickle of the young moon, and by its pale light I saw that Olesya's eyes were full of big unshed tears.

“Olesya, what is it?” I asked anxiously.

She seized my hands and began to kiss them one after the other.

“Darling... how good you are! How kind you are!” she said in a trembling voice. “I was just walking and thought: how you love me!.. And do you know, I terribly want to do something for you, something very, very pleasant.”

“Olesya... my dear girl, calm yourself...”

“Listen, tell me,” she went on, “would you be very pleased if I ever went to church? Only the truth, the real truth tell me.”

I pondered. A superstitious thought suddenly flashed in my head: might not some misfortune come of it?

“Why are you silent? Well, say quickly, would you be glad of it or is it all the same to you?”

“How shall I say it, Olesya?” I began with a stammer. “Well, yes, perhaps it would be pleasant to me. I have told you many times that a man may not believe, may doubt, even laugh at last. But a woman... a woman should be devout without reasoning. In that simple and tender trustfulness with which she gives herself under God's protection I always feel something touching, womanly and beautiful.”

I fell silent. Olesya did not respond either, hiding her head against my breast.

“And why did you ask me that?” I asked with curiosity.

She suddenly started.

“Oh, nothing... I just asked... Don't pay attention. Well, goodbye, dear. Come tomorrow.”

She disappeared. For a long time I still gazed into the darkness, listening to the quick steps receding from me. Suddenly a sudden horror of presentiment seized me. I irresistibly wanted to run after Olesya, catch her up and beg, implore, even demand, if need be, that she should not go to church. But I restrained my unexpected impulse and even, I remember, setting off on my way, I said aloud:

“It seems you yourself, my dear Vanya, have caught the superstition.”

Oh, my God! Why did I not obey then the vague attraction of the heart, which, I now believe absolutely, never errs in its quick secret presentiments.

## XII · Trinity Day

The day after this meeting fell exactly on the feast of the Holy Trinity, which that year coincided with the day of the great martyr Timothy, when, according to folk tales, there are signs foretelling a failed harvest. The village of Perebrod was reckoned, ecclesiastically, a dependent parish; that is, although it had a church of its own, it had no separate priest, and only now and then, in Lent and on great feasts, the priest of the village of Volchye came.

That day I had to ride on official business to the neighbouring little town, and I set off about eight in the morning, while it was still cool, on horseback. For my journeys I had long ago bought myself a small stallion of six or seven years, of the local unprepossessing breed but very lovingly and carefully tended by his former owner, a county surveyor. The horse was called Tarantchik. I had grown very attached to this dear animal with strong, slender, shapely legs, with a shaggy forelock from under which fiery little eyes looked out angrily and distrustfully, with firm, energetically pressed lips. His colour was rather rare and comical: all grey, mouse-coloured, and only on the croup ran mottled white and black spots.

I had to ride through the whole village. The large green square running from the church to the tavern was entirely occupied by long rows of carts in which peasants of the neighbouring villages, Volosha, Zulnya and Pechalovka, had come to the festival with their wives and children. People bustled among the carts. In spite of the early hour and strict regulations, drunken men were already seen among them (vodka on feast days and at night was sold on the sly by the former tavern-keeper Srul). The morning was windless, stifling. The air was sultry, and the day promised to be unbearably hot. On the heated sky, as if covered with silvery dust, not a single cloud appeared.

Having done everything I needed in the little town, I took a hasty bite at the inn of stuffed Jewish pike, washed it down with abominable, cloudy beer, and set off home. But, passing the smithy, I remembered that Tarantchik's shoe on the left fore had long been loose, and stopped to have the horse reshod. This took me another hour and a half, so that when I rode up to the outskirts of Perebrod it was between four and five in the afternoon.

The whole square swarmed with a drunken, clamouring crowd. The fence and porch of the tavern were literally packed with customers, pushing and crushing one another; the Perebrod peasants mingled with the visitors, sitting on the grass in the shade of the carts. Everywhere were seen heads thrown back and bottles raised up. There was not one sober man left. The general intoxication had reached that limit when a peasant begins to exaggerate his drunkenness boisterously and boastfully, when all his movements take on a slack and heavy sweep, when instead of, say, nodding his head in assent, he sinks down with his whole body, bends his knees and, suddenly losing his balance, helplessly staggers backward. Children romped and squealed there too, under the feet of horses calmly chewing hay. In another place a woman, hardly able to keep on her feet herself, was dragging home, weeping and swearing, by the sleeve her resisting, hideously drunk husband...

In the shade of the fence a dense knot of some twenty peasants, men and women, sat close around a blind lyre-player, and his trembling, nasal tenor, accompanied by the ringing monotonous buzz of the instrument, stood out sharply from the continuous hum of the crowd. From afar I heard the familiar words of a “duma,” an old ballad:

Oh, the star rose, the evening star, over Pochaev it stood. Oh, the Turkish host came forth, like a black cloud...

Further this ballad tells how the Turks, unable to take the Pochaev monastery by assault, resolved to take it by cunning. With this object they sent, as if in gift to the monastery, an enormous candle stuffed with gunpowder. They brought this candle on twelve pairs of oxen, and the delighted monks were already about to light it before the icon of the Mother of God of Pochaev, but God did not allow the villainous design to be accomplished.

And the senior reader dreamed: do not take that candle, carry it out into the open field, hew it with axes.

And so the monks carried it out into the open field, began to hew it, and bullets and cartridges began flying, away, in all directions...

The unbearably hot air seemed entirely saturated with a loathsome mixed smell of burnt vodka, onions, sheepskin coats, strong coarse tobacco and the exhalations of dirty human bodies. Making my way carefully among the people and with difficulty holding back Tarantchik, who tossed his head, I could not fail to notice that from all sides unceremonious, curious and hostile glances followed me. Contrary to custom, not one man took off his cap, but the noise seemed to die down at my appearance. Suddenly somewhere in the very middle of the crowd a drunken, hoarse shout rang out, which I did not hear clearly, but in answer to it came restrained laughter. A woman's voice began to remonstrate fearfully with the bawler:

“Hush, you fool... What are you yelling for! He'll hear...”

“And what if he hears?” the peasant went on provokingly. “Is he my superior or what? He only, in the forest, with his own...”

A repulsive, long, dreadful phrase hung in the air together with a burst of frenzied laughter. I turned my horse sharply back and convulsively gripped the handle of my whip, seized by that mad fury which sees nothing, thinks of nothing and fears nothing. And suddenly a strange, painful, wistful thought flashed through my head: “All this has happened once before, many, many years ago in my life... The sun was blazing just as hot... The huge square was just so flooded with a noisy, excited crowd... I turned back just so in a fit of furious wrath... But where was it? When? When?..” I lowered the whip and galloped home.

Yarmola, coming slowly out of the kitchen, took the horse from me and said roughly:

“There's a steward from the Marinovka estate sitting in your room, young master.”

It seemed to me that he wanted to add something more, something very important for me and unpleasant, it even seemed to me that a fleeting expression of malicious mockery slid across his face. I deliberately lingered in the doorway and looked back at Yarmola with defiance. But he was already, without looking at me, dragging the horse by the bridle, which stretched out its neck and stepped carefully with its feet.

In my room I found the clerk of the neighbouring estate, Nikita Nazarych Mishchenko. He wore a grey jacket with enormous ginger checks, narrow cornflower-blue trousers and a fiery-red tie, with his hair pomaded and parted in the middle, all fragrant with Persian lilac. Seeing me, he jumped up from his chair and began to scrape and bow, not bowing but somehow breaking in the small of his back, with a smile that bared the pale gums of both jaws.

“I have the honour to bow,” Nikita Nazarych chattered amiably. “Very pleased to see you... I've been waiting for you here since mass. It's long since I saw you, I even missed you. Why do you never look in on us? Our Stepan young ladies even laugh at you.”

And suddenly, seized by a sudden recollection, he burst into uncontrollable laughter.

“There, I tell you, was a lark today!” he exclaimed, choking and spluttering. “Ha-ha-ha-ha... I even split my sides laughing!..”

“What is it? What lark?” I asked roughly, not hiding my displeasure.

“After mass a scandal took place here,” Nikita Nazarych went on, interrupting his speech with volleys of laughter. “The Perebrod girls... No, by God, I can't stand it... The Perebrod girls caught a witch here in the square... That is, of course, they consider her a witch in their peasant ignorance... Well, and didn't they give her a shaking!.. They wanted to smear her with tar, but she wriggled out somehow, got away...”

A terrible guess flashed in my mind. I rushed to the clerk and, beside myself with agitation, gripped him hard by the shoulder.

“What are you saying!” I cried in a frenzied voice. “Stop neighing, the devil take you! What witch are you talking about?”

He suddenly stopped laughing at once and goggled at me with round, frightened eyes.

“I... I... truly, I don't know, sir,” he stammered in confusion. “It seems some Samuilikha... Manuilikha... or... Allow me... The daughter of some Manuilikha?.. The peasants were chattering something of the sort here, but, to confess, I did not remember.”

I made him tell me in order all that he had seen and heard. He spoke absurdly, incoherently, tangled in details, and every minute I interrupted him with impatient questions and exclamations, almost curses. From his story I understood very little and only two months later reconstructed the whole sequence of this accursed event from the words of its eyewitness, the wife of the government forester, who had also been at mass that day.

My presentiment had not deceived me. Olesya had overcome her fear and come to church; although she arrived only in the middle of the service and stood in the church porch, her coming was noticed at once by all the peasants who were in the church. All through the service the women whispered and looked back.

However, Olesya found in herself enough strength to stand to the end of the mass. Perhaps she did not understand the real meaning of these hostile looks, perhaps out of pride she disregarded them. But when she came out of the church, then right by the fence a knot of women surrounded her on all sides, growing every minute larger and larger and pressing ever closer around Olesya. At first they only silently and unceremoniously stared at the helpless girl, who looked fearfully about her. Then crude jeers, strong words, curses showered, accompanied by laughter; then separate exclamations merged into a general piercing women's din in which nothing could be made out and which wound up still more the nerves of the roused crowd. Several times Olesya tried to pass through this living terrible ring, but she was constantly pushed back into the middle.

Suddenly a shrill old woman's voice shrieked from somewhere behind the crowd: “Smear her with tar, the bitch!” (It is known that in Little Russia the smearing with tar even of the gates of a house where a girl lives is for her tied up with the greatest, indelible disgrace.) Almost in the same moment over the heads of the raging women appeared a tar pot with a brush, passed from hand to hand.

Then Olesya, in a fit of rage, horror and despair, threw herself on the first of her tormentors who came to hand so impetuously that she knocked her off her feet. At once a scuffle boiled up on the ground, and dozens of bodies mingled into one common screaming mass. But Olesya, by some miracle, managed to slip out of this tangle, and she ran headlong along the road, without a kerchief, her clothes torn to rags, through which in many places her bare body was visible. After her, together with abuse, laughter and hooting, stones flew. But only a few pursued her, and those soon fell behind... Having run some fifty paces, Olesya stopped, turned to the infuriated crowd her pale, scratched, bloodied face and cried so loudly that every word of hers was heard in the square:

“Very well!.. You will remember this yet! You'll all weep your fill yet!”

This threat, as the same eyewitness told me later, was pronounced with such passionate hatred, in such a resolute, prophetic tone, that for a moment the whole crowd seemed to freeze, but only for a moment, because at once a new burst of abuse rang out.

I repeat that many details of this incident I learned much later. I had not strength or patience to listen to the end of Mishchenko's story. I suddenly remembered that Yarmola had surely not yet unsaddled the horse, and, without saying a word to the astonished clerk, I went hastily out into the yard. Yarmola was indeed still walking Tarantchik along the fence. I quickly bridled the horse, tightened the girths and, going round, so as not again to make my way through the drunken crowd, galloped to the forest.

## XIII · The Hare and the Wolf

It is impossible to describe the state in which I was during my furious gallop. At moments I quite forgot where and why I was going; there remained only a vague consciousness that something irreparable, absurd and terrible had happened, a consciousness like the heavy causeless anxiety that sometimes seizes a man in a feverish nightmare. And at the same time, how strange! there did not cease to quiver in my head, in time with the horse's hoofbeats, the nasal, broken voice of the blind lyre-player:

Oh, the Turkish host came forth, like a black cloud...

Having reached the narrow path leading straight to Manuilikha's hut, I dismounted from Tarantchik, on whom, along the edges of the saddle-cloth and in the places where his skin touched the harness, thick foam stood out in white lumps, and led him by the bridle. From the strong heat of the day and from the fast riding the blood roared in my head as if pumped by some enormous, ceaseless pump.

Having tied the horse to the wattle fence, I went into the hut. At first it seemed to me that Olesya was not at home, and my chest and mouth even turned cold with fear, but a minute later I saw her lying on the bed, face to the wall, her head hidden in the pillows. She did not even turn at the noise of the opening door.

Manuilikha, sitting right there beside her on the ground, got to her feet with difficulty and waved her arms at me.

“Quiet! Don't make a noise, you accursed one,” she whispered with menace, coming right up to me. And, looking straight into my eyes with her faded, cold eyes, she hissed spitefully:

“Well? You've played your game, my dear?”

“Listen, granny,” I objected sternly, “this is not the time for scores and reproaches. What is the matter with Olesya?”

“Sh-h... quiet! Olesya is lying unconscious, that's what is the matter with Olesya... If you had not poked in where you had no business, and not babbled nonsense to the girl, nothing bad would have happened. And I, the old fool, looked on and indulged it... But my heart sensed trouble... It sensed something evil from that very day when you almost by force burst into our hut. What? Will you say it was not you who egged her on to drag herself to church?” the old woman suddenly flew at me with a face twisted with hatred. “Not you, accursed young master? Don't lie, and don't wag your fox's tail, shameless one! Why did you need to lure her to church?”

“I didn't lure her, granny... I give you my word on that. She wanted it herself.”

“Ah, my woe, woe!” Manuilikha clapped her hands. “She came running back from there, with no face on her, all her shift torn to shreds... Bareheaded... She tells how it all was, and herself laughs and weeps by turns... Just like a regular shrieker. She lay down on the bed... kept weeping, and then I see she seems to doze. And I, the old fool, was glad: now, I think, it will all pass off with sleep. I look, her arm has hung down, I think, I must straighten it, the arm will go numb... I touched her, my dove, by the hand, and she is all burning with heat... So the fever has begun with her... For an hour she talked without stopping, so fast and so pitifully... Only just now she fell silent for a minute. What have you done? What have you done to her?” the old woman exclaimed with a new surge of despair.

And suddenly her brown face gathered into a monstrous, repulsive grimace of weeping: her lips stretched and dropped down at the corners, all the facial muscles tensed and trembled, her brows rose, wrinkling her forehead in deep folds, and from her eyes, unusually fast, tears as big as peas poured. Clasping her head with her hands and laying her elbows on the table, she began to rock back and forth with her whole body and wailed in a sing-song half-voice:

“My little daughter-r-r! My dear little grand-daughter-r-r!.. Oh, bitter is my sorr-r-row, ach-h-hing...”

“Don't bawl, old woman,” I interrupted Manuilikha roughly. “You'll wake her!”

The old woman fell silent but with the same terrible grimace on her face went on rocking back and forth while big tears fell on the table... About ten minutes passed in this way. I sat beside Manuilikha and listened wistfully to a fly beating on the window pane with a monotonous, broken buzz.

“Granny!” Olesya's weak, barely audible voice rang out suddenly. “Granny, who is with us?”

Manuilikha hobbled hurriedly to the bed and at once began to wail again:

“Oh, my little grand-daughter, my own-n-n! Oh, bitter for me, old, ach-h-hing...”

“Ah, granny, stop it!” Olesya said with pitiful entreaty and suffering in her voice. “Who is sitting in our hut?”

I went cautiously, on tiptoe, to the bed, with that awkward, guilty awareness of one's own health and one's own coarseness which one always feels near a sick person.

“It is I, Olesya,” I said, lowering my voice. “I have just ridden from the village... And all morning I was in town... You don't feel well, Olesya?”

Not taking her face from the pillows, she stretched her bare arm backward, as if seeking something in the air. I understood this movement and took her hot hand in mine. Two enormous blue marks, one above the wrist and the other above the elbow, stood out sharply on the white, tender skin.

“My dear,” Olesya began, slowly, with difficulty separating one word from another. “I want... to look at you... but I cannot... They have... disfigured me all over... Do you remember... you... liked my face so?.. It's true, you did, my own?.. And I was always so glad of it... And now you will be disgusted... to look at me... Well, so... I do not want...”

“Olesya, forgive me,” I whispered, bending to her very ear.

Her burning hand pressed mine firmly and long.

“Oh, what!.. What, my dear?.. Aren't you ashamed even to think of it? What are you guilty of here? It's all I alone, silly... Well, why did I go... really? No, my sun, don't blame yourself...”

“Olesya, allow me... Only first promise that you will allow...”

“I promise, my dear... anything you want...”

“Allow me, please, to send for the doctor... I beg you! Well, if you like, you need not do anything of what he orders. But agree at least for me, Olesya.”

“Oh, dear... What a trap you have caught me in! No, better allow me not to keep my promise. If I were really ill, lying at death's door, even then I would not let a doctor near me. And now am I ill? It's only from fright that I have become like this, it will pass by evening. And if not, granny will give me a tincture of lily of the valley or brew raspberry in the teapot. Why a doctor here? You are my best doctor. There, you have come, and at once I feel easier... Ah, only one thing is bad for me: I want to look at you with one little eye, but I'm afraid...”

With a tender effort I lifted her head from the pillow. Olesya's face blazed with a feverish flush, her dark eyes shone unnaturally bright, her dry lips quivered nervously. Long red scratches furrowed her forehead, cheeks and neck. Dark bruises were on her forehead and under her eyes.

“Don't look at me... I beg you... I am ugly now,” Olesya whispered pleadingly, trying to cover my eyes with her palm.

My heart overflowed with pity. I pressed my lips to Olesya's hand, lying motionless on the blanket, and began to cover it with long, quiet kisses. Earlier too I had sometimes kissed her hands, but she had always taken them from me with hurried, shy fright. Now she did not resist this caress, and with her other, free hand she softly stroked my hair.

“You know everything?” she asked in a whisper.

I bowed my head in silence. True, I had not understood everything from Nikita Nazarych's story. I only did not want Olesya to be upset by recalling the morning's incident. But suddenly at the thought of the insult to which she had been subjected a wave of uncontrollable fury rushed upon me.

“Oh! Why was I not there at that time!” I cried, straightening up and clenching my fists. “I would... I would...”

“Come, enough... enough... Don't be angry, my dear,” Olesya gently interrupted me.

I could no longer hold back the tears that had long been pressing my throat and burning my eyes. Falling with my face on Olesya's shoulder, I sobbed noiselessly and bitterly, shaking in my whole body.

“You are weeping? You are weeping?” In her voice there sounded surprise, tenderness and compassion. “My dear... Come, stop, stop... Don't torment yourself, my love... I am so well beside you. Let us not weep while we are together. Let us at least spend the last days gaily so that it will not be so hard for us to part.”

I raised my head in amazement. A vague presentiment suddenly slowly gripped my heart.

“The last days, Olesya? Why the last? Why must we part?”

Olesya closed her eyes and was silent for several seconds.

“We must say goodbye, Vanechka,” she began resolutely. “As soon as I am a little better, granny and I will go away from here at once. We cannot stay here any longer...”

“Are you afraid of something?”

“No, my dear, I am afraid of nothing, if need be. But why lead people into sin? Perhaps you don't know... I there... in Perebrod... threatened from anger and shame... And now, whenever anything happens, they will at once say it was us: whether cattle start to die or someone's hut catches fire, we shall be blamed for everything. Granny,” she addressed Manuilikha, raising her voice, “is it not true what I say?”

“What did you say, little granddaughter? I didn't hear, to tell the truth!” the old woman mumbled, coming closer and putting her palm to her ear.

“I say that now, whatever trouble may happen in Perebrod, they will pile it all on you and me.”

“Oh, true, true, Olesya: they'll pile it all on us, the unfortunate... We're not to live in the white world, they'll destroy us, you and me, quite destroy us, the accursed... And then, when they drove me out of the village... What? Wasn't it the same? I threatened... also out of vexation... one striped fool, and she, snap, her child died. That is, I was not to blame in it in the slightest, and yet they almost killed me, the damned... They began pelting me with stones... I run from them, and only guard you, little one... Well, I think, let me be hit, but why should the innocent child be hurt?.. In a word, barbarians, filthy gallows-birds!”

“But where will you go? You have no relatives or acquaintances anywhere... Finally, you need money to settle in a new place.”

“We'll manage somehow,” Olesya said carelessly. “And granny will find money; she has laid by something.”

“Fine money indeed!” the old woman objected with displeasure, moving away from the bed. “Orphan's kopecks, drenched in tears...”

“Olesya... And what about me? You don't even want to think of me!” I exclaimed, feeling a bitter, sick, unkind reproach against Olesya rise in me.

She raised herself and, unembarrassed by her grandmother's presence, took my head in her hands and kissed me several times in a row on the forehead and cheeks.

“It is of you that I think most of all, my own. Only... you see... it is not our fate to be together... that's it!.. Do you remember, I cast the cards on you? Everything has come out just as they said then. So fate does not want our happiness. And if it were not for that, do you think I should have been afraid of anything?”

“Olesya, again about your fate?” I exclaimed impatiently. “I do not want to believe in it... and I never will believe!..”

“Oh, no, no... don't say that,” Olesya whispered fearfully. “I am afraid not for myself, for you, my dear. No, better don't start talk about it at all.”

In vain I tried to dissuade Olesya, in vain I painted before her pictures of untroubled happiness which neither envious fate nor coarse, evil people would disturb. Olesya only kissed my hands and shook her head.

“No... no... no... I know, I see,” she repeated insistently. “There will be nothing for us but grief... nothing... nothing...”

Bewildered, thrown off by this superstitious obstinacy, I at last asked:

“But in any case you will let me know the day of your departure?”

Olesya pondered. Suddenly a faint smile ran across her lips.

“I will tell you a little tale on that... Once a wolf was running through the forest, saw a little hare and says to him: ‘Hare, hare, I shall eat you!’ The hare began to beg: ‘Have mercy on me, wolf, I still want to live, I have little children at home.’ The wolf does not agree. Then the hare says: ‘Well, let me at least live three more days in the world, and then eat me. All the same it will be easier for me to die.’ The wolf gave him those three days, does not eat him, but only keeps guard. One day passed, another day passed, at last the third is ending.”

“‘Well, now get ready,’ says the wolf, ‘I shall begin to eat you now.’ Here my hare wept bitter tears: ‘Ah, why did you, wolf, give me those three days! Better you had eaten me at once as soon as you saw me. Otherwise for all three days I did not live but only tormented myself!’ My dear, that hare spoke the truth. What do you think?”

I was silent, seized by a wistful presentiment of approaching loneliness. Olesya suddenly rose and sat up in bed. Her face at once became serious.

“Vanya, listen...” she said deliberately. “Tell me: while you were with me, were you happy? Was it good for you?”

“Olesya! And you still ask!”

“Wait... Did you regret that you got to know me? Did you think of another woman when you saw me?”

“Not for a moment! Not only in your presence, but even when left alone, I thought of no one but you.”

“Were you jealous of me? Were you ever displeased with me? Were you not bored with me?”

“Never, Olesya! Never!”

She laid both hands on my shoulders and looked into my eyes with inexpressible love.

“Then know, my dear, that you will never remember me ill or with malice,” she said so convincingly, as if she read the future in my eyes. “When we part, it will be hard for you at first, oh, how hard... You will weep, you will find no place anywhere. And then it will all pass, all be smoothed away. And you will think of me without grief, but lightly and joyfully.”

She again threw her head back on the pillows and whispered in a weakened voice:

“And now go, my dear... Go home, my love... I am a little tired. Wait... kiss me... Don't be afraid of granny... she will allow it. You will allow it, granny?”

“Well, say goodbye, say goodbye properly,” the old woman grumbled in displeasure. “Why hide from me?.. I've known long...”

“Kiss me here and here again... and here,” Olesya said, touching with her finger her eyes, cheeks and mouth.

“Olesya! You say goodbye to me as if we should not see each other again!” I exclaimed in fright.

“I don't know, I don't know, my dear. I know nothing. Well, go with God. No, wait... one more minute... Bend your ear to me... Do you know what I regret?” she whispered, touching her lips to my cheek. “That I have no little child by you. Ah, how glad I would have been of it!”

I went out onto the porch accompanied by Manuilikha. Half the sky was covered by a black cloud with sharp, curly edges, but the sun still shone, leaning toward the west, and in this mingling of light and approaching darkness there was something ominous. The old woman looked up, shading her eyes with her palm like an umbrella, and shook her head significantly.

“There will be a storm over Perebrod today,” she said in a convincing tone. “And, as like as not, even with hail.”

## XIV · The Hail

I was already riding up to Perebrod when a sudden whirlwind spun up and drove columns of dust along the road. The first rare, heavy drops of rain fell.

Manuilikha had not been mistaken. The storm, slowly gathering through all that hot, unbearably stifling day, burst with extraordinary force over Perebrod. Lightning flashed almost without a break, and from the peals of thunder the panes in the windows of my room shook and rang. About eight in the evening the storm died down for a few minutes, but only to begin again with fresh fury. Suddenly something poured with a deafening crash onto the roof and walls of the old house. I rushed to the window. Enormous hail, the size of a walnut, was falling headlong to the earth, bouncing high upward afterwards. I looked at the mulberry tree growing right by the house: it stood quite bare; all its leaves had been beaten off by the terrible blows of the hail... Under the window appeared the figure, barely visible in the darkness, of Yarmola, who, covered to his head with a coarse cloak, had run out of the kitchen to close the shutters.

But he was too late. Into one of the panes a huge lump of ice struck with such force that it broke, and its fragments flew ringing across the floor of the room.

I felt tired and lay down on the bed without undressing. I thought that I should not be able to sleep at all that night and that till morning I should toss from side to side in helpless anguish, so I decided I had better not take off my clothes, in order to tire myself a little afterwards by monotonous walking about the room. But a very strange thing happened to me: it seemed to me that I had closed my eyes for only a minute; and when I opened them, long bright rays of the sun were already streaming through the cracks in the shutters, and countless golden motes of dust whirled in them.

Yarmola stood over my bed. His face expressed stern anxiety and impatient expectation: he must have been waiting here a long time for me to wake.

“Young master,” he said in his dull voice in which anxiety could be heard. “Young master, you must leave here...”

I swung my legs off the bed and looked at Yarmola in amazement.

“Leave? Leave where? What for? You must have gone mad?”

“I haven't gone mad at all,” Yarmola snapped back. “Haven't you heard what yesterday's hail did? In half the village the rye is as if trampled by feet. Lame Maxim's, Kozel's, Mut's, the Prokopchuks', Gordy Olefir's... That devilish witch has sent harm indeed... may she perish!”

Suddenly, in one moment, the whole of yesterday came back to me, the threat pronounced by Olesya near the church, and her fears.

“Now the whole community is in revolt,” Yarmola went on. “Since morning they have all got drunk again and are yelling... And they cry out something bad about you too, young master... And do you know what our community is like?.. If they do anything to the witches, then so it ought to be, that's a just deed; and to you, young master, I say one thing: run away quickly.”

So Olesya's fears had been justified. I had to warn her at once of the trouble threatening her and Manuilikha. I dressed hurriedly, rinsed my face with water as I went, and half an hour later was already riding at a fast trot in the direction of Bisov Kut.

The nearer I drew to the cabin on hen's legs, the more a vague, wistful anxiety grew in me. I told myself with certainty that at any moment some new, unexpected grief would overtake me.

I almost ran along the narrow path winding over the sandy knoll. The windows of the hut were open, the door was flung wide.

“Lord! What has happened?” I whispered, entering the porch with a sinking heart.

The hut was empty. In it reigned that sad, dirty disorder which is always left after a hasty departure. Heaps of rubbish and rags lay on the floor, and in the corner stood the wooden frame of the bed...

With a constricted heart full of tears I was about to leave the hut when suddenly my attention was caught by a bright object, evidently hung on purpose on the corner of the window frame. It was a string of cheap red beads, known in Polesye by the name of “corals,” the only thing that remained to me as a memory of Olesya and of her tender, generous love.
