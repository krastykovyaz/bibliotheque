## I · The Old Gypsy

A damp, cold wind blew from the sea, carrying across the steppe the pensive melody of waves breaking on the shore and of the rustling bushes along the coast. Now and then its gusts brought wrinkled yellow leaves and tossed them on the fire, fanning the flame; the gloom of the autumn night that surrounded us shuddered and, shrinking back in fear, revealed for a moment the boundless steppe on the left, the endless sea on the right, and straight in front of me the figure of Makar Chudra, an old Gypsy, who was guarding the horses of his camp, pitched some fifty paces from us.

Paying no heed to the cold waves of wind that had blown open his chekmen, bared his hairy chest and beat on it without mercy, he half reclined in a fine, strong pose, facing me, drew steadily at his huge pipe, let thick clouds of smoke out of his mouth and nose, and, his eyes fixed on something beyond my head, in the dead-silent darkness of the steppe, he talked to me without pause and without a single movement to shield himself from the sharp blows of the wind.

“So you go on the road? That is good! You have chosen a fine lot for yourself, falcon. That is how it should be: walk and look, and when you have seen enough, lie down and die. That is all there is to it!”

“Life? Other people?” he went on, after listening sceptically to my objection to his “That is how it should be.” “Ha! And what is that to you? Are you not life yourself? Other people live without you and will live on without you. Do you think anyone needs you? You are not bread, you are not a stick, and nobody needs you at all.”

“Learn and teach, you say? Can you learn how to make people happy? No, you cannot. Grow grey first, and then say that people must be taught. Taught what? Everyone knows what he needs. The cleverer ones take what there is; the stupider ones get nothing; and each learns for himself…”

“They are funny, those people of yours. They huddle together in a heap and crush one another, and there is so much room on the earth,” he swept his arm wide over the steppe. “And they all work. What for? For whom? Nobody knows. You see a man ploughing and you think: drop by drop, with his sweat, he will pour his strength out onto the earth, and then he will lie down in it and rot. Nothing will be left of him, he sees nothing from his field, and he dies as he was born, a fool.”

“Well, was he born just to scratch at the earth and die, without even having time to scratch out a grave for himself? Does he know freedom? Does the breadth of the steppe mean anything to him? Does the talk of the sea waves gladden his heart? He is a slave, from the moment he is born a slave all his life, and that is all! What can he do with himself? Only hang himself, if he grows a little wiser.”

“Now look at me: in fifty-eight years I have seen so much that if you wrote it all down on paper, you could not fit it into a thousand bags like yours. Come, tell me, what lands have I not been in? You cannot. You do not even know the lands where I have been. That is how to live: go, go, and that is all. Do not stand long in one place; what is there in it? Look how day and night run round the earth, chasing one another; so you, too, run from thoughts about life, so as not to stop loving it. If you start to think, you will stop loving life; it is always so. It happened to me too. Ha! It happened, falcon.”

“I sat in prison, in Galicia. ‘Why do I live in the world?’ I thought out of boredom (it is dull in prison, falcon, oh, how dull!) and melancholy took hold of my heart when I looked out of the window at the field; it took hold of it and gripped it with pincers. Who can say why he lives? Nobody can say, falcon! And one should not ask oneself about it. Live, and that is all! Walk about and look around you, and then melancholy will never take you. I nearly hanged myself with my belt then, that is how it was!”

“Heh! I talked once with a certain man. A strict man, one of yours, a Russian. You must live, he says, not as you yourself want, but as it is written in the word of God. Submit to God, and he will give you everything you ask of him. And he himself was all in holes, in rags. So I told him to ask God for some new clothes. He grew angry and drove me away, cursing. And before that he had been saying that one must forgive people and love them. Well, let him forgive me, if my speech offended his worship. A teacher, too! They teach us to eat less, and themselves eat ten times a day.”

He spat into the fire and fell silent, filling his pipe again. The wind howled softly and mournfully, horses neighed in the darkness, and a tender, passionate song of reverie drifted from the camp. It was the beautiful Nonka, Makar's daughter, singing. I knew her voice, of a deep, chesty timbre, which always sounded somehow strange, discontented and demanding, whether she sang a song or said “good day.” On her swarthy, matt face the haughtiness of a queen was frozen, and in her dark brown eyes, veiled by a kind of shadow, flashed the knowledge of her irresistible beauty and a contempt for everything that was not herself.

Makar handed me the pipe.

“Smoke! Does the girl sing well? There you are! Would you like such a one to fall in love with you? No? Good! That is right: do not trust girls, and keep away from them. For a girl, kissing is better and sweeter than smoking a pipe is for me, but once you have kissed her, the freedom in your heart is dead. She binds you to her with something you cannot see, and you cannot tear it, and you give her your whole soul. It is true! Beware of girls! They always lie! I love you, she says, more than anything in the world, but just prick her with a pin and she will tear your heart to pieces. I know! Ha, how much I know! Well, falcon, would you like me to tell you a true story? Only remember it, and once you remember it, you will be a free bird all your days.”

## II · Radda

“There lived in the world a Zobar, a young Gypsy, Loiko Zobar. All Hungary, and Bohemia, and Slavonia, and everything round the sea knew him; he was a dashing fellow! There was not a village in those parts where five or ten of the people had not sworn an oath to God to kill Loiko, but he went on living, and if he took a fancy to a horse, you could set a whole regiment of soldiers to guard it, and Zobar would be riding it all the same! Ha! Was he afraid of anyone? If Satan himself came to him with all his retinue, then, if he did not put a knife in him, he would certainly give him a good cursing, and as for a kick in the snout for each of the devils, he would give them that for certain!

“And all the camps knew him or had heard of him. He loved only horses and nothing else, and even that not for long: he would ride one, and then sell it, and the money, let whoever wanted it take it. He had nothing he held dear; if you needed his heart, he would tear it out of his chest himself and give it to you, if only it did you good. That was the kind of man he was, falcon!

“Our camp was roaming through Bukovina at that time, some ten years ago. One spring night we were sitting there: I, and Danilo the soldier, who had fought alongside Kossuth, and old Nur, and all the others, and Radda, Danilo's daughter.

“You know my Nonka? A queen of a girl! Well, but you cannot compare Radda with her; it would be too great an honour for Nonka! Of this Radda, words can say nothing. Perhaps her beauty could be played on a fiddle, and even then only by someone who knows that fiddle as he knows his own soul.

“She dried up many a young man's heart, oh, many! In Moravia one magnate, an old man with a forelock, saw her and stood rooted to the spot. He sits on his horse and gazes, trembling as in a fever. He was handsome as the devil on a feast day, his coat embroidered with gold, at his side a sabre flashing like lightning, and when his horse stamped a foot the whole sabre showed itself set with precious stones, and the blue velvet on his cap was like a piece of the sky; an imposing lord, that old man! He looked and looked, and then he says to Radda: ‘Hey! Kiss me, and I will give you a purse of money.’ And she turned away to one side, and that was all! ‘Forgive me if I offended you, only look at me a little more kindly,’ the old magnate at once lowered his pride, and he threw a purse at her feet, a big purse, brother! And she, as if by chance, kicked it into the mud with her foot, and that was all.

“‘Ah, what a girl!’ he groaned, and brought the whip down on his horse, and the dust rose in a cloud.

“And the next day he appeared again. ‘Who is her father?’ he thundered through the camp. Danilo came out. ‘Sell me your daughter, take whatever you want!’ And Danilo said to him: ‘Only the gentry sell everything, from their pigs to their conscience, but I fought with Kossuth and I trade in nothing!’ The man roared and reached for his sabre, but one of us thrust a lit piece of tinder into the horse's ear, and it carried the fellow away. And we broke camp and moved on. We go a day, we go two, and look, he has caught up! ‘Hey, you,’ he says, ‘before God and before you my conscience is clear; give me the girl for my wife: I will share everything with you, I am very rich!’ He is burning all over and swaying in the saddle like feather-grass in the wind. We fell to thinking.

“‘Well, daughter, speak!’ said Danilo into his moustache.

“‘If an eagle flew into a raven's nest of her own free will, what would she become?’ Radda asked us. Danilo laughed, and all of us with him.

“‘Well said, daughter! Did you hear, lord? It will not do! Look for doves; they are more yielding.’ And on we went.

“And that lord snatched off his cap, flung it to the ground and galloped away so that the earth shook. That was what Radda was like, falcon!

## III · Loiko Zobar

“Yes! So one night we are sitting there and we hear music floating over the steppe. Good music! It set the blood on fire in your veins, and it called you somewhere. All of us, we felt, from that music longed for something after which there would be no need to live any more, or, if we were to live, then as tsars over all the earth, falcon!

“Out of the darkness a horse was cut out, and on it a man sits and plays as he rides up to us. He stopped by the fire, stopped playing, and, smiling, looks at us.

“‘Ha, Zobar, it is you!’ Danilo cried to him joyfully. So there he was, Loiko Zobar!

“His moustaches lay on his shoulders and mingled with his curls, his eyes burn like clear stars, and his smile is a whole sun, by God! As if he had been forged out of a single piece of iron together with his horse. He stands there all as if in blood, in the light of the fire, and flashes his teeth, laughing! May I be damned if I did not love him as myself before he had said a word to me, or even noticed that I too lived in God's wide world!

“That, falcon, is the kind of men there are! He looks into your eyes and takes your soul captive, and you are not ashamed at all, but even proud. With such a man you yourself become better. There are few such men, friend! Well, so be it if there are few. If there were a great deal of good in the world, it would not be counted as good. That is how it is! Now listen to what comes next.

“Radda says: ‘You play well, Loiko! Who made you a fiddle so ringing and so responsive?’ And he laughs: ‘I made it myself! And I made it not of wood but of the breast of a young girl whom I loved dearly, and the strings I twisted from her heart. The fiddle still lies a little, but I know how to hold a bow!’

“As we know, our brother tries at once to cloud a girl's eyes, so that they will not set his heart afire but will themselves grow dim with sadness for him; and Loiko did the same. But he had picked the wrong one. Radda turned aside and, yawning, said: ‘And they told us Zobar was clever and quick. How people lie!’ And she walked away.

“‘Ha, beauty, your teeth are sharp!’ Loiko flashed his eyes, getting down from his horse. ‘Greetings, brothers! Here I am, come to you!’

“‘We welcome the guest!’ said Danilo in answer. We kissed, we talked, and lay down to sleep… We slept soundly. And in the morning we look: Zobar's head is bound with a rag. What is this? It was the horse that had struck him with its hoof as he slept.

“Ha, ha, ha! We understood who that horse was, and smiled into our moustaches, and Danilo smiled too. Well, was Loiko not worth Radda? Why, he was worth more! A girl, however fine, has a narrow, shallow soul, and though you hang a pood of gold round her neck, she will be no better than she is. Well, never mind!

“So we live and live in that place, and our affairs at that time were going well, and Zobar was with us. What a comrade he was! Wise as an old man, knowing everything, and he understood Russian letters and Magyar too. Sometimes he would begin to talk and you would never sleep, just listening to him! And as for his playing, may thunder strike me if anyone else in the world ever played so! He would draw the bow across the strings once, and your heart shudders; draw it again, and it freezes, listening, and he plays and smiles. You wanted to weep and to laugh at the same moment, listening to him. Now someone is groaning bitterly, begging for help, and it cuts your breast like a knife. And now the steppe is telling the sky tales, sad tales. A girl weeps, seeing her brave lad off! The brave lad calls the maiden out into the steppe. And suddenly, hey! A free, living song crashes like thunder, and the very sun, you would think, will start dancing across the sky to that song! That is how it was, falcon!

## IV · The Song and the Whip

“Every vein in your body understood that song, and you became wholly its slave. And if Loiko had cried then: ‘To the knives, comrades!’ we would all have gone to the knives, against whomever he pointed. He could do anything with a man, and all loved him, loved him dearly, only Radda alone would not look at the lad; and that would be well enough if it were only that, but she also mocks him. She stung Zobar's heart deeply, oh, deeply! Loiko grinds his teeth, tugging at his moustache, his eyes look darker than an abyss, and at times such a flash comes into them that you fear for your soul. Loiko goes far out into the steppe at night, and his fiddle weeps till morning, weeps, burying Zobar's freedom. And we lie and listen and think: what is to be done? And we know that when two stones roll toward each other, one must not step between them; they will cripple you. So the matter went on.

“So we were sitting, all gathered, talking over our affairs. It grew dull. Danilo asks Loiko: ‘Sing us a song, Zobar, cheer our souls!’ He turned his eye to Radda, who lay not far from him with her face turned up, looking at the sky, and struck the strings. And the fiddle began to speak, as though it were really a girl's heart! And Loiko sang:

Hey, hey! A fire burns in my breast, and the steppe is so wide! Swift as the wind is my racing horse, and steady is my hand!

“Radda turned her head and, half rising, smiled into the singer's eyes. He flared up like the dawn.

Hey, hop! Come, comrade mine, shall we ride on ahead? The steppe lies wrapped in a stern gloom, but dawn awaits us there! Hey, hey! We will fly and meet the day, soar up into the height; only mind your mane, and do not brush the beauty of the moon!

“That was singing! Nobody sings like that now! And Radda says, slowly, through her teeth:

“‘You had better not fly so high, Loiko; you may fall, and into a puddle, nose first, and dirty your moustache, mind you.’ Loiko looked at her like a wild beast, but said nothing; the lad endured it and sang on:

Hey, hop! What if the day should come, and find us fast asleep? Hey, hey! Then we two together will burn in the fire of shame!

“‘That is a song!’ said Danilo. ‘I never heard such a song; may Satan make a pipe of me if I lie!’

“Old Nur twirled his moustaches and shrugged his shoulders, and Zobar's dashing song was to all our liking! Only Radda did not like it.

“‘So once a mosquito whined, mimicking the screech of an eagle,’ she said, as if she had flung snow at us.

“‘Perhaps you want the whip, Radda?’ Danilo reached toward her, but Zobar flung his cap on the ground and says, all black as the earth:

“‘Stop, Danilo! A hot horse needs a steel bit! Give me your daughter to be my wife!’

“‘That is a speech!’ Danilo smiled into his moustache. ‘Then take her, if you can!’

“‘Good!’ said Loiko, and says to Radda: ‘Well, girl, listen to me a little, and do not be proud! I have seen many of your sisters, oh, many! And not one has touched my heart as you have. Ah, Radda, you have taken my soul captive! Well, what of it? What is to be will be, and there is no horse on which a man can gallop away from himself!.. I take you as my wife before God, on my honour, before your father and all these people. But mind you, do not cross my will; I am a free man and I shall live as I wish!’ And he came up to her, teeth clenched, eyes flashing. We look: he held out his hand to her; now, we think, Radda has put the bridle on the steppe horse! Suddenly we see him throw up his arms and, crash, fall on the back of his head!..

“What was the wonder? It was as if a bullet had struck the lad in the heart. It was Radda, who had hooked the thong of her whip handle round his legs and jerked him toward her; that was why Loiko fell.

“And once more the girl lies without moving and smiles in silence. We watch to see what will happen, and Loiko sits on the ground and has clutched his head in his hands, as though afraid it will burst. And then he rose quietly and walked off into the steppe, looking at no one. Nur whispered to me: ‘Watch him!’ And I crept after Zobar over the steppe in the darkness of the night. That is how it was, falcon!”

Makar knocked the ashes out of his pipe and began to fill it again. I wrapped myself tighter in my greatcoat and, lying there, looked into his old face, black with sun and wind. Shaking his head sternly and gravely, he whispered something to himself; his grey moustaches stirred, and the wind tousled the hair on his head. He looked like an old oak scorched by lightning, but still mighty, strong and proud of his strength. The sea whispered with the shore as before, and the wind carried its whisper over the steppe as before. Nonka no longer sang, and the clouds that had gathered in the sky made the autumn night darker still.

## V · The Ravine

“Loiko walked, dragging one foot after the other, head hanging and arms dropped like whips, and when he came to the ravine, to the stream, he sat down on a stone and groaned. He groaned so that my heart was bathed in blood with pity, but still I did not go up to him. You cannot help grief with words, is that not so?! There! An hour he sits, two hours he sits, and a third he does not stir; he sits.

“And I lie not far off. The night is bright, the moon has flooded all the steppe with silver, and everything can be seen far away.

“Suddenly I see: Radda is coming hurriedly from the camp.

“I felt glad! ‘Ah, splendid!’ I think, ‘a dashing girl, Radda!’ Here she came up to him; he does not hear. She laid her hand on his shoulder; Loiko shuddered, unclasped his hands and raised his head. And how he leaps up, and for his knife! Ah, he will cut the girl, I see, and I was about to shout toward the camp and run to them, when suddenly I hear:

“‘Drop it! I will smash your head!’ I look: Radda has a pistol in her hand, and she is aiming it at Zobar's forehead. What a devil of a girl! Well, I think, now they are equal in strength, what will come next?

“‘Listen!’ Radda thrust the pistol into her belt and says to Zobar: ‘I did not come to kill you, but to make peace; throw down the knife!’ He threw it down and looks gloomily into her eyes. It was a wonder, brother! Two people stand and look at each other like wild beasts, and both are such fine, dashing people. The bright moon looks at them, and I, and that is all.

“‘Now listen to me, Loiko: I love you!’ says Radda. He only shrugged his shoulders, as if bound hand and foot.

“‘I have seen brave lads, but you are bolder and handsomer than any of them, in soul and in face. Every one of them would shave off his moustache if I blinked an eye at him; they would all fall at my feet if I wished it. But what is the use? They are not very bold to begin with, and I would turn them all into women. Few bold Gypsies are left in the world, few, Loiko. I never loved anyone, Loiko, but I love you. And I love freedom too! Freedom, Loiko, I love more than you. But without you I cannot live, as you cannot live without me. So I want you to be mine in soul and in body, do you hear?’ He smiled.

“‘I hear! It gladdens my heart to hear your speech! Come, say more!’

“‘And this too, Loiko: however you twist, I shall overcome you, you will be mine. So do not waste time; my kisses and caresses are waiting for you... I shall kiss you hard, Loiko! Under my kiss you will forget your dashing life... and your living songs, that so delight the brave Gypsies, will no longer sound over the steppes; you will sing tender songs of love to me, to Radda... So do not waste time. I have said it, which means that tomorrow you will submit to me as to an elder comrade, a hero. You will bow down at my feet before the whole camp and kiss my right hand, and then I shall be your wife.’

“That is what the devil of a girl wanted! Such a thing had never been heard of; only long ago among the Montenegrins it was so, the old men said, but among Gypsies, never! Come, falcon, think up something funnier. You will rack your brains for a year and not think of it!

“Loiko sprang aside and cried out over the whole steppe, like one wounded in the chest. Radda trembled, but did not betray herself.

“‘Well, farewell till tomorrow, and tomorrow you will do what I told you. Do you hear, Loiko?’

“‘I hear! I will do it,’ Zobar groaned, and stretched out his hands to her. She did not even look back at him, and he swayed like a tree broken by the wind and fell to the ground, sobbing and laughing.

“That is how the cursed Radda wore the lad out. I could barely bring him to himself.

“Ehe! What devil needs people to suffer grief? Who likes to hear how a human heart groans, torn with sorrow? Think about that!..

## VI · The Knife

“I returned to the camp and told everything to the elders. They thought, and decided to wait and see what would come of it. And this is what came. When we all gathered in the evening around the fire, Loiko came too. He was troubled and had grown terribly thin in a night, his eyes sunken; he lowered them and, without raising them, said to us:

“‘Here is how it is, comrades: this night I looked into my heart and found no place in it for my old free life. Radda alone lives there, and that is all! There she is, the beauty Radda, smiling like a queen! She loves her freedom more than me, and I love her more than my freedom, and I have decided to bow at Radda's feet, as she commanded, so that all may see how her beauty has conquered the bold Loiko Zobar, who before her played with girls as a gyrfalcon plays with ducks. And then she will be my wife and will caress and kiss me, so that I shall no longer want to sing to you, and I shall not regret my freedom! Is it so, Radda?’ He raised his eyes and looked at her sorrowfully. She nodded silently and sternly and pointed with her hand to her feet. And we watched and understood nothing. We even wanted to go away somewhere, only not to see Loiko Zobar fall at a girl's feet, even if that girl was Radda. We were ashamed of something, and sorry, and sad.

“‘Well!’ Radda cried to Zobar.

“‘Ha, do not hurry, you will have time, you may even get tired of it…’ he laughed. Like steel ringing, he laughed.

“‘So that is the whole matter, comrades! What remains? What remains is to try whether my Radda's heart is as strong as she showed it to me. I shall try. Forgive me, brothers!’

“We had not yet had time to guess what Zobar meant to do, and Radda was already lying on the ground, and in her breast, to the hilt, stood Zobar's curved knife. We were turned to stone.

“And Radda pulled out the knife, flung it aside and, pressing the wound with a lock of her black hair, said, smiling, loud and clear:

“‘Farewell, Loiko! I knew you would do so!..’ And she died…

“Did you understand the girl, falcon?! That is the kind of devil of a girl she was, may I be damned for ever and ever!

“‘Ah! And I will bow at your feet, proud queen!’ Loiko roared over the whole steppe, and, throwing himself on the ground, pressed his lips to the feet of dead Radda and went still. We took off our caps and stood in silence.

“What do you say to such a thing, falcon? There! Nur began: ‘We must bind him!..’ Hands would not have risen to bind Loiko Zobar, no one's would have, and Nur knew it. He waved his hand and stepped aside. And Danilo picked up the knife that Radda had thrown aside and looked at it a long time, moving his grey moustaches; Radda's blood had not yet cooled on that knife, and it was so curved and sharp. And then Danilo went up to Zobar and thrust the knife into his back, just opposite the heart. The old soldier Danilo was a father to Radda too!

“‘That is the way!’ Loiko said clearly, turning to Danilo, and went to catch up with Radda.

“And we watched. Radda lay with her hand, holding the lock of hair, pressed to her breast, and her open eyes were in the blue sky, and at her feet lay the bold Loiko Zobar, sprawled. His curls had fallen across his face, and it could not be seen.

“We stood and thought. Old Danilo's moustache trembled, and his thick brows knit. He looked at the sky and said nothing, and Nur, hoary as a harrier, lay down with his face to the earth and wept so that his old shoulders shook.

“There was something to weep for, falcon!

“… You are walking, so walk your own road, without turning to one side. Walk straight on. Perhaps you will not perish in vain. That is all, falcon!”

Makar fell silent and, putting his pipe into his pouch, wrapped his chekmen across his chest. Rain was beginning to drizzle, the wind grew stronger, and the sea rumbled dully and angrily. One by one the horses came up to the dying fire and, having looked us over with their large, intelligent eyes, stood motionless, ringing us round.

“Hop, hop, ehoy!” Makar called to them gently and, patting with his palm the neck of his favourite black horse, said to me: “Time to sleep!” Then he wrapped himself head and all in his chekmen and, stretching out mightily on the ground, fell silent.

I did not want to sleep. I looked into the darkness of the steppe, and in the air before my eyes floated the regally beautiful and proud figure of Radda. She had pressed her hand with the lock of black hair to the wound in her breast, and through her swarthy, slender fingers blood oozed drop by drop, falling to the ground in fiery-red little stars.

And at her heels floated the bold lad Loiko Zobar; his face was veiled by strands of thick black curls, and from beneath them fell frequent, cold, heavy tears…

The rain grew stronger, and the sea sang a gloomy and solemn hymn to the proud pair of Gypsy beauties, Loiko Zobar and Radda, the daughter of old Danilo the soldier.

And the two of them circled in the darkness of the night, smoothly and silently, and the handsome Loiko could in no way draw level with the proud Radda.
