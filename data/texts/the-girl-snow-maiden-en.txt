## I · The Snowball

Once upon a time there lived an old man and an old woman. They had no children, and no grandchildren either. One holiday they went out through the gate to watch the neighbours' children at play. The children were rolling little balls of snow, and throwing them, and laughing. The old man bent down and picked up one small snowball. He turned it over in his hands, and he said:

“Wife, wife, if only we had a little daughter! One as white as this snow, and as round as this snowball!”

The old woman looked at the snowball. She shook her head, and she said:

“What can we do, husband? If we have none, we have none. There is nowhere to get one.” But the old man carried the snowball into the house all the same. He put it in a little pot, covered it with a bit of cloth, and set it on the window sill. Then the sun came up. It shone on the pot, and warmed it, and slowly, slowly, the snow began to melt. Soon the old man and the old woman heard something. Squeak, squeak! A tiny little sound, under the cloth! They hurried to the window and looked. And in the pot lay a little girl. She was as white as snow, and as round as a snowball.

## II · The Snow-Maiden

And the little girl spoke to them, and this is what she said: “I am the girl Snow-Maiden. I was rolled from the snow of spring, I was warmed by the sun of spring, and the sun has made my cheeks pink.”

Oh, how glad the old people were! They lifted her out of the pot. The old woman ran to cut and to sew, so that the little girl would have a pretty dress. And the old man wrapped the Snow-Maiden in a soft towel, and rocked her, and looked after her, and sang to her:

“Sleep, our little Snow-Maiden, our sweet little cake. You were rolled from the snow of spring, you were warmed by the sun of spring.”

“We will give you drink, we will give you food, we will dress you in a flowery dress, and teach you to be wise.”

And so the Snow-Maiden grew up, and she was a joy to the old people. She was so clever, and so sensible, that girls like her are found only in tales, and never in real life.

## III · Zhuchka and the Fox

Everything went well for the old people. It was cosy in the house, and it was good out in the yard. The animals came safely through the winter, and the hens were let out into the yard. But then, one day, there was trouble. The old man had a little dog called Zhuchka. When the hens were moved from the house into the shed, a fox came to Zhuchka. She pretended to be ill, and she begged and begged, in a thin little voice:

“Dear Zhuchka, little Zhuchka, with your white paws and your silky tail, please let me into the shed to get warm!”

Zhuchka had been running after the old man in the woods all day long, and she did not know that the old woman had put the hens in the shed. She felt sorry for the poor sick fox, and she let her in. And the fox took two hens, and ran home with them. When the old man heard about it, he was very cross with Zhuchka, and he sent her out of the yard.

“Go where you like!” said the old man. “You are no good to me as a watchdog!”

So Zhuchka went away from the old man's yard, crying. And only two people were sorry for her: the old woman, and the little Snow-Maiden.

## IV · The Berries

Summer came, and the berries began to ripen. The Snow-Maiden's friends, the village girls, came to call for her. “Come with us to the forest, to pick berries!” But the old people did not want to hear of it, and they would not let her go. Then the girls promised that they would never let go of her hand. And the Snow-Maiden herself begged them, because she wanted so much to pick berries and to see the forest. So at last the old people let her go. They gave her a little basket, and a piece of pie.

Off ran the girls with the Snow-Maiden, hand in hand. But when they came to the forest and saw all the berries, they forgot everything else. They ran off this way and that, picking berries, and calling to one another across the trees.

They picked a great many berries, but they lost the Snow-Maiden in the forest. She called, and nobody answered. The poor little girl began to cry. She went to look for the path, and only got more lost. So she climbed up into a tree, and she called out: “Hallo-o! Hallo-o!”

## V · Up in the Tree

Then she heard twigs crackling, and bushes bending. A big brown bear came by, and he asked her: “Why are you crying, little maiden? Why are you weeping, pretty one?”

“Hallo-o! I am the girl Snow-Maiden. I was rolled from the snow of spring, and the sun of spring made my cheeks pink. My friends asked me from Grandfather and Grandmother, took me to the forest for berries, and left me here!”

“Come down,” said the bear. “I will take you home.”

“No, bear,” answered the Snow-Maiden. “I will not go with you. I am afraid you will eat me.”

The bear went away. Then a grey wolf came running, and he asked her:

“Why are you crying, little maiden? Why are you weeping, pretty one?”

“Hallo-o! I am the girl Snow-Maiden. I was rolled from the snow of spring, and the sun of spring made my cheeks pink. My friends asked me from Grandfather and Grandmother, took me to the forest for berries, and left me here!”

“Come down,” said the wolf. “I will take you home.”

“No, wolf, I will not go with you. I am afraid you will eat me.”

The wolf went away. Then Mistress Fox came walking, and she asked her:

“Why are you crying, little maiden? Why are you weeping, pretty one?”

“Hallo-o! I am the girl Snow-Maiden. I was rolled from the snow of spring, and the sun of spring made my cheeks pink. My friends asked me from Grandfather and Grandmother, took me to the forest for berries, and left me here!”

“Oh, you beauty! Oh, you clever girl! Oh, you poor little thing! Come down quickly, and I will take you home!”

“No, fox. Your words are sweet, but I do not trust them. You would lead me to the wolf, and give me to the bear. I will not go with you!”

The fox walked round and round the tree. She looked up at the Snow-Maiden, and tried to coax her down. But the little girl would not come.

## VI · Zhuchka Comes

Then, far away in the forest, a dog began to bark. “Woof, woof, woof!” And the Snow-Maiden cried out:

“Hallo-o, Zhuchka dear! Hallo-o, my darling! I am here, the Snow-Maiden, rolled from the snow of spring, and warmed by the sun of spring. My friends asked me from Grandfather and Grandmother, took me to the forest for berries, and left me here. The bear wanted to carry me off, but I did not go with him. The wolf wanted to lead me away, but I would not. The fox wanted to coax me down, but I was not fooled. But with you, Zhuchka, I will go!”

When the fox heard the dog barking, she swished her bushy tail, and away she went!

The Snow-Maiden climbed down from the tree. Zhuchka ran up to her, and kissed her, and licked her whole little face. Then she set off to lead her home.

The bear stood behind a stump. The wolf stood in a clearing. The fox crept about in the bushes.

But Zhuchka barked and barked, and all of them were afraid of her, and not one dared to come near.

## VII · Home Again

So they came home. The old man and the old woman cried for joy. They gave the Snow-Maiden a drink, and food to eat, and they put her to bed, and tucked her in under a little blanket. And they sang her song once more:

“Sleep, our little Snow-Maiden, our sweet little cake. You were rolled from the snow of spring, you were warmed by the sun of spring.”

“We will give you drink, we will give you food, we will dress you in a flowery dress, and teach you to be wise.”

And Zhuchka was forgiven. They gave her warm milk, and took her back into their hearts. They put her in her old place again, to guard the yard.
