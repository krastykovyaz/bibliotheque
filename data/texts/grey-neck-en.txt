## I · The Birds Get Ready

The first cold of autumn turned the grass yellow, and all the birds began to worry. They were getting ready for a long journey, and every one of them looked very serious. It was no small thing to fly thousands of miles! Many a poor bird would grow tired on the way, and some would meet with accidents. There was plenty to think about.

The big birds, the swans and the geese and the ducks, got ready with a grand and important air, for they knew how hard the journey would be. But the smallest birds, the little sandpipers and plovers, made the most noise and bustle. They flocked together and flitted from one shore to the other over the shallows and the marshes, as quick as a handful of peas thrown on the ground. The little birds had so much to do!

The forest stood dark and silent, because the best singers had flown away already, without waiting for the cold.

“Why are those little ones in such a hurry?” grumbled the old Drake, who never liked to be disturbed. “We shall all fly away in good time. I don't see what there is to fuss about.”

“You have always been a lazybones, and that is why you don't like to see others busy,” explained his wife, the old Duck.

“Me, a lazybones? You are simply unfair to me, that is all. Perhaps I worry more than anyone, only I don't show it. What good would it do if I ran along the shore from morning till night, shouting, getting in everyone's way and bothering everybody?”

The Duck was not very pleased with her husband, and now she grew quite cross. She said:

“Just look at the others, you lazybones! Look at our neighbours, the geese and the swans. It is a joy to watch them. They live so happily together. A swan or a goose never leaves the nest, and always flies in front of the little ones. But you don't care about your children at all. You only think of yourself, and of filling your tummy. A lazybones, that is what you are. I can hardly bear to look at you!”

“Don't grumble, old woman! I never say a word about your unpleasant temper. Everyone has some faults. It is not my fault that a goose is a foolish bird and fusses over her chicks. My rule is never to meddle in other people's business. Why should I? Let everyone live in their own way.”

The Drake loved to talk wisely, and somehow it always turned out that he, the Drake, was right, and clever, and better than everybody else. The Duck was long used to it. But now she was upset about something quite different.

“What sort of father are you?” she cried at her husband. “Fathers take care of their children, but you don't care a straw!”

“Are you speaking of Grey Neck? What can I do if she cannot fly? It is not my fault.”

Grey Neck was the name of their little daughter, who could not fly. Her wing had been hurt in the spring, when a Fox crept up to the brood and caught hold of one of the ducklings. The old Duck rushed bravely at the Fox and saved the little one, but one small wing was broken.

“It frightens me even to think how we shall leave Grey Neck here alone,” the Duck said again and again, with tears in her eyes. “Everyone will fly away, and she will be left all by herself, quite alone. We shall fly to the warm south, and my poor little one will be cold here. She is our daughter, and how I love her, my Grey Neck! Do you know, old man, I think I shall stay here with her for the winter.”

“And the other children?”

“They are strong. They will manage without me.”

The Drake always tried to change the subject when Grey Neck was mentioned. Of course he loved her too, but why worry for nothing? Well, she would stay, and she might freeze. It was a pity, but there was nothing to be done. And he had to think of the other children as well. His wife was always upset, but one must look at things sensibly. Deep down the Drake felt sorry for his wife, but he did not truly understand a mother's grief.

## II · Left Behind

As the day of parting came nearer, the old Duck was twice as tender with her little daughter. Poor Grey Neck did not yet know what parting and loneliness were, and she watched the others getting ready with the curiosity of a child. Sometimes, it is true, she felt a little envious that her brothers and sisters were so happy to be off, and that soon they would be somewhere far, far away, where there is no winter.

“You will come back in the spring, won't you?” Grey Neck asked her mother.

“Yes, yes, we shall come back, my dear. And then we shall all live together again.”

To comfort Grey Neck, who was beginning to grow thoughtful, her mother told her about other ducks who had stayed for the winter. She knew two such pairs herself.

“You will get along somehow, my darling,” the old Duck comforted her. “At first you will be lonely, and then you will get used to it. If only we could carry you to the warm spring that never freezes, even in winter, it would be quite all right. It is not far from here... But what is the use of talking? We cannot carry you there.”

“I shall think of you all the time,” said poor Grey Neck. “I shall think: where are they, what are they doing, are they happy? Then it will be just as if I were with you.”

The old Duck had to gather all her strength so as not to show her sorrow. She tried to look cheerful, and cried quietly when no one could see. Oh, how sorry she was for dear, poor Grey Neck! She hardly noticed her other children now, and it seemed to her that she did not love them at all.

How quickly time flew! There was already a whole row of cold mornings, and the frost had turned the birches yellow and the aspens red. The water in the river grew dark, and the river itself seemed wider, because the banks were bare and the bushes were losing their leaves. The cold autumn wind tore off the dry leaves and carried them away. The sky was often covered with heavy clouds, which dropped a fine autumn rain. There was not much that was pleasant, and for days now flocks of travelling birds had been passing overhead. The marsh birds went first, because the marshes were beginning to freeze. The water birds stayed longest. What made Grey Neck saddest of all was the flight of the cranes, because they called so mournfully, as if they were calling her to come with them. For the first time her heart tightened with a secret feeling that something was coming, and she watched the flock of cranes for a long time as it vanished in the sky.

“How lovely it must be for them,” thought Grey Neck.

The swans, the geese and the ducks were getting ready too. Single families joined into big flocks. The old, experienced birds taught the young ones. Every morning the young birds went out with merry cries on long flights, to make their wings strong for the great journey. First the clever leaders trained small groups, and then all together. What a noise there was, what young laughter and joy! Only Grey Neck could not join in these flights, and she watched them from far away. There was nothing to do but accept it. But how she could swim, and how she could dive! The water was everything to her.

“It is time to go... it is time!” said the old leaders. “What are we waiting for?”

And time flew, flew quickly. The great day came. The whole flock gathered into one living heap on the river. It was an early autumn morning, when the water was still covered with thick mist. The flock was three hundred ducks. Only the quacking of the chief leaders could be heard. The old Duck had not slept all night, because it was the last night she would spend with Grey Neck.

“Stay close to that bank over there, where the little spring runs into the river,” she advised. “The water there will not freeze all winter long.”

Grey Neck kept apart from the flock, like a stranger. Yes, everyone was so busy with the great journey that nobody paid any attention to her. The old Duck's heart ached as she looked at poor Grey Neck. Several times she made up her mind to stay, but how can one stay when there are other children, and one must fly with the flock?

“Off we go!” the chief leader called out loudly, and the whole flock rose up into the air at once.

Grey Neck was left alone on the river and watched the flock for a long time as it flew away. At first they all flew in one living heap, and then they stretched out into a long, neat triangle and vanished.

“Am I really all alone?” thought Grey Neck, and the tears came. “How lonely I am. Oh, I wish I could fly too.”

## III · The Frozen River

The river where Grey Neck was left rolled merrily through mountains covered with thick forest. It was a wild place, with no houses anywhere. In the mornings the water along the banks began to freeze, and in the daytime the ice, as thin as glass, melted again.

“Will the whole river freeze?” thought Grey Neck with fear.

She felt lonely, and thought and thought about her brothers and sisters who had flown away. Where were they now? Had they arrived safely? Did they remember her? She had plenty of time to think about everything. So she learned what loneliness was. The river was empty, and life was only left in the forest, where the grouse whistled and the squirrels and hares hopped about. Once, out of boredom, Grey Neck wandered into the forest, and she was terribly frightened when a Hare suddenly tumbled head over heels out from under a bush.

“Oh, how you frightened me, silly!” said the Hare, when he had calmed down a little. “My heart jumped right down into my feet! And what are you doing here? All the ducks flew away long ago.”

“I cannot fly. The Fox hurt my little wing when I was very small.”

“That Fox again! There is no worse animal. She has been after me for a long time too. Be careful of her, especially when the river is covered with ice. She will pounce on you in no time.”

They made friends. The Hare was just as helpless as Grey Neck, and he saved his life by running away all the time.

“If I had wings like a bird, I don't think I would be afraid of anyone in the world! You, even without wings, can swim, and if something happens you just dive into the water,” he said. “But I tremble all the time. I have enemies all around. In summer one can hide somewhere, but in winter everything can be seen.”

Soon the first snow fell, but the river still did not give in to the cold. Whatever froze at night, the water broke up again. It was a hard struggle. The most dangerous nights were the clear, starry ones, when everything fell silent and there were no waves on the river. Then the river seemed to fall asleep, and the cold tried to chain it with ice while it slept. And so it happened. There was a still, still, starry night. The dark forest stood quiet on the bank, like a guard of giants. The mountains seemed higher, as they do at night. The high moon poured its trembling, sparkling light over everything. The mountain river, which was so noisy by day, grew quiet, and the cold crept up to it very, very softly, hugged the proud, wilful beauty tightly, and covered her as with a pane of mirror glass. Grey Neck was in despair, for only the very middle of the river had not frozen, where a wide patch of open water was left. There was only a small space left to swim in. Her sorrow grew greatest when a Fox appeared on the bank, the very same Fox that had hurt her wing.

“Well, well, an old friend! Good day to you!” said the Fox sweetly, stopping on the bank. “It is a long time since we met... Congratulations on the winter.”

“Go away, please. I don't want to talk to you.”

“And that is how you thank me for my kindness! Fine manners, I must say! But people say far too much about me. They do something wrong themselves, and then put it all on me. Well, goodbye for now!”

When the Fox had gone away, the Hare came hopping up and said:

“Be careful, Grey Neck. She will come again.”

And Grey Neck began to be afraid, just as the Hare was afraid. The poor thing could not even enjoy the wonders that were happening all around her. Real winter had come. The earth was covered with a snow-white carpet, without one dark spot. Even the bare birches, alders, willows and rowans were dressed in hoarfrost, as if in silver down. And the fir trees became more stately than ever. They stood covered with snow, as if they had put on costly, warm fur coats. Yes, it was wonderful and beautiful all around, but poor Grey Neck knew only one thing: that this beauty was not for her, and she trembled at the thought that her patch of water would freeze at any moment and she would have nowhere to go. And, sure enough, the Fox came a few days later, sat down on the bank and began again:

“I have missed you, little duck... Come out here, and if you will not, I shall come to you myself. I am not proud...”

And the Fox began to creep carefully over the ice, right up to the open water. Grey Neck's heart stood still. But the Fox could not get close to the water, because the ice there was still very thin. She laid her head on her front paws, licked her lips and said:

“How silly you are, little duck... Climb out onto the ice! Well, goodbye for now! I am in a hurry, I have things to do...”

The Fox began to come every day to see whether the water had frozen. The frosts did their work. Of the big patch of open water only one little window was left, no wider than a man's arms can stretch. The ice was strong now, and the Fox sat right at the edge. Poor Grey Neck dived into the water in fear, and the Fox sat there and laughed at her unkindly:

“Never mind, dive away, I shall have you all the same... You had better come out by yourself.”

From the bank the Hare saw what the Fox was doing, and his whole hare's heart was angry:

“Oh, what a shameless Fox... and how unlucky Grey Neck is! The Fox will catch her!”

## IV · The Old Hunter

In all likelihood the Fox would have caught Grey Neck when the open water froze over completely, but it turned out differently. The Hare saw it all with his own slanting eyes.

It was morning. The Hare had jumped out of his burrow to eat and to play with the other hares. It was a hearty frost, and the hares warmed themselves by tapping paw against paw. It was cold, but it was fun all the same.

“Brothers, look out!” someone cried.

Danger really was close. At the edge of the forest stood a bent old hunter, who had crept up on skis without a sound and was looking for a hare to shoot.

“Ah, a warm fur coat for the old woman,” he thought, choosing the biggest hare.

He even raised his gun, but the hares noticed him and dashed into the forest like mad.

“Ah, you cunning rascals!” the old man said crossly. “Just wait... Silly things, they don't understand that the old woman cannot do without a fur coat. She can't be left to freeze. You won't fool Akintich, however much you run. Akintich is cleverer. The old woman told Akintich: 'Mind, old man, don't come home without a fur coat!' And you just jump away!”

The old man set off to look for the hares by their tracks, but the hares scattered through the forest like peas. The old man grew very tired, scolded the cunning hares and sat down on the river bank to rest.

“Oh dear, old woman, old woman, our fur coat has run away!” he said aloud. “Well, I shall rest a little, and then go and look for another...”

There sits the old man, sad as can be, and then, look! A Fox is creeping along the river, creeping just like a cat.

“Well, well, what luck!” said the old man, delighted. “A collar for the old woman's coat is crawling up to me all by itself... Perhaps she is thirsty, or maybe she wants to catch a little fish...”

The Fox crept right up to the open water where Grey Neck was swimming, and lay down on the ice. The old man's eyes were weak, and because of the Fox he could not see the duck.

“I must take aim carefully, so as not to spoil the collar,” he thought, aiming at the Fox. “Otherwise the old woman will scold if the collar has holes in it. One needs skill in everything. Without the right tools you cannot even catch a flea.”

The old man took a long time aiming, choosing a place in his future collar. At last the shot rang out. Through the smoke the hunter saw something dart across the ice, and he ran to the open water as fast as he could. Twice he fell on the way, and when he reached the water he could only throw up his hands: there was no collar anywhere, and in the water swam only one frightened Grey Neck.

“Well, I never!” gasped the old man, throwing up his hands. “This is the first time I have seen a Fox turn into a duck. What a cunning animal!”

“Grandfather, the Fox ran away.”

“Ran away? So there goes the collar for your coat, old woman! What am I to do now, eh? Well, that was a bad business... And you, silly, why are you swimming about here?”

“I could not fly away with the others, grandfather. One of my little wings is hurt.”

“Ah, silly, silly little thing... Why, you will freeze here, or the Fox will catch you! Yes...”

The old man thought and thought, and shook his head, and decided:

“I tell you what we shall do. I will carry you home to my grandchildren. How glad they will be! And in the spring you will lay some eggs for the old woman and hatch out little ducklings. Isn't that so? There now, silly...”

The old man lifted Grey Neck out of the water and tucked her inside his coat, against his chest. “I shall say nothing to the old woman,” he thought, as he set off for home.

“Let her fur coat, collar and all, go on running about in the forest for a while. The main thing is how glad the grandchildren will be!”

The hares had seen it all, and they laughed happily. Never mind, the old woman would not freeze on her warm stove, even without a fur coat.
